Light, memory and materials: three readings

Research notes · full text available

A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

In a photonic circuit, light can do part of the computation, but the values that configure it still have to come from somewhere. If every adjustment requires repeated access to digital memory and its converters, some of the energy advantage can disappear along the way.

Lam and colleagues explore a nearby solution: connecting small capacitors to silicon microring resonators. The stored charge adjusts each resonator’s optical response and acts as a network weight. Their prototype integrates four resonators with memory cells; measurements from the device are then used to study a larger network through emulation.

The memory is not permanent. Charge leaks away and the weight gradually changes: under the measured conditions, the retention time constant is about 0.83 milliseconds. That brings refresh and power consumption into the picture, but it also raises a useful question: how long does a value really need to last if the operation finishes much sooner?

The more than 26-fold power saving reported by the authors comes from an architectural comparison, not a measurement of a complete processor. The paper’s appeal lies in connecting a real component’s behaviour to the choices involved in building a system.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

A phase-change material can retain its configuration after the writing stimulus is removed. For programmable photonics, that is appealing: a circuit could be adjusted without continuously heating it. But there is a catch: if the material absorbs too much light, every added element makes life harder for the next one.

Shen and colleagues investigate antimony selenide, Sb₂Se₃, and propose using the refractive-index difference between its phases while keeping losses low in the telecommunications band. Their design divides a small patch of material into 32 segments that could be programmed with visible light.

The combination of scales makes the paper interesting: it starts with calculations of the material’s structure and bonding, then moves to simulations of a photonic component. The authors estimate 32 states and explore their use in a computing architecture.

A necessary qualification: the device and its performance are simulated. Reproducible writing, cycling endurance and operation in a physical array remain to be demonstrated.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

A conventional camera records a scene and sends the information elsewhere. What if part of its recent history could remain in the sensor itself? That question makes this paper appealing, even if the response is not digital memory in the usual sense.

The device combines GeSe with CsPbBr₃. With no applied bias it responds quickly to light; with a small voltage, its conductivity persists much longer. The team attributes the difference to carrier dynamics and material defects that promote charge trapping.

In one demonstration, the trace left by a moving object carries information about its path. A neural network then analyses that signal to predict the trajectory: the sensor helps retain temporal information, but it does not make the prediction on its own.

The contrast between the two modes is intriguing because fast detection and signal retention usually compete. The next challenge is to take that behaviour from one device to vision systems with many elements and less controlled operating conditions.

Also caught my attention

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

The authors describe a thin-film lithium niobate device whose state is controlled with light. The proposal points towards adding memory to an established photonic platform. For now this is a brief mention: I have not found an openly available full text.
