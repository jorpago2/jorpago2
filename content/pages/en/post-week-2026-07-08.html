Polarization, memory and perovskites

Week of July 8 to 14, 2026

Measuring and preparing polarisation on a chip

[1] C. G. Valdez, A. J. Miller, A. R. Kroo, C. Roques-Carmes, D. A. B. Miller, and O. Solgaard, “Integrated photonic polarization synthesizer and analyzer,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 309, Jul. 10, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02405-3. Publication date record Open access.

Polarization carries information that can be useful in communications, sensors, and quantum experiments. However, preparing and measuring it usually requires additional optical components, so it is interesting to transfer both functions to an integrated circuit that can be reconfigured.

That is what Valdez and colleagues, from Stanford University, in the United States proposes. Their device combines a coupler that separates the polarization components with a programmable network of Mach-Zehnder interferometers. When operating as a generator, the network adjusts the amplitudes and phases of these components. By reversing the path, the configurations necessary to combine the signals allow the incident polarization state to be inferred.

The authors demonstrate the generation of distributed states across the Poincaré sphere and the measurement of Stokes vectors on the chip. Additionally, the optical signal does not need to be absorbed for analysis, so it can continue to other processing steps. The proposal has been manufactured using CMOS-compatible processes in a commercial foundry, although its behavior will still need to be evaluated within complete systems and against environmental and manufacturing variations.

A learning rule on a single memristor

[2] J. Huang et al., “Realization of the Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro rule in a single memristor,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8473, Jul. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75488-6. Open access.

Biological synapses do not respond to an isolated signal without taking into account what happened before. Some plasticity rules describe how the threshold between strengthening and weakening a connection changes depending on its previous activity, a way to prevent the system from always remaining at one of its extremes.

Huang and colleagues study whether a CuInP₂S₆ memristor can reproduce aspects of one of these rules, known as Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro. In their device, ion movement influences conductance and preserves part of the stimulation history. Furthermore, the electrical capacitance of the junction generates reverse pulses during the transient response, which help produce frequency-dependent weakening.

Beihang University, in China, participates in the work. The result is attractive because it brings together several ingredients of the learning rule in a two-terminal device, although the team itself points out that its time constant limits the demonstration to frequencies on the order of 0 to 25 Hz. The authors propose an equivalent circuit to explore faster regimes, which should be evaluated along with the complexity it would add to a real array.

Perovskites that grow with fewer defects

[3] Y. Han et al., “Living growth of ultra-bright 2D perovskites with long-lived carriers,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8629, Jul. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75434-6. Publication date record Open access.

Two-dimensional perovskites have very attractive optical properties, but their defects can trap carriers and reduce the time during which they remain available to emit light or generate an electrical signal. How the crystal is prepared is therefore as important as its composition.

Han and colleagues test slow and approximately isothermal growth that favors the incorporation of precursors into existing nuclei, limiting the appearance of new crystals. The team, with the participation of Zhejiang University and Westlake University, in China, obtains lamellar perovskite crystals of different compositions and studies their optical properties.

In some samples they observe long-lasting components in photoluminescence and, in addition, laser emission under optical pumping. This last test was carried out at 80 K and in vacuum, an important condition when evaluating its possible applications. The improvement in crystalline quality opens interesting questions for emitters and detectors, although environmental stability and device operation at room temperature require additional checks.
