Week of July 8, 2026

Polarization, memory and perovskites

Week of July 8 to 14, 2026

After you measure the polarisation of a beam, could you send the same light on to the next experiment? This week's first chip lets you do just that. We'll also ask what a single memristor remembers of earlier pulses and what a perovskite's glow reveals once the excitation pulse has ended.

Photonics

Measuring and preparing polarisation on a chip

[1] C. G. Valdez, A. J. Miller, A. R. Kroo, C. Roques-Carmes, D. A. B. Miller, and O. Solgaard, “Integrated photonic polarization synthesizer and analyzer,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 309, Jul. 10, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02405-3.

If two beams carry the same power, how would we distinguish them without looking at where they come from? We could look at how the electric field oscillates. To do this in an optics bench we usually add components to the beam path, so it is curious to find a circuit that finds out that orientation and still lets the light pass through to the next experiment.

That is what Valdez and colleagues, from Stanford University, in the United States proposes. Their device combines a coupler that separates the polarization components with a programmable network of Mach-Zehnder interferometers. When operating as a generator, the network adjusts the amplitudes and phases of these components. By reversing the path, the configurations necessary to combine the signals allow the incident polarization state to be inferred.

We can imagine the fit running across the Poincaré sphere, where each point represents a state of polarisation. Once the light is gathered at a port, the position of the phase controls tells how it entered. The interesting thing for whoever designs the rest of the chip is that, after that reading, it has a recombined beam that can be used at another point in the circuit.

Related reading. Wide-band polarization controller for Si photonic integrated circuits. Optics Letters (2016).

Electronics

A learning rule on a single memristor

[2] J. Huang et al., “Realization of the Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro rule in a single memristor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8473, Jul. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75488-6.

The same succession of pulses can strengthen a connection or weaken it depending on the activity it had before. In a learning model you can write that story as a variable. In a tiny device, however, you have to find something physical that is still there when the pulse is removed.

Huang and colleagues study whether a CuInP₂S₆ memristor can reproduce aspects of one of these rules, known as Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro. In their device, ion movement influences conductance and preserves part of the stimulation history. Furthermore, the electrical capacitance of the junction generates reverse pulses during the transient response, which help produce frequency-dependent weakening.

In the device studied by the team, with the participation of Beihang University, in China, a junction with two terminals is enough to see part of that behaviour. If we ask ourselves at what rate he learns, there is a very specific limit. The demonstration covers approximately 0 to 25 Hz. The authors explore higher rates with an equivalent circuit, although transferring it to a matrix would also require counting the components necessary to achieve them.

Related reading. Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro Learning Rule Realized in Polysaccharide-Gated Synaptic Transistors with Tunable Threshold. ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces (2020).

Materials

Perovskites that grow with fewer defects

[3] Y. Han et al., “Living growth of ultra-bright 2D perovskites with long-lived carriers,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8629, Jul. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75434-6.

A perovskite can glow very brightly during the excitation pulse and then go out quickly. If we want to know what happens to the charges that the pulse leaves on the crystal, it is advisable to continue looking when the source has already been turned off. A defect that traps a charge can deprive us of the photon we expected to see later.

Han and colleagues test slow and approximately isothermal growth that favors the incorporation of precursors into existing nuclei, limiting the appearance of new crystals. The team, with the participation of Zhejiang University and Westlake University, in China, obtains lamellar perovskite crystals of different compositions and studies their optical properties.

In some samples, the photoluminescence retains a long-lasting component. The team also finds laser emission under optical pumping, although for this test it cools the sample to 80 K and works in a vacuum. They are two observations that complement each other well. One follows the fate of the charges after the pulse and the other asks if the crystal can sustain laser emission under known conditions.

Related reading. Long-range exciton transport and slow annihilation in two-dimensional hybrid perovskites. Nature Communications (2020).
