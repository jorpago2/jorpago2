Week of July 29, 2026

Radio beams, microwave computing and plasmons in a crystal

Follow a radio signal along a kilometre of fibre and you can watch eight antennas send it out over the air. Or stop at the circuit and ask how much of the waveform you really need to read. On a much smaller scale, we'll see an infrared wave cross a crystalline flake with remarkably little spreading.

Photonics

Photonics helps direct various radio beams

[1] Z. Ni et al., “Fully-connected microwave photonic multi-beamformer with fast beam-steering for broadband wireless communication,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7743, Aug. 4, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74485-z.

To point an antenna we could rotate it by hand. If we have eight antennas, there is another much faster possibility, distributing the signal so that each one emits a little before or after its neighbours. The waves will add up best in the direction we choose. The difficulty comes when we ask for several beams, each with its own direction, for a wide range of frequencies.

Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University in China and collaborators at Huawei have built a network that uses optical delay lines embedded in silicon to govern an array of antennas. Each optical channel carries radio information and goes through adjustable delays before being converted back into an electrical signal, allowing the direction of the beams to be controlled.

The test runs approximately one kilometre of fibre and another 20 m through the air. The eight-element cluster takes about 60 ns to change direction. I find the comparison at 13.1 GHz especially informative, because it shows what is gained by connecting all the elements to each beam. Compared to the partially connected network tested as a reference, the gain increases by 2.9 dB.

Related reading. Multi-Beam Microwave Photonic Beamforming Based on Self-Coherent Detection of a WDM Signal. Journal of Lightwave Technology (2022).

Electronics

A microwave network that transforms data as it arrives

[2] B. Govind, P. Raigoza, and A. Apsel, “Broadband encoding and high-speed probabilistic bit generation with integrated microwave neurons,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9252, Jul. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75783-2.

Before choosing a converter to digitize a radio signal, it is worth asking what we want to know about it. To recognize a pattern, a track that occupies much less bandwidth than the entire waveform may be sufficient. In Cornell University, in the United States, Govind and colleagues look for that clue in the response of a microwave CMOS circuit itself.

The signal passes through waveguides and resonators with non-linear capacitive elements, so that the output depends on both the phase and the amplitude of what is input. In one of the tests, eight-bit symbols arrive at 2.5 gigabits per second. By repeating the reading, the team obtains a distribution that varies with the symbol received.

Therein lies the most interesting nuance. A single read does not recover those eight bits, but several reads may be enough to distinguish patterns. How many are worth taking will depend on the decision that has to be made next.

Related reading. An integrated microwave neural network for broadband computation and communication. Nature Electronics (2025).

Materials

A crystal that channels plasmons in the infrared

[3] F. Aghashirinov et al., “Intrinsic plasmon canalization in the biaxial van der Waals crystal MoOCl2,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 9, pp. 1238–1244, Aug. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02243-9.

Normally we expect a wave to broaden as it moves away from the small region where we excite it. In a MoOCl₂ sheet there is a mid-infrared band where the field maps show something very different. Aghashirinov and colleagues see plasmon-polaritons advancing in a narrow beam because, near an optical transition, the two directions of the crystal allow very different propagations.

Using near-field optical microscopy, they observed this channeling at room temperature and compared the maps with electromagnetic simulations. They also studied sheets of different thicknesses, between 18 and 60 nanometers, and found that the wavelength associated with the channeled regime could shift by more than one micrometer. The accessible range is close to relevant molecular vibrations, which makes it interesting to later study its application to sensors.

In the study, with the participation of Universität Stuttgart, in Germany, and Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, in Italy, metal parts do appear in the assembly. They are used to launch and observe the waves. What I like about that distinction is that it puts the result in the right place. The property that directs propagation already comes with the crystal, so it can be explored without starting by etching a metasurface.

Related reading. Manipulating Hyperbolic Plasmon Polaritons at Near-Infrared in an Anisotropic van der Waals Crystal. Nano Letters (2025).
