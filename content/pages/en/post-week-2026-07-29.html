Week of July 29, 2026

Directional light, microwave neurons and channeled plasmons

Week from July 29 to August 4, 2026.

Photonics

Two edges to conduct polaritons with different polarization

[1] M. Kędziora, A. Opala, M. Zaremba, H. Sigurðsson, and B. Piętka, “Chiral polariton transport enabled by optical spin Hall effect in perovskite waveguides,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9188, Jul. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75965-y.

Among the participants is the University of Warsaw, in Poland.

When light interacts intensely with electronic excitations of a semiconductor, hybrid states called polaritons can appear. These retain part of the optical character and part of the material, making them interesting for controlling signals over small distances. Kędziora and his collaborators use a lead halide perovskite microwire to study how these states propagate and how their direction is related to the polarization of the emitted light.

By exciting the crystal above a threshold, the team observes edge emission with polarization distributions characteristic of an optical spin Hall effect. The authors report chirality greater than 80% and polarized propagation over tens of micrometers, while the coherence between the edges helps to fix the relationship between the observed states.

The microwire functions as a natural cavity and avoids incorporating the distributed mirrors often used in other polaritonic platforms. For now, the demonstration is carried out by optical excitation and does not constitute a complete communication circuit, although it offers a way to study the information transport associated with polarization in a relatively simple integrated geometry.

Electronics

A microwave network that transforms data as it arrives

[2] B. Govind, P. Raigoza, and A. Apsel, “Broadband encoding and high-speed probabilistic bit generation with integrated microwave neurons,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9252, Jul. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75783-2.

The study comes from Cornell University, in the United States.

Before analyzing a radio signal, a conversion to digital data is usually carried out, which imposes speed and consumption requirements on subsequent electronics. Govind and his collaborators investigate a partial alternative using a microwave network made with CMOS technology, in which the nonlinear response of the components transforms the signals before a lower bandwidth digital readout.

The circuit contains guides and resonators with non-linear capacitive elements, whose response depends on the amplitude and phase of the input data. In one of the tests, the team introduces eight-bit symbols at 2.5 gigabits per second and, through repeated measurements of the output signal, obtains a probabilistic representation that depends on the received pattern. The result is not a complete copy of all eight bits into a single bit, but rather a statistical encoding of characteristics of the input.

This distinction matters when evaluating its possible uses, because the information that can be recovered depends on the reading bandwidth, the sampling, and the task to be solved. The work shows a concrete physical circuit and studies how to exploit its dynamics, but leaves open the integration of these operations with complete receiving systems and consumption comparisons at the application scale.

Materials

A crystal that channels plasmons in the infrared

[3] F. Aghashirinov et al., “Intrinsic plasmon canalization in the biaxial van der Waals crystal MoOCl2,” Nat. Nanotechnol., vol. 21, no. 9, pp. 1238–1244, Aug. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02243-9.

The Universität Stuttgart, in Germany, and the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, in Italy, participate.

In certain crystals, light coupled to excitations of the material can propagate with very different characteristics depending on the direction. Aghashirinov and his collaborators study this anisotropy in MoOCl₂ sheets and show that, near a transition of their optical response, plasmon-polaritons can advance in narrow beams in the mid-infrared.

Using near-field optical microscopy, they observed this channeling at room temperature and compared the maps with electromagnetic simulations. They also studied sheets of different thicknesses, between 18 and 60 nanometers, and found that the wavelength associated with the channeled regime could shift by more than one micrometer. The accessible range is close to relevant molecular vibrations, which makes it interesting to later study its application to sensors.

The anisotropy that makes the effect possible belongs to the crystal itself, although metallic elements were used in the measurements to excite and observe the waves. Thus, the result does not imply that any device based on the material can do without an auxiliary structure, but rather that the central property of propagation does not depend on fabricating a metasurface on it.
