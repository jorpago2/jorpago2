Week of July 29, 2026

Radio beams, microwave computing and plasmons in a crystal

How can several radio beams stay on course as the frequency changes? A photonic network adjusts the delay of each signal. Elsewhere, a circuit extracts features from an incoming microwave signal and a crystal flake channels infrared waves without an etched waveguide.

Photonics

Photonics helps direct various radio beams

[1] Z. Ni et al., “Fully-connected microwave photonic multi-beamformer with fast beam-steering for broadband wireless communication,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7743, Aug. 4, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74485-z.

With a suitable delay at each element of an antenna array, the emitted waves reinforce one another in a direction of our choosing. The problem becomes more complicated when you want to transmit several beams at the same time and maintain their direction over a wide frequency range, since small delay mismatches alter the radiation pattern.

Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University in China and collaborators at Huawei have built a network that uses optical delay lines embedded in silicon to govern an array of antennas. Each optical channel carries radio information and goes through adjustable delays before being converted back into an electrical signal, allowing the direction of the beams to be controlled.

With eight antenna elements, the team switches direction in about 60 ns. To test the link it carries the signal over roughly a kilometre of fibre and then transmits it through the air over 20 m. At 13.1 GHz, connecting every antenna element to each beam gives 2.9 dB more gain than the partially connected reference network.

Electronics

A microwave network that transforms data as it arrives

[2] B. Govind, P. Raigoza, and A. Apsel, “Broadband encoding and high-speed probabilistic bit generation with integrated microwave neurons,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9252, Jul. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75783-2.

Digitising a whole radio waveform can produce more data than are needed to make a decision about the signal. Govind and his collaborators investigate a partial alternative using a microwave network made with CMOS technology, in which the nonlinear response of the components transforms the signals before a lower bandwidth digital readout.

The circuit contains guides and resonators with non-linear capacitive elements, whose response depends on the amplitude and phase of the input data. In one of the tests, the team introduces eight-bit symbols at 2.5 gigabits per second and, through repeated measurements of the output signal, obtains a probabilistic representation that depends on the received pattern. The result is not a complete copy of all eight bits into a single bit, but rather a statistical encoding of characteristics of the input.

This distinction matters when evaluating its possible uses, because the information that can be recovered depends on the reading bandwidth, the sampling, and the task to be solved.\n\nThe study comes from Cornell University, in the United States.

Materials

A crystal that channels plasmons in the infrared

[3] F. Aghashirinov et al., “Intrinsic plasmon canalization in the biaxial van der Waals crystal MoOCl2,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 9, pp. 1238–1244, Aug. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02243-9.

When a crystal responds very differently along its two axes, an infrared wave can travel across a flake without spreading as it would in a uniform isotropic medium. Aghashirinov and his collaborators study this anisotropy in MoOCl₂ sheets and show that, near a transition of their optical response, plasmon-polaritons can advance in narrow beams in the mid-infrared.

Using near-field optical microscopy, they observed this channeling at room temperature and compared the maps with electromagnetic simulations. They also studied sheets of different thicknesses, between 18 and 60 nanometers, and found that the wavelength associated with the channeled regime could shift by more than one micrometer. The accessible range is close to relevant molecular vibrations, which makes it interesting to later study its application to sensors.

The anisotropy that makes the effect possible belongs to the crystal itself, although metallic elements were used in the measurements to excite and observe the waves. Thus, the result does not imply that any device based on the material can do without an auxiliary structure, but rather that the central property of propagation does not depend on fabricating a metasurface on it.\n\nThe Universität Stuttgart, in Germany, and the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, in Italy, participate.
