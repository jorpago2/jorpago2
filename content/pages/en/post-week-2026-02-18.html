A gradual image, a delayed neuron and two twisted crystals

A photograph normally preserves the instant you took it. Here the pattern depends on when you look, because liquid is still moving between the pillars. In the other papers a memristor takes almost a second to relax, and the angle between two lithium niobate crystals matters enough to settle it before you bond them.

Photonics

An image that appears little by little

[1] M.-Z. Zhao et al., “Time-programmable coloration via 3D metastructures for optical encryption,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 118, Feb. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02202-y.

If you look at the same surface again a few seconds later, you may see another drawing even though no one has reprinted it. The explanation begins with some polymer pillars whose colour depends on the liquid that surrounds them. As this liquid moves between them, the light returned by each area changes and, with it, what can be read.

Researchers at Jilin University and City University of Hong Kong, in China, have printed three-dimensional polymer structures using laser writing and have taken advantage of how their optical response changes when the refractive index around them varies. By adding water and then glycerol, diffusion gradually modifies the environment of the structures, so that the colors evolve over time.

To that way of reading is added one of erasing. When capillary forces cause the pillars to collapse, the change is no longer undone simply by waiting for the fluid to be removed. If you plan to store information in this way, therefore, you also have to specify when to look and what conditions the surface can withstand before the pattern is lost.

Related reading. Programmable optical encryption using thickness-controlled stretchable chiral liquid crystal elastomers. Light: Science & Applications (2025).

Electronics

An electrode that gives time to the artificial neuron

[2] Q.-X. Li et al., “Feedback neurons based on perovskite memristor with nickel single-atom engineered reduced graphene oxide cathode,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3085, Feb. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69805-2.

Give two equal pulses to a memristor and the second one may return a different current. The interesting question if you want to use it as a neuron is how long you can separate the two pulses before that memory is lost. Here the response is of the order of 780 ms, long enough for two nearby signals to leave a joint trace.

A team with researchers from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University and the Shenzhen University, in China, has studied a perovskite device with a reduced graphene oxide electrode incorporating isolated nickel atoms. The authors relate this modification to changes in the interface barrier and in ion migration, which in turn influence the evolution of the current after applying electrical pulses.

The nickel electrode changes the interface barrier and how ions move, according to the team's interpretation. The conductance can go up or down depending on what stimuli have arrived, and that combination is then used in learning and optimization tests. In other words, 780 ms is how long it takes for the electrical trace to fade. If they were how long it takes for the current to appear, we would be talking about a very different neuron.

Related reading. Leaky integrate-and-fire neurons based on perovskite memristor for spiking neural networks. Nano Energy (2020).

Materials

What happens when two lithium niobate crystals are twisted

[3] A. Rogers et al., “Polar discontinuities, emergent conductivity, and critical twist-angle-dependent behaviour at wafer-bonded ferroelectric interfaces,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1842, Feb. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68553-7.

Before joining two lithium niobate wafers, you can rotate one relative to the other without changing what material is on either side. Once joined, however, that twist is written in the interface. Rogers and colleagues wonder if its effect can be read by passing current through it.

The researchers join two ferroelectric crystals by thermocompression and examine the effects of their polarization directions and the angle between their lattices. Localized conductivity appears at many of the interfaces studied, associated with the discontinuity in polarization. At certain angles, however, they also observe a local rearrangement of dipoles, suggesting that the twist changes how the system compensates accumulated charge.

The Queen’s University Belfast, in the United Kingdom, the TU Dresden, in Germany, and the Complutense University of Madrid, in Spain, participate. The authors relate what they observe to the electronic structure of the rotated interfaces. There is a distinction there worth preserving. Choosing the angle and measuring a different conductance already provides an experimental tool. Identifying exactly which electronic states carry that current requires even more work.

Related reading. Real-Time 3D Imaging of Nanoscale Ferroelectric Domain Wall Dynamics in Lithium Niobate Single Crystals under Electric Stimuli: Implications for Domain-Wall-Based Nanoelectronic Devices. ACS Applied Nano Materials (2019).
