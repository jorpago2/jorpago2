Week of February 18, 2026

Colors that change over time and materials that respond to current

From February 18 to 24, 2026

Photonics

An image that appears little by little

[1] M.-Z. Zhao et al., “Time-programmable coloration via 3D metastructures for optical encryption,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 118, Feb. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02202-y. Open access.

The color of a surface does not always come from a pigment. If its structure modifies the way light is transmitted or dispersed, the tones can depend on the geometry and also the surrounding medium. That sensitivity opens the door to images that change when they come into contact with a liquid, although controlling the sequence of changes requires more than making a surface of a single color.

Researchers at Jilin University and City University of Hong Kong, in China, have printed three-dimensional polymer structures using laser writing and have taken advantage of how their optical response changes when the refractive index around them varies. By adding water and then glycerol, diffusion gradually modifies the environment of the structures, so that the colors evolve over time.

The authors construct a proof of concept in which different messages are successively revealed as the local concentration changes. Time thus ceases to be just the moment in which the image is consulted and becomes part of the information necessary to read it. However, the demonstration is carried out under laboratory conditions and does not by itself establish the security of an encryption system against real attacks. The interest lies above all in showing how an optical structure and the diffusion of a liquid can be combined to produce a programmable temporal response.

Electronics

An electrode that gives time to the artificial neuron

[2] Q.-X. Li et al., “Feedback neurons based on perovskite memristor with nickel single-atom engineered reduced graphene oxide cathode,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3085, Feb. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69805-2. Open access.

In a memristor, the conductance depends on the electrical signals it has received, but the usefulness of that response changes greatly depending on how long it persists. To imitate some neuronal functions, a transient memory can be useful, capable of accumulating stimuli and relaxing afterwards, as long as it also responds in a controlled manner to signals of opposite polarity.

A team with researchers from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University and the Shenzhen University, in China, has studied a perovskite device with a reduced graphene oxide electrode incorporating isolated nickel atoms. The authors relate this modification to changes in the interface barrier and in ion migration, which in turn influence the evolution of the current after applying electrical pulses.

In the devices tested, they observed a relaxation time close to 780 milliseconds and a gradual modulation of the conductance, with responses that can increase or decrease depending on the stimulation. They also explore how to incorporate measured behavior into learning and optimization tasks, although those results should be read in conjunction with implementation details and not as a universal comparison between computers. The most immediate contribution is to show how much the dynamics of a neuromorphic device can change when the interface is designed at the atomic scale.

Materials

VO₂ speed seen from the inside

[3] A. Pofelski et al., “Switching speed limits in electrically driven VO2 structural Mott–Peierls transition,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3139, Feb. 24, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69904-0. Open access.

Vanadium dioxide can change from an insulating state to a conductive state when the temperature changes or an electrical stimulus is applied. This transition is of interest for both electronic and photonic devices, but knowing how long it takes for the resistance to change does not tell the whole story, since the crystal structure also transforms and needs to recover before the next cycle.

Researchers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, the University of California at San Diego and other institutions in the United States, Canada and France have observed this evolution in a VO₂ film integrated into a device. To do so, they combined electrical excitation with pulsed electron microscopy and diffraction, two tools that allow us to examine how the structure responds while the switching is repeated.

In images obtained at 1 MHz, metallic regions appear near the electrodes and the transformation front advances at an estimated speed of 4.54 nanometers per nanosecond. On the other hand, measurements at higher frequencies make it possible to study up to several gigahertz how the recovery of the crystal lattice limits reversible switching. The speed of the transformation front and the maximum frequency are not interchangeable quantities, but together they help relate what occurs inside the material to the electrical conditions of the device. For those working with VO₂, that connection is especially valuable when comparing promises of ultra-fast switching with the repeatability that a real-world application demands.
