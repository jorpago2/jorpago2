Week of October 7, 2026

Light beneath a monolayer, carbon dots and heat in a ferroelectric

Bring light close to a monolayer, give an oxide somewhere to start growing, and find out which way heat leaves a ferroelectric film. All three problems turn on what happens near an interface, something you could easily miss if all you saw was the finished device.

Photonics

Concentrating light beneath a monolayer

[1] O. Matthiessen et al., “Topology-optimized dielectric cavities for enhanced excitonic light emission from WSe2,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 387, Oct. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02436-w.

Participating institutions
Purdue University West Lafayette (United States), Harvard University (United States)

A beam can over-illuminate the area occupied by a monolayer and still allow only a small fraction of its field to interact with it. To bring this field closer to the excitons of the semiconductor, it is necessary to concentrate it right where the layer is. It is also advisable to leave an outlet for the emitted light, because at some point it will have to be taken to the detector.

An international team has used topology optimization to design silicon cavities on sapphire and combined them with WSe₂ layers. The design seeks a region of intense field located just below the semiconductor, so that the excitons can interact with it and part of the emitted light is collected to the outside.

The measured photoluminescence increases about ten times compared to the comparison regions. If we divide the emission by the active area, a greater improvement appears, which answers another question. To choose a cavity, it is important to know both how much light the detector collects and what surface generated it. Added to this comparison is a workshop problem, the roughness and deformations that appear when manufacturing such small holes.

Related reading. Nanometer-scale photon confinement in topology-optimized dielectric cavities. Nature Communications (2022).

Electronics

A layer of carbon dots helps deposit an insulator

[2] S. Wong et al., “Integration of high-κ oxide on 2D semiconductors with carbon-dot monolayer assembly as van der Waals interfacial layer,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10362, Oct. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78053-3.

Participating institutions
University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (United States), Monash University (Australia)

To cover a graphene or MoS₂ surface with an insulator, the difficult part may be precisely the first layer. On a surface with few anchor points, the oxide tends to start in some areas before others. Where gaps remain, it is difficult to prevent leakage or control the current well with the transistor gate.

Sunny Wong and his collaborators propose first covering the surface with a monolayer of small carbon dots. They then deposit hafnium oxide by atomic layer deposition, taking advantage of the fact that the chemical groups on the edges of the dots facilitate the formation of a more continuous film on materials such as MoS₂ and graphene.

We can see what the treatment brings by sweeping the gate voltage in one direction and then retracing our steps. If charges remain trapped, the transistor turns on at different forward and reverse voltages. With carbon dots the separation is below 30 mV. The figure takes on another dimension if we think of a half-volt power supply, where every ten millivolts takes up an appreciable part of the available margin.

Related reading. Van der Waals integration of high-κ perovskite oxides and two-dimensional semiconductors. Nature Electronics (2022).

Materials

A ferroelectric that also has to evacuate heat

[3] S. Makarem et al., “Thermal conductivity and optical phonon scattering of CMOS compatible aluminum nitride based ferroelectric thin films,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10725, Oct. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77264-y.

Suppose that, by changing the composition of a film, we manage to reverse its polarisation with less electric field. Before choosing it for a memory, we would also want to know how it is going to remove the heat, because the atoms that we have introduced can make it difficult for the vibrations that transport it to travel.

Sara Makarem and her collaborators, from the University of Virginia and Pennsylvania State University in the United States, study ferroelectric films based on aluminum nitride doped with boron and, in some cases, scandium. They compare compositions and thicknesses using thermal transport measurements and infrared ellipsometry, with which they analyze the response of optical vibrations of the crystalline lattice.

The team measures the thermal conductivity perpendicular to the film, just in the direction that the heat would follow to reach the neighbouring layer. In the samples studied, this conductivity is related to the life of an optical mode observed by ellipsometry. It is interesting to be able to follow a thermal track with an optical measurement, as long as we remember where the comparison came from. Specific compositions and thicknesses were tested, and the correlation will have to be verified again in another family.

Related reading. Room-Temperature Voltage Tunable Phonon Thermal Conductivity via Reconfigurable Interfaces in Ferroelectric Thin Films. Nano Letters (2015).
