Week of October 7, 2026

Light beneath a monolayer, carbon dots and heat in a ferroelectric

Concentrating light beneath a monolayer and preparing a surface for dielectric deposition are both interface problems. The third paper asks what happens when a ferroelectric has to carry away heat while retaining its electrical response.

Photonics

Concentrating light beneath a monolayer

[1] O. Matthiessen et al., “Topology-optimized dielectric cavities for enhanced excitonic light emission from WSe2,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 387, Oct. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02436-w.

Participating institutions
Purdue University West Lafayette (United States), Harvard University (United States)

A single-layer semiconductor can emit light from excitons, but its very small thickness also limits the volume in which it can interact with an optical field. A well-designed cavity helps concentrate that field where the material is located, although making very small holes without spoiling the intended response requires careful control of the geometry.

An international team has used topology optimization to design silicon cavities on sapphire and combined them with WSe₂ layers. The design seeks a region of intense field located just below the semiconductor, so that the excitons can interact with it and part of the emitted light is collected to the outside.

The measurements show an approximately tenfold increase in photoluminescence compared to the comparison regions. The article also presents a larger enhancement normalized by area, but that figure responds to another way of comparing and should not be confused with the directly measured intensity. Additionally, the authors examine how the emission lifetime changes and point out the challenges of reproducing such small geometries when roughness and warping appear during manufacturing.

Electronics

A layer of carbon dots helps deposit an insulator

[2] S. Wong et al., “Integration of high-κ oxide on 2D semiconductors with carbon-dot monolayer assembly as van der Waals interfacial layer,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10362, Oct. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78053-3.

Participating institutions
University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (United States), Monash University (Australia)

Placing a good gate insulator on a two-dimensional semiconductor can be more difficult than it seems, because its surface barely offers any starting points for uniform oxide growth. If the layer appears in the form of islands or contains gaps, controlling the transistor and reducing leakage becomes more complicated.

Sunny Wong and his collaborators propose first covering the surface with a monolayer of small carbon dots. They then deposit hafnium oxide by atomic layer deposition, taking advantage of the fact that the chemical groups on the edges of the dots facilitate the formation of a more continuous film on materials such as MoS₂ and graphene.

The team compares the surfaces obtained with and without this intermediate layer and studies its integration into transistors.

Materials

A ferroelectric that also has to evacuate heat

[3] S. Makarem et al., “Thermal conductivity and optical phonon scattering of CMOS compatible aluminum nitride based ferroelectric thin films,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10725, Oct. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77264-y.

When new material is introduced into a memory, it is easy to first notice how its polarization changes and the voltage required to write it. However, the heat generated by the device also has to leave, and modifications that facilitate switching can affect that other property.

Sara Makarem and her collaborators, from the University of Virginia and Pennsylvania State University in the United States, study ferroelectric films based on aluminum nitride doped with boron and, in some cases, scandium. They compare compositions and thicknesses using thermal transport measurements and infrared ellipsometry, with which they analyze the response of optical vibrations of the crystalline lattice.

The results show that the disorder introduced by the composition modifies the phonon scattering and can reduce the thermal conductivity in the direction perpendicular to the film. In the samples examined, the authors also found a relationship between this conductivity and the lifetime of an optical mode measured by ellipsometry. This relationship offers a characterization tool for this system, without establishing a universal rule for all ferroelectrics.

A composition that makes the polarization easier to reverse can also scatter the vibrations that carry heat. The team therefore measures thermal conductivity through the film, in the direction heat would have to travel to reach the next layer. Choosing a film for its coercive field also sets part of that thermal resistance.
