Week of October 7, 2026

Small cavities and interfaces that do something new

From October 7 to 13, 2026

Concentrating light beneath a monolayer

[1] O. Matthiessen et al., “Topology-optimized dielectric cavities for enhanced excitonic light emission from WSe2,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 387, Oct. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02436-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
Purdue University West Lafayette (United States) · Harvard University (United States)

A single-layer semiconductor can emit light from excitons, but its very small thickness also limits the volume in which it can interact with an optical field. A well-designed cavity helps concentrate that field where the material is located, although making very small holes without spoiling the intended response requires careful control of the geometry.

An international team has used topology optimization to design silicon cavities on sapphire and combined them with WSe₂ layers. The design seeks a region of intense field located just below the semiconductor, so that the excitons can interact with it and part of the emitted light is collected to the outside.

The measurements show an approximately tenfold increase in photoluminescence compared to the comparison regions. The article also presents a larger enhancement normalized by area, but that figure responds to another way of comparing and should not be confused with the directly measured intensity. Additionally, the authors examine how the emission lifetime changes and point out the challenges of reproducing such small geometries when roughness and warping appear during manufacturing.

A layer of carbon dots helps deposit an insulator

[2] S. Wong et al., “Integration of high-κ oxide on 2D semiconductors with carbon-dot monolayer assembly as van der Waals interfacial layer,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10362, Oct. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78053-3. Open Access.

Participating institutions
University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (United States) · Monash University (Australia)

Placing a good gate insulator on a two-dimensional semiconductor can be more difficult than it seems, because its surface barely offers any starting points for uniform oxide growth. If the layer appears in the form of islands or contains gaps, controlling the transistor and reducing leakage becomes more complicated.

Sunny Wong and his collaborators propose first covering the surface with a monolayer of small carbon dots. They then deposit hafnium oxide by atomic layer deposition, taking advantage of the fact that the chemical groups on the edges of the dots facilitate the formation of a more continuous film on materials such as MoS₂ and graphene.

The team compares the surfaces obtained with and without this intermediate layer and studies its integration into transistors. The proposal is interesting because it treats the interface as an active part of the manufacturing process, although it will still be necessary to assess uniformity and cleanliness in larger scale processes, as well as the behavior of the devices during prolonged periods of operation.

A polarization that appears between natural layers

[3] J. Dai et al., “Interfacial ferroelectricity at naturally-formed van der Waals interfaces,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11403, Oct. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78336-9. Open Access.

Participating institutions
East China Normal University (China) · Huazhong University of Science and Technology (China) · Fudan University (China)

Layered materials can be placed in relative orientations that alter their symmetry and give rise to properties absent in each separate layer. In this context, a displaceable interface can generate electrical polarization, but artificially preparing it with the desired order is not always simple.

Jiawen Dai and his collaborators study naturally formed interfaces in SnSe₂ crystals and find configurations in which the relative arrangement of the layers allows a ferroelectric response perpendicular to the plane. To investigate their origin, they combine piezoelectric response measurements with structural observations and calculations, and examine how stacking configurations relate to polarization change.

The result expands the possibilities for exploring layered semiconductors with memory properties, although observing a ferroelectric response in a sample is not yet equivalent to fabricating a reproducible complete memory. Before getting there, it will be necessary to control the formation of these interfaces and precisely determine the contribution of other phenomena that can also affect electrical measurements.
