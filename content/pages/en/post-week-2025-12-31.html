Week of December 31, 2025

Light learns to travel, remember and change direction

31 December 2025 to 6 January 2026

Light plays three different roles this week. In a barium titanate modulator it carries the signal we want to write. In a sensor array it leaves a trace that can be used to track motion, whereas the test of the last crystal is how differently it transmits two polarizations in the infrared.

Photonics

A modulator with the material in its favor

[1] C. Deng et al., “Self-buffered epitaxy of barium titanate on oxide insulators enables high-performance electro-optic modulators,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 21, Jan. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-025-02081-9.

A voltage changes the refractive index of barium titanate, allowing an electrical signal to be written onto light. The challenge is retaining that electro-optic response in a thin film grown on a chip.

The substrate must help order the atoms without introducing strain that weakens the film’s response. Its index must also allow light to stay in the waveguide.

Chenguang Deng's team at Tsinghua University in China addresses this problem by growing BaTiO₃ on an insulating oxide known as LSAT. During the early stages, an accommodation zone is formed that helps organize the film, whose stress distribution stabilizes regions with different polar structures. The authors measure an electro-optic coefficient greater than 358 pm/V and, in addition, observe that the Curie temperature of the film rises to about 200 °C.

In one arm of a Mach–Zehnder interferometer, the film changes the optical phase, which the team can read out as a change in intensity where the two arms meet. At 1550 nm the half-wave voltage–length product is 0.7 V·cm. When comparing modulation speeds across designs, it matters that the reported bandwidth of 28 GHz was measured at a 6 dB drop.

Electronics

A photosynapse that looks in several bands

[2] G.-H. Dun et al., “Ultra-wide spectrum photosynapse array with 64k-scale for neuromorphic fusion imaging,” Nature Communications, vol. 16, no. 1, Art. no. 10699, Jan. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-66810-9.

When an object crosses a camera’s field of view, a processor usually reconstructs its path from successive frames. A pixel that senses visible and infrared light and retains a trace of exposure could start that task at the sensor itself.

That is the idea explored by Guan-Hua Dun and his collaborators, including researchers from Tsinghua University and Xi'an Jiaotong University, in China. Their device combines carbon nanotubes with molybdenum oxide. While the nanotubes help capture light over a wide range of wavelengths, the trapping states associated with the oxide influence the evolution of the electrical signal after illumination, providing behavior similar to that of an artificial synapse.

A polydopamine treatment helps the active layer coat a 256 × 256 pixel array uniformly. The team checks four wavelengths between 365 nm and 10.6 µm and combines visible and infrared data to track trajectories.

The array retains a stronger signal where the object has just passed. To assign a label to the trajectory, the researchers digitise the signal and send it to a neural network. Their test on a small set of trajectories thus shows which part of recognition takes place at the sensor and which takes place after readout.

Materials

A crystal that separates light also into the infrared

[3] Q. Ren et al., “[Hg3Se2]2- cluster drives giant optical anisotropy and broad infrared transparency,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 40, Jan. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-66148-2.

A birefringent crystal presents different indices to two polarizations of the same light. Separating them in the infrared requires a large index difference in the region where the material is transparent.

Qixian Ren and his collaborators, from the Xinjiang Institute of Physics and Chemistry of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, propose a crystal with the formula Hg₁₈Ga₈Se₈Cl₃₂. Its structure incorporates linear groupings of mercury and selenium that, according to the team's calculations, contribute to the material responding very differently depending on the direction of the optical field. The underlying idea is to relate that organization at an atomic scale with a property that can later be used in a component.

The samples present a transparency window that extends approximately from 0.4 to 25 µm, while birefringence measurements at 1.064, 1.54 and 3.5 µm give values ​​of 0.517, 0.468 and 0.453, respectively. These results are especially interesting because they show a notable response at several infrared wavelengths, although they do not mean that birefringence was measured with the same procedure throughout the entire transparent window. On the other hand, the figure of 0.871 highlighted in the article corresponds to a measurement at 546 nm and 90 K, a condition that is important to remember when comparing it with other materials.

An optical component would require uniformity across larger crystals as well as provision for their mercury content during machining and use.
