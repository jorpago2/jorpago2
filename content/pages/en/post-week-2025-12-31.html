Week of December 31, 2025

Light learns to travel, remember and change direction

31 December 2025 to 6 January 2026

This week we focus on a modulator made with barium titanate, a sensor array that preserves the fingerprint of light for a time and a crystal capable of separating polarizations even in the infrared. All three papers are open access and were published electronically within the week.

Photonics

A modulator with the material in its favor

[1] C. Deng et al., “Self-buffered epitaxy of barium titanate on oxide insulators enables high-performance electro-optic modulators,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 21, Jan. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-025-02081-9.

To introduce information into a photonic circuit, some property of light must be modified, and a common way to do this is to apply an electrical voltage to a material whose refractive index responds to it. Barium titanate has long attracted interest for its strong electro-optic response, although translating that advantage into a thin, device-compatible film is far less straightforward than the number for the ideal material suggests.

A difficulty appears at the time of growth. The film needs a support that helps organize its atoms and, at the same time, allows the light to be confined where it should circulate. These conditions do not always go hand in hand, so imperfections and tensions between the film and the substrate can end up weakening precisely the response that is sought to be exploited.

Chenguang Deng's team at Tsinghua University in China addresses this problem by growing BaTiO₃ on an insulating oxide known as LSAT. During the early stages, an accommodation zone is formed that helps organize the film, whose stress distribution stabilizes regions with different polar structures. The authors measure an electro-optic coefficient greater than 358 pm/V and, in addition, observe that the Curie temperature of the film rises to about 200 °C.

The important test comes when the material stops being just a promising film and becomes part of a Mach-Zehnder interferometer. In this device, the voltage changes the phase of the light that travels through one of the branches and, therefore, modifies the intensity at the output. The authors obtain a voltage-length product of 0.7 V·cm at 1550 nm and measure an electro-optic bandwidth of 28 GHz with a roll-off criterion of 6 dB. The result points to an interesting route to manufacture fast and efficient modulators, although it will be necessary to study how this growth is translated to broader platforms and integration processes.

Electronics

A photosynapse that looks in several bands

[2] G.-H. Dun et al., “Ultra-wide spectrum photosynapse array with 64k-scale for neuromorphic fusion imaging,” Nat Commun, vol. 16, no. 1, Art. no. 10699, Jan. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-66810-9.

A conventional camera records a scene and then delivers the data to another system for processing. If we want to recognize an object with visible and infrared light, we must also combine signals that often come from different sensors. That is why it is attractive that the light-sensitive element itself retains part of the information received, even for a short time, so that the detection and some temporary operations begin on the same device.

That is the idea explored by Guan-Hua Dun and his collaborators, including researchers from Tsinghua University and Xi'an Jiaotong University, in China. Their device combines carbon nanotubes with molybdenum oxide. While the nanotubes help capture light over a wide range of wavelengths, the trapping states associated with the oxide influence the evolution of the electrical signal after illumination, providing behavior similar to that of an artificial synapse.

One of the most striking aspects is the scale. The team fabricates a 256 by 256 pixel matrix and uses a polydopamine treatment to promote uniform deposition of the active layer on a surface containing different materials. The authors test the response at four wavelengths, from 365 nm in the ultraviolet to 10.6 µm in the mid-infrared, and test the combination of visible and infrared information in imaging and trajectory recognition tasks.

It is worth considering what this experiment demonstrates. Although the temporal response of the device provides some of the processing, the classification described also uses a neural network executed after the data is acquired and digitized. Furthermore, the tests are carried out under controlled conditions and with a small collection of trajectories, so there is still some distance left until we know how the system would behave outside the laboratory. Still, integrating multispectral detection and memory response into an array of this size provides an interesting foundation for studying more complex neuromorphic sensors.

Materials

A crystal that separates light also into the infrared

[3] Q. Ren et al., “[Hg3Se2]2- cluster drives giant optical anisotropy and broad infrared transparency,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 40, Jan. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-66148-2.

When light passes through certain crystals, its propagation depends on the orientation of the polarization. This property, known as birefringence, allows us to build components that separate or modify the polarization, but finding materials that do this job well in the infrared is not immediate, since a large difference between refractive indices must coexist with a sufficiently low absorption at the wavelengths of interest.

Qixian Ren and his collaborators, from the Xinjiang Institute of Physics and Chemistry of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, propose a crystal with the formula Hg₁₈Ga₈Se₈Cl₃₂. Its structure incorporates linear groupings of mercury and selenium that, according to the team's calculations, contribute to the material responding very differently depending on the direction of the optical field. The underlying idea is to relate that organization at an atomic scale with a property that can later be used in a component.

The samples present a transparency window that extends approximately from 0.4 to 25 µm, while birefringence measurements at 1.064, 1.54 and 3.5 µm give values ​​of 0.517, 0.468 and 0.453, respectively. These results are especially interesting because they show a notable response at several infrared wavelengths, although they do not mean that birefringence was measured with the same procedure throughout the entire transparent window. On the other hand, the figure of 0.871 highlighted in the article corresponds to a measurement at 546 nm and 90 K, a condition that is important to remember when comparing it with other materials.

The next step will be to check how far the crystals can grow with the necessary uniformity and how they behave when incorporated into practical devices. Furthermore, as it contains mercury, any application will have to consider the handling of the material and its possible restrictions, along with the optical performances that have already been measured.

Also worth a look

A metasurface in three dimensions. Stephanie Malek and her team at Sandia National Laboratories in the United States study how lithography can introduce vertical asymmetry in nonlocal metasurfaces, giving them greater control over their optical resonances. The work appeared on 5 January in ACS Photonics. The publisher provides the abstract and supplementary information, while the article is presented as subscription content.

Smaller micro-LEDs. Yuzhu Pan and colleagues investigate a way to keep current away from edge defects in blue InGaN micro-LEDs by creating a region through fluorine-ion implantation. In the abstract, published on 6 January in ACS Photonics, they describe improvements in devices between 6 and 10 µm. The publisher presents the main text as paid content.

A cell on a memristor. Bowen Zhong and collaborators from institutions of the Chinese Academy of Sciences propose an architecture that combines a cell and a memristor to obtain oscillating signals sensitive to chemical stimuli. The article, published on 6 January in Nature Materials, presents an experimental array of 10 by 10 elements. The publisher does not list the main text as open access.
