Week of September 30, 2026

A spin read by light, a magnet that remembers it and a stable dielectric

A 22-nanosecond clock makes much more sense once you know which part of the experiment was repeated. We'll also meet a light pulse that makes it easier to write a magnet, and a search for ceramics in which high permittivity would be little use if the capacitor lost too much energy on the way.

Photonics

A quantum dot links a spin to telecom photons

[1] J. M. Michl et al., “A spin-photon interface in the telecom C-band with long hole spin dephasing time,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10458, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77282-w.

Participating institutions
Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg (Germany)

To communicate a quantum dot with a fibre, it is advisable that both work at a wavelength well known in telecommunications. This dot emits about 1.55 µm, and the team places it in a circular resonator to access the spin of a hole. The correlations between the photons it collects allow us to prepare that spin and follow what remains of its phase after some time.

With the magnetic field set to 20 mT, the researchers measure a dephasing time of about 22 ns. The wavelength makes the source relevant to fibre links, while the time gives a measure of how long the spin phase remains available.

The 22 ns are best understood by imagining the measurement repeated many times. Even if we prepare the gap in a similar way, its spin may proceed at a slightly different frequency with each repetition. When averaging, the signals stop matching and contrast is lost. That is the clock that is ticking in the test, that of the inhomogeneous spin shift at 20 mT. The exit of a photon from the cavity would be another measurement.

Related reading. Spin-photon entanglement with direct photon emission in the telecom C-band. Nature Communications (2024).

Electronics

Light pulses write several states into magnetic memory

[2] W.-Q. Li et al., “Photon-driven magnetization switching in Fe₃GeTe₂ enables programmable, multilevel magnetic memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10456, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77178-9.

Participating institutions
National Tsing Hua University (Taiwan)

To write with light on a magnet, we can first ask ourselves what makes it easier to change state and then what makes it choose a specific direction. In the Fe₃GeTe₂ ferromagnet, the two questions can be distinguished by varying the colour of the pulse, the energy it delivers per unit area and its polarisation. The three controls act on the same region, but do different jobs.

Using selected pulses, the team writes and erases states with different magnetisations and checks that they persist when the light is removed. The demonstration explores how one region of material can hold more than two memory states.

Magneto-optical measurements show that with photons of 2.3 eV the coercivity decreases, so that it costs less to change the magnetization. If enough energy is delivered per unit area, the direction of rotation of the circular polarisation also comes into play. The possibility of preparing several persistent states becomes interesting precisely there, because we can ask separately what paved the way and what decided the state in which the illuminated region remained.

Related reading. Writing magnetic memory with ultrashort light pulses. Nature Reviews Materials (2019).

Materials

A dielectric that tries not to change with temperature

[3] J. Qi et al., “Temperature-stable high-κ oxides for miniaturized capacitors,” Nature Electronics, Oct. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41928-026-01721-1.

It takes more than just high permittivity to make a good small capacitor. We would also like it to lose little energy and retain its capacity when heated. If we tested all possible ceramic compositions to find that balance, we would spend a lot of time in front of the kiln before making the first device.

Junlei Qi and his collaborators have combined machine learning, crystal family selection and experimental fabrication to search for oxides with that balance of properties. Among the participating institutions is Tsinghua University, in China. After studying different compositions, the team prepares a ceramic with a tungsten bronze-type structure and adjusts its composition by incorporating bismuth and germanium.

The chosen composition offers a relative permittivity of about 185, a loss tangent of less than 0.0005 and a thermal coefficient that reaches an absolute value of 30 parts per million per degree. What closes the search well is that it does not end in a list of candidates. The team manufactures single-layer capacitors, so that you can check what volume is needed to store a charge with the ceramic that has passed the selection.

Related reading. Large polarization gradients and temperature-stable responses in compositionally-graded ferroelectrics. Nature Communications (2017).
