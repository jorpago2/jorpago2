Week of September 30, 2026

A spin read by light, a magnet that remembers it and a stable dielectric

This week light is used to read a spin state and to write states in a magnetic material. The third paper addresses another kind of stability, keeping a dielectric’s response steady as the temperature changes.

Photonics

A quantum dot links a spin to telecom photons

[1] M. J. M. et al., “A spin-photon interface in the telecom C-band with long hole spin dephasing time,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10458, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77282-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg (Germany)

A quantum dot inside a circular resonator emits near 1.55 µm, a wavelength commonly used in fibre. The team uses correlations between photons to herald and examine the spin of a hole in the dot.

With the magnetic field set to 20 mT, the researchers measure a dephasing time of about 22 ns. The wavelength makes the source relevant to fibre links, while the time gives a measure of how long the spin phase remains available.

Electronics

Light pulses write several states into magnetic memory

[2] L. Wei-Qing et al., “Photon-driven magnetization switching in Fe₃GeTe₂ enables programmable, multilevel magnetic memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10456, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77178-9. Open access.

Participating institutions
National Tsing Hua University (Taiwan)

In the layered ferromagnet Fe₃GeTe₂, a light pulse’s intensity and energy affect how readily the magnetisation can change. Once the pulse lowers that barrier enough, the handedness of its circular polarisation determines the response.

Using selected pulses, the team writes and erases states with different magnetisations and checks that they persist when the light is removed. The demonstration explores how one region of material can hold more than two memory states.

Materials

A dielectric that tries not to change with temperature

[3] J. Qi et al., “Temperature-stable high-κ oxides for miniaturized capacitors,” Nat Electron, Oct. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41928-026-01721-1. Open access.

When reducing the size of a capacitor, it is important that its material stores charge efficiently, loses little energy and maintains stable behavior when the temperature changes. It is difficult to optimize all three properties at once, and the number of possible compositions makes testing them all in the laboratory impractical.

Junlei Qi and his collaborators have combined machine learning, crystal family selection and experimental fabrication to search for oxides with that balance of properties. Among the participating institutions is Tsinghua University, in China. After studying different compositions, the team prepares a ceramic with a tungsten bronze-type structure and adjusts its composition by incorporating bismuth and germanium.

The sample studied combines a dielectric constant close to 185 with low losses and a reduced variation of capacitance with temperature. The authors also fabricate capacitor prototypes to examine the application of the material. The algorithm helps guide the search, but the conclusion also rests on how the samples are synthesized and characterized, an important distinction when comparing predicted materials with real components.
