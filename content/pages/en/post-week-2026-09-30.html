Week of September 30, 2026

A spin read by light, a magnet that remembers it and a stable dielectric

A photon at a wavelength familiar from fibre optics can help reveal how long a spin retains its phase, while a more intense pulse can write a magnetic state in another material. The third paper changes tools and scale to search for a ceramic whose capacitance varies little with temperature.

Photonics

A quantum dot links a spin to telecom photons

[1] J. M. Michl et al., “A spin-photon interface in the telecom C-band with long hole spin dephasing time,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10458, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77282-w.

Participating institutions
Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg (Germany)

To read a spin at a wavelength familiar from fibre optics, the team integrates a quantum dot emitting near 1.55 µm into a circular resonator. The team uses correlations between photons to herald and examine the spin of a hole in the dot.

With the magnetic field set to 20 mT, the researchers measure a dephasing time of about 22 ns. The wavelength makes the source relevant to fibre links, while the time gives a measure of how long the spin phase remains available.

The measurement combines many preparations of the spin. Even when they start similarly, slight differences in precession frequency gradually wash out the contrast in the average. The 22 ns therefore describe the hole’s inhomogeneous dephasing time at that field, a distinct quantity from the time a photon spends in the resonator.

Related reading. Spin-photon entanglement with direct photon emission in the telecom C-band. Nature Communications (2024).

Electronics

Light pulses write several states into magnetic memory

[2] W.-Q. Li et al., “Photon-driven magnetization switching in Fe₃GeTe₂ enables programmable, multilevel magnetic memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10456, Oct. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77178-9.

Participating institutions
National Tsing Hua University (Taiwan)

In the ferromagnet Fe₃GeTe₂ a light pulse can lower the barrier to reversing the magnetization, with the effect depending on both photon energy and the energy delivered per unit area. Once the pulse lowers that barrier enough, the handedness of its circular polarisation determines the response.

Using selected pulses, the team writes and erases states with different magnetisations and checks that they persist when the light is removed. The demonstration explores how one region of material can hold more than two memory states.

The magneto-optical measurements distinguish two effects of a pulse. Photons at 2.3 eV lower the coercivity, making the magnetic state easier to change. Once enough energy is delivered per unit area, the helicity of the light also matters. This sequence explains why colour, intensity and polarization are not interchangeable settings when writing a state.

Related reading. Writing magnetic memory with ultrashort light pulses. Nature Reviews Materials (2019).

Materials

A dielectric that tries not to change with temperature

[3] J. Qi et al., “Temperature-stable high-κ oxides for miniaturized capacitors,” Nature Electronics, Oct. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41928-026-01721-1.

To shrink a capacitor, designers need a dielectric with high permittivity, low losses and a capacitance that stays within tolerance as the circuit warms up. It is difficult to optimize all three properties at once, and the number of possible compositions makes testing them all in the laboratory impractical.

Junlei Qi and his collaborators have combined machine learning, crystal family selection and experimental fabrication to search for oxides with that balance of properties. Among the participating institutions is Tsinghua University, in China. After studying different compositions, the team prepares a ceramic with a tungsten bronze-type structure and adjusts its composition by incorporating bismuth and germanium.

The selected ceramic has a relative permittivity of about 185, a loss tangent below 0.0005 and a temperature coefficient within ±30 parts per million per degree. Making single-layer capacitors with it lets the team test the space needed for a given capacitance in an actual component.

Related reading. Large polarization gradients and temperature-stable responses in compositionally-graded ferroelectrics. Nature Communications (2017).
