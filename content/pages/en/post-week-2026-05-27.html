Week of May 27, 2026

Growing quantum dots to order, building a neuron and choosing a crystal’s faces

Thirty-six resonators won't do much good if their quantum dots grow in the wrong places. Across the lab, two illuminated nanowires work together to fire a neuron, while in a blue LED even the crystal face exposed during growth matters. All three experiments start taking shape long before anyone switches them on.

Photonics

Growing quantum dots where the resonators will go

[1] K. Gaur et al., “Scalable quantum photonic platform based on site-controlled quantum dots coupled to circular Bragg grating resonators,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 260, Jun. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02343-0.

Participating institutions
Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg (Germany)

If you want to collect light from a quantum dot, you want it to be right in the centre of the resonator. Now imagine that you have to make thirty-six. If you let the dots grow and then find them one by one, a lot of the work is figuring out where to draw each resonator. Gaur and colleagues change the order. They first deform the crystal in the places where they want the InGaAs dots to grow. This way you know where to put the centre.

Of the 36 devices, 35 fall within the range for which the model predicts extraction greater than 20%. The team measures five and, in the best alignment, obtains an extraction efficiency of 47.1%, with an uncertainty of 3.8 points.

If you keep track until the end of the assembly, it comes to 3.4%. Both percentages describe the same source. The first helps you decide how to make the resonator and the second, how long you will have to wait to collect photons in your own experiment.

Related reading. Bright Electrically Contacted Circular Bragg Grating Resonators with Deterministically Integrated Quantum Dots. ACS Nano (2024).

Electronics

An artificial neuron built with nanowires

[2] J. E. Sestoft et al., “Nanoscale photonic artificial neuron with biological signal processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4798, May 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71446-4.

Two light signals can do opposite things when they reach an artificial neuron. One brings it closer to the firing threshold and the other slows it down. What is attractive about doing it with nanowires is being able to decide the sign of each input by connecting the detector itself, before resorting to additional circuits to add or subtract currents.

Researchers at University of Copenhagen, in Denmark, and Lund University, in Sweden, have built an optoelectronic neuron from semiconductor nanowires. The structure combines two indium phosphide photodiodes connected with opposite polarities and a transistor based on indium arsenide, so that illumination modifies the signal that governs the transistor.

If you illuminate one photodiode first and then both, you can find out how much the second input has to contribute to compensate for the first. This test says more than indicating that the transistor has turned on, because it shows how two competing signals are weighted in the same structure. It is exactly the operation that you would be interested in preserving if you wanted to place many neurons in the area that one now occupies.

Related reading. Artificial nanophotonic neuron with internal memory for biologically inspired and reservoir network computing. Neuromorphic Computing and Engineering (2023).

Materials

Choosing which faces grow in a light-emitting perovskite

[3] H. Chen et al., “High-quality perovskite crystal for efficient and bright pure-blue light-emitting diodes with narrow emission,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4697, May 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71292-4.

Participating institutions
Soochow University (China)

When a perovskite grows, each face of the crystal reveals atoms with a different environment. That matters if you want to make a blue LED. A charge that gets trapped in a defect is no longer available to produce the photon you expected. The team uses a molecule that preferentially binds to certain faces to intervene while the crystal is still taking shape.

The test of that growth strategy comes when the group makes LEDs from the material. They compare two shades of blue. Each calls for a different composition, so trying the same approach in both gives us a way to see which parts of the result carry over when the colour changes.

There are two blues worth looking at side by side. At 473 nm, with a spectral width of 14 nm, the external quantum efficiency reaches 14%. When moving up to 488 nm it reaches 23.8%. If you are interested in setting the blue of a screen, the choice is not resolved by sticking with the highest percentage. First you will have to decide what colour you need and then what efficiency the device offers precisely in that colour.

Related reading. A Multifunctional Additive Strategy Enables Efficient Pure‐Blue Perovskite Light‐Emitting Diodes. Advanced Materials (2023).
