Week of May 27, 2026

Growing quantum dots to order, building a neuron and choosing a crystal’s faces

A quantum dot can emit excellent photons and still miss the resonator meant to collect them. Placement matters again when several nanowires form an artificial neuron and when choosing which faces a crystal exposes in a blue LED.

Photonics

Growing quantum dots where the resonators will go

[1] K. Gaur et al., “Scalable quantum photonic platform based on site-controlled quantum dots coupled to circular Bragg grating resonators,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 260, Jun. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02343-0.

Participating institutions
Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg (Germany)

To choose where each InGaAs quantum dot grows, the team uses structures buried beneath the surface to strain the crystal at specific sites. The alignment is built into the growth step.

In an array of 36 devices, 35 fall within the offset at which the model predicts more than 20% photon extraction. Measurements on five devices show what the resonator gains when the dot sits near the centre.

In the best-aligned device the measured extraction efficiency reaches 47.1%, with an uncertainty of 3.8 percentage points. Including losses along the entire path brings the end-to-end efficiency to 3.4%. For anyone connecting this source to a second experiment, the first figure describes the resonator, while the second says how much remains at the end of the path.

Related reading. Bright Electrically Contacted Circular Bragg Grating Resonators with Deterministically Integrated Quantum Dots. ACS Nano (2024).

Electronics

An artificial neuron built with nanowires

[2] J. E. Sestoft et al., “Nanoscale photonic artificial neuron with biological signal processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4798, May 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71446-4.

An artificial neuron needs to combine inputs that reinforce one another with inputs that oppose them, so its response depends on the balance. If these signals arrive as light, it is tempting for detection to already occur in the elements participating in the calculation, although this requires connecting optical and electrical functions on a very small scale.

Researchers at University of Copenhagen, in Denmark, and Lund University, in Sweden, have built an optoelectronic neuron from semiconductor nanowires. The structure combines two indium phosphide photodiodes connected with opposite polarities and a transistor based on indium arsenide, so that illumination modifies the signal that governs the transistor.

Illuminating one photodiode moves the transistor towards its threshold, while illuminating the other moves it away. Varying the strength of each input then shows which combination is sufficient to produce an output. The nanowires themselves perform the summation.

Related reading. Artificial nanophotonic neuron with internal memory for biologically inspired and reservoir network computing. Neuromorphic Computing and Engineering (2023).

Materials

Choosing which faces grow in a light-emitting perovskite

[3] H. Chen et al., “High-quality perovskite crystal for efficient and bright pure-blue light-emitting diodes with narrow emission,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4697, May 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71292-4.

Participating institutions
Soochow University (China)

Which atoms are exposed as a perovskite grows depends on the crystal face, and so do the defects that can trap charge. The researchers direct the growth of a perovskite to favour some faces and limit those that undermine light emission.

With this material they make a blue diode that emits at 473 nm with a spectral width of 14 nm. They also build a sky-blue device using the same strategy, allowing them to compare how the results change as the colour shifts.

The two shades of blue give different results even with the same growth strategy. External quantum efficiency reaches 14% at 473 nm but 23.8% at 488 nm. Wavelength therefore matters as much as the percentage when comparing emitters that might define the blue primary of a display.

Related reading. A Multifunctional Additive Strategy Enables Efficient Pure‐Blue Perovskite Light‐Emitting Diodes. Advanced Materials (2023).
