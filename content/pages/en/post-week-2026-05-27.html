Week of May 27, 2026

Growing quantum dots to order, building a neuron and choosing a crystal’s faces

Where a material starts to grow matters twice this week, for centring a quantum dot in its resonator and for getting blue light out of a perovskite. Between those two studies, an artificial neuron takes shape from nanowires.

Photonics

Growing quantum dots where the resonators will go

[1] K. Gaur et al., “Scalable quantum photonic platform based on site-controlled quantum dots coupled to circular Bragg grating resonators,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 260, Jun. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02343-0.

Participating institutions
Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg (Germany)

The team concentrates strain at chosen locations so InGaAs quantum dots grow there, then builds circular resonators around those positions. The alignment is built into the growth step.

In an array of 36 devices, 35 fall within the offset at which the model predicts more than 20% photon extraction. Measurements on five devices show what the resonator gains when the dot sits near the centre.

Electronics

An artificial neuron built with nanowires

[2] J. E. Sestoft et al., “Nanoscale photonic artificial neuron with biological signal processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4798, May 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71446-4.

When designing brain-inspired circuits, one question is how to bring together signals that can be reinforced or inhibited and then produce a nonlinear response. If these signals arrive as light, it is tempting for detection to already occur in the elements participating in the calculation, although this requires connecting optical and electrical functions on a very small scale.

Researchers at University of Copenhagen, in Denmark, and Lund University, in Sweden, have built an optoelectronic neuron from semiconductor nanowires. The structure combines two indium phosphide photodiodes connected with opposite polarities and a transistor based on indium arsenide, so that illumination modifies the signal that governs the transistor.

Illuminating one photodiode moves the transistor towards its threshold, while illuminating the other moves it away. Varying the strength of each input then shows which combination is sufficient to produce an output. The nanowires themselves perform the summation.

Materials

Choosing which faces grow in a light-emitting perovskite

[3] H. Chen et al., “High-quality perovskite crystal for efficient and bright pure-blue light-emitting diodes with narrow emission,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4697, May 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71292-4.

Participating institutions
Soochow University (China)

Different crystal faces can leave different kinds of defect at the surface. The researchers direct the growth of a perovskite to favour some faces and limit those that undermine light emission.

With this material they make a blue diode that emits at 473 nm with a spectral width of 14 nm. They also build a sky-blue device using the same strategy, allowing them to compare how the results change as the colour shifts.
