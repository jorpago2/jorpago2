Week of May 27, 2026

Design circuits with algorithms and give new functions to known materials

From May 27 to June 2, 2026

Draw a photonic circuit starting with what it should do

[1] T. Bi et al., “Inverse-designed silicon nitride nanophotonics,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6943, May 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73390-9. Open Access.

A waveguide, splitter or reflector usually starts with a geometry that we know and then we adjust until we get the desired response. Inverse design approaches the problem from the other direction, since it starts with the function that is needed and lets an algorithm look for a structure capable of performing it. For the solution to be useful, however, it must also be able to be manufactured.

A team at Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light in Germany and Harvard University in the United States, along with other institutions, has applied this strategy to low-loss silicon nitride circuits. The authors design and manufacture components to combine or separate signals based on their wavelength or spatial mode, as well as reflectors that can form optical cavities.

Optimization is not limited to finding the ideal response in a simulation. It also imposes minimum sizes and geometric restrictions that take into account lithography and etching, before contrasting the behavior of the manufactured devices. The work helps envision more compact and varied component libraries for a platform already used in integrated photonics, although each design remains dependent on specific tolerances and manufacturing processes.

A VO₂ neuron that adjusts its activity

[2] L. Zhang, T. Zhang, P. J. Tiw, X. Wu, Y. Su, and Y. Yang, “Homeostatic dendritic neuron based on co-integrated volatile and non-volatile memristors for neuromorphic processing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6918, May 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73669-x. Open Access.

The signals that we are interested in processing change over time at different speeds. A neuromorphic architecture that aspires to recognize these patterns needs some way to represent this temporal diversity and, in addition, prevent the activity of its neurons from being permanently saturated or too weak.

Researchers at Peking University in China are studying a system that combines vanadium dioxide devices with resistive memories and CMOS circuits. The resistance transition of VO₂ when exceeding a threshold voltage is used to build an artificial neuron, while other parts of the circuit allow programmable delays to be introduced in the signals reaching it.

The team tests devices and circuits and also uses network simulations to study tasks such as fault detection and speech recognition. Therefore, the classification results and energy figures must be interpreted with the conditions of that architecture and its models, without assuming that the entire system has been integrated on a single chip. The proposal is interesting because it uses the material's own dynamics as part of the calculation and combines it with memory elements that fulfill a different function.

An organic monolayer inside a microlaser

[3] L. Zhang et al., “Continuous-wave organic laser enabled by monolayer molecular crystal,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7061, Jun. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73984-3. Open Access.

Organic materials can emit light efficiently and be processed at relatively low temperatures, but maintaining lasing under continuous excitation poses difficulties, including the accumulation of excited states that promote losses and deterioration associated with heating. A very thin film can offer advantages, provided a cavity capable of taking advantage of its emission is found.

A team from Soochow University, Southeast University and Tsinghua University, in China, has placed a monolayer of organic molecular crystal between layers of hexagonal boron nitride and formed an optical microdisk with them. According to the authors, the organization of the molecules favors rapid emission, while the boron nitride acts as part of the cavity and helps manage heat.

By exciting the device with a continuous laser, they observe an emission threshold, spectral narrowing, and coherence signals compatible with laser operation at room temperature. The tests include an operating half-life that reaches eight hours under the conditions studied. The pump is optical, so the integration and electrical excitation necessary for more autonomous applications remains to be demonstrated, but the combination of monolayer and cavity offers a suggestive starting point for organic optoelectronics.
