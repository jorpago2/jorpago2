Week of March 18, 2026

Circular light, memory in a synapse and amorphous regions written in a crystal

Circularly polarised light can reveal which electronic states a monolayer admits. Focus a pulse inside a crystal and you can leave a permanent mark several planes below the surface. Between those experiments we'll meet a sensor that turns light into electrical pulses and a neighbouring synapse that can hold on to the result.

Photonics

Circularly polarized light and excitons on a metasurface

[1] M. J. Wurdack et al., “Intrinsically chiral exciton polaritons in an atomically-thin semiconductor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2742, Mar. 24, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70875-5.

A monolayer of WS₂ has one plane of tungsten between two planes of sulfur. It barely takes up space and, even so, a beam with circular polarisation in one direction can excite states different from those it reaches when rotating in the other. The question is whether that preference can be preserved when the material returns light.

Wurdack and colleagues place a WS₂ monolayer on a metasurface designed to support a chiral optical resonance. When the coupling between that resonance and the semiconductor’s excitons is strong enough, polaritons appear, hybrid states that share properties of light and matter. Here the authors observe circularly polarized emission associated with two energy levels, a sign that these chiral hybrid states have formed.

The Australian National University, in Australia, and the Friedrich Schiller University Jena, in Germany, participate in the experiment. The comparison I would make when looking at the spectrum is with the same monolayer without resonance coupling. If both the energies at which the light appears and its polarisation change, the formation of states that share properties of the exciton and the optical field can be followed. Noting only that the sample shines brighter would leave that part of the story unresolved.

Related reading. Motional narrowing, ballistic transport, and trapping of room-temperature exciton polaritons in an atomically-thin semiconductor. Nature Communications (2021).

Electronics

A neuron that sees and a synapse that stores

[2] J. Wang et al., “Homogeneous integration of two-dimensional material-based optoelectronic neurons and ferroelectric synapses for neuromorphic vision,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2538, Mar. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68905-3.

To see an object pass by, it is not always necessary to send an entire photograph every time it moves. You can also convert the light into pulses where it arrives and let the nearby electronics preserve what happened. The practical problem is to ensure that the element that triggers and the one that holds the weight of a connection can be manufactured together.

Researchers from Peking University and Peking Jiaotong University, China, have explored a shared platform based on molybdenum disulfide. Its phototransistor converts light pulses into signals that feed an electronic neuron, while ferroelectric transistors made from the same semiconductor act as synapses whose weights can be modified. In this way, the system links detection with calculation operations that resemble some of those used in spiking neural networks.

Once the matrix is constructed, there are two quite different ways of asking it what it knows how to do. You can shine a light on a device and measure what pulses it produces, or use its responses to simulate a larger network and test what it recognizes. The studio does both. Therefore, when reading a percentage of correct answers, it is worth following the signal one step further and seeing where the measured matrix ends and the simulated task begins.

Materials

Writing amorphous regions inside a crystal

[3] Z. Wang et al., “Single-pulse lithography of amorphous photonic architectures inside all-inorganic dielectric crystals,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 177, Mar. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02253-1.

If your crystal is transparent, why limit yourself to drawing the optical structures on its upper face? Wang and colleagues focus an ultrashort pulse into the sample. Where enough energy is deposited, a small region loses crystalline order. By moving the focus you can decide how deep each mark goes before illuminating the finished piece.

The technique is tested in several transparent dielectrics, including lithium niobate, quartz and yttrium vanadate. By controlling the position and orientation of the written regions, the authors build three-dimensional photonic structures that alter light propagation and its nonlinear response. They also study second- and third-harmonic generation, processes in which optical frequency changes through interaction with the material.

The team, which includes Zhejiang University in China and RMIT University in Australia, uses the written regions to generate other colours. It measures an efficiency of about 3% for the second harmonic and 0.1% for the third. It is a difference that would be noticed on the optical table. You can write both structures in a similar way, but to know how much light you will have available on the other side you have to say which of the two harmonics you want to collect.

Related reading. Self-organized phase-transition lithography for all-inorganic photonic textures. Light: Science & Applications (2021).
