Trapped light and structures that store information

Week of 18–24 March 2026

Photonics

Circularly polarized light and excitons on a metasurface

[1] M. J. Wurdack et al., “Intrinsically chiral exciton polaritons in an atomically-thin semiconductor,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2742, Mar. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70875-5. Date in Crossref Open access.

A layer of tungsten disulfide just one atom thick can interact differently with the two circular polarizations of light. This sensitivity is linked to the semiconductor’s electronic states and has attracted interest because of the possibility of controlling information associated with its valleys. The question is how to strengthen that interaction in a photonic structure without losing control over polarization.

Wurdack and colleagues place a WS₂ monolayer on a metasurface designed to support a chiral optical resonance. When the coupling between that resonance and the semiconductor’s excitons is strong enough, polaritons appear, hybrid states that share properties of light and matter. Here the authors observe circularly polarized emission associated with two energy levels, a sign that these chiral hybrid states have formed.

The collaboration includes the Australian National University and Friedrich Schiller University Jena, in Germany. Although the device is a fundamental demonstration rather than a complete quantum circuit, its interest lies in combining a resonance designed through nanofabrication with the properties of a semiconductor monolayer, opening a way to study and selectively control light–matter interaction.

Electronics

An oxygen reservoir to control memory

[2] C. Li et al., “High-speed energy-efficient memristor confined in sub-5 nm space with elemental oxygen reservoir layer,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4117, Mar. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70806-4. Open access.

In many oxide memristors, the electrical state changes when oxygen vacancies are redistributed and conducting paths form or break. That mobility provides the memory, but it can also make it harder for operations to be repeated in the same way every time. Li and colleagues propose controlling the process by confining a 4.5 nm layer of hafnium oxide next to a layer that acts as an oxygen reservoir.

The structure lies between two-dimensional HfS₂ and MoS₂ materials, while the reservoir layer participates in the redistribution of vacancies and helps reduce current in the high-resistance state. The authors report switching times of 8 and 15 nanoseconds for the two transitions, alongside tests of up to 100,000 cycles and retention for 100,000 seconds.

The study includes Wuhan University of Science and Technology and Hunan University, in China. The measurements show how interface design can influence both the speed and stability of a cell, although variability across many devices and manufacturing requirements will need to be examined before extrapolating the result to a large integrated memory.

Materials

Writing amorphous regions inside a crystal

[3] Z. Wang et al., “Single-pulse lithography of amorphous photonic architectures inside all-inorganic dielectric crystals,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 177, Mar. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02253-1. Date record Open access.

Photonic fabrication often arranges structures on a surface, but a transparent crystal also offers space inside it. Wang and colleagues show that an ultrashort laser pulse can locally transform the interior and create small amorphous regions whose optical properties differ from those of the surrounding crystalline material.

The technique is tested in several transparent dielectrics, including lithium niobate, quartz and yttrium vanadate. By controlling the position and orientation of the written regions, the authors build three-dimensional photonic structures that alter light propagation and its nonlinear response. They also study second- and third-harmonic generation, processes in which optical frequency changes through interaction with the material.

The work brings together Zhejiang University in China and RMIT University in Australia. The possibility of writing inside crystals that already have useful photonic properties is particularly interesting, although the performance of each structure depends on fabrication and illumination conditions, and developing complete three-dimensional circuits requires considerably more than creating an individual unit.

Also worth a look

Wang and colleagues observe a modification of graphene’s electronic structure induced by an intense light field, a result that connects ultrafast optics and quantum materials.
