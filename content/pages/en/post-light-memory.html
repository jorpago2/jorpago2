Three readings on light, memory and materials

A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

Participating institutions
University of British Columbia (Canada) · Queen’s University (Canada) · Princeton University (United States)

A photonic processor can calculate with light, but it needs an electrical voltage to set how much each signal contributes. When that weight is stored in digital memory, a converter has to turn the number into a voltage. To reduce reliance on converters, Lam and colleagues try another way of storing the setting. They attach a capacitor to each of the four silicon rings on their chip, so that the stored charge maintains the voltage that controls how much light passes through.

Holding the voltage this way comes at a cost. As the capacitor loses charge, the weight drifts from its programmed value. In the prototype, the decay had a characteristic time of about 0.83 milliseconds. To assess the effect on computation, the team used measurements from the chip to emulate a larger network and see how much drift it could tolerate before the result changed. That tolerance matters for power consumption because it determines how often the capacitors would need recharging. Under the conditions they analysed, the authors estimate that the analogue memory and its control circuitry would draw less than one twenty-sixth of the power of the reference design.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

Participating institutions
Xi’an Jiaotong University (China)

A phase-change material can retain the state in which a laser pulse leaves it, which means a component can stay programmed after the laser is switched off. The difficulty is that some of these materials also absorb part of the light carrying the signal. If a calculation sends that light through several components in succession, the small losses from each add up.

To reduce those losses, Shen and colleagues propose antimony selenide, which lets the two jobs be done at different wavelengths. The material absorbs the visible light used to rearrange atoms in selected regions. Light at telecommunications wavelengths then passes through with little loss and is divided between two outputs according to the pattern written into the material. The simulation resolves 32 levels. How many can be used in a real device, however, depends on whether repeated writes leave each level close enough to its intended value to tell it apart from its neighbours.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

Participating institutions
Nanjing Normal University (China) · Nanjing University (China) · Jiangsu Maritime Institute (China) · Hubei Normal University (China) · Accelink Technologies (China) · RMIT University (Australia)

If the signal from one exposure takes time to fade, part of the old image blends into the next one. In a camera that is usually a nuisance, but Li and colleagues use the same effect to keep information about motion. Applying a small voltage to their sensor, which combines GeSe and CsPbBr₃, traps some of the charge generated by light, so the device keeps responding for a while after the object has passed.

In their test, when the team moved a toy car past the sensor, its response left a trace that a neural network could use to predict where the car would go next. How long that trace lasts matters because a longer trace preserves more of the route but also has more chance of overlapping with another object’s trail. Testing the sensor with two crossing paths would help show how long the signal can persist before the traces become hard to tell apart.

Also caught my attention

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

Participating institutions
Shandong University (China)

Lam’s circuit stores its setting in a capacitor. Gao and colleagues explore storing it in the device itself, which is made from thin-film lithium niobate, a material already used in photonic circuits. According to the abstract and the university’s report, light can write and erase several states that the device retains after the stimulus is removed.
