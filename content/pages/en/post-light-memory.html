Light, memory and materials: three readings

Research notes · full text available

A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

A photonic processor can calculate with light, but it still needs somewhere to store the values that determine how much each signal contributes. If those values are kept in digital memory, they have to be converted into voltages to control the circuit. Lam and colleagues try a different approach on a chip with four silicon rings. Each ring is connected to a capacitor, whose charge sets the voltage that controls the passage of light.

As the capacitor loses charge, the weight of the signal changes. Under the conditions of the experiment, the decay had a characteristic time of about 0.83 milliseconds. The authors used measurements from the chip to emulate a larger network and see how much a weight could drift before affecting the result. Their estimate that the memory and control circuitry could draw more than 26 times less power than the reference design depends in part on how often the capacitors need to be recharged.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

One advantage of phase-change materials is that they hold the state they were set to, even after the laser that programmed them is switched off. The catch on a chip is that some of these materials also absorb part of the light used in the calculation. A small loss in one component can add up as the signal travels through the circuit.

Shen and colleagues propose using antimony selenide, which absorbs visible light but has low loss at telecommunications wavelengths. A visible laser could rearrange the atoms in selected regions of the material, changing how the signal is divided between two outputs. The simulation produces 32 distinct levels. Once the component is built, a revealing measurement will be how closely the levels match when the same pattern is written again. If they vary too much from one cycle to the next, it will be hard to use all 32.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

In a camera, it is usually a nuisance if the signal from one exposure lingers into the next, because part of the old image mixes with the new one. Li and colleagues use that effect to retain information about motion. In their sensor, which combines GeSe and CsPbBr₃, a small voltage causes some of the charge generated by light to become trapped, so the device keeps responding for a while after the object has passed.

In the test with a toy car, that lingering response left a trace of the route, which a neural network used to predict where the car would go next. There is a trade-off worth exploring here. The longer the signal lasts, the more of the route it preserves, but the more likely the traces of two objects are to overlap. A test with crossing paths would help show where that limit lies.

Also caught my attention

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

Thin-film lithium niobate is already used in circuits that control the passage of light. Gao and colleagues describe how they use light to write and erase several states that the material subsequently retains. It brings us back to the question raised by Lam’s work, with the setting stored in the material itself. This note draws on the abstract and the university’s report.
