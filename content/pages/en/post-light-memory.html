A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

In the chip built by Lam and colleagues, each of the four silicon rings has a capacitor beside it. The charge on that capacitor controls how much light the ring lets through and therefore how much that signal contributes to the calculation. What interests me about the setup is that the capacitor starts losing charge as soon as it is disconnected, yet it may hold enough charge to finish the calculation. The question is how far the weight can drift before the result changes.

In the prototype, the charge decayed on a timescale of about 0.83 milliseconds. The team used measurements from the chip to emulate a larger network and see how that decay might affect it. The more than 26-fold reduction in power highlighted in the paper comes from comparing two designs. The four-ring experiment gives a sense of how long the circuit could keep working before the capacitors need recharging.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

In Figure 4 of Shen’s paper, a small patch of antimony selenide is divided into 32 regions. A visible laser can change how the atoms are arranged in some of those regions, so that the pattern written into the material determines how the light is divided between the two outputs.

What I like about the design is how differently the material responds to the light that programs it and the light that carries the signal. It absorbs visible laser light, while losses at telecommunications wavelengths are low, which helps when several components are connected in series. The 32 levels come from simulation. Once a device is built, I would like to see how easily two neighbouring levels can be distinguished after several write and erase cycles.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

The toy car in Figure 5 shows what memory adds to the sensor built by Li and colleagues. When a small voltage is applied to the device, which combines GeSe and CsPbBr₃, some of the charge generated by light gets trapped and the signal takes longer to fade. In that interval, the passing car leaves a trace of its path.

A neural network uses that trace to estimate where the car will go. I like the way the work is divided: the sensor retains something about what has just happened, and the network uses it to predict what happens next. One way to see how far the method can go would be to cross two objects’ paths and see whether their traces can still be distinguished at the same retention time.

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

Gao and colleagues explore another way to store information in a photonic circuit by using light to change the state of a device made from thin-film lithium niobate. I included it because the material is already used in integrated photonics and the prospect of storing a state in the same component caught my attention.
