Light, memory and materials: three readings

Research notes · full text available

A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

Light can carry information inside a chip and take part in certain calculations. But the circuit still needs instructions about how to process it. That means storing control values close to where the calculation happens.

Lam and colleagues test one way to do this: they connect small capacitors, which store electric charge, to silicon microring resonators. These are tiny rings through which light travels. Changing the charge on a capacitor changes how its ring responds to light. That adjustment can represent a neural-network weight: a value that determines how much a signal influences the result.

The charge does not last forever: it leaks away, and the adjustment changes over time. Under the measured conditions, the retention time constant was about 0.83 milliseconds. Keeping the value longer requires refreshing it. The question is whether an operation can finish before that loss affects its result.

The authors fabricated and measured a circuit with four resonators and used those measurements to emulate a larger network. Their estimated power saving—more than 26 times compared with a particular reference architecture—is a comparative calculation, not a measurement of a complete processor.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

Some materials can switch between an ordered, crystalline state and a disordered, amorphous one. Each state affects light differently and can remain after the stimulus that produced it is removed. That opens the possibility of circuits that can be programmed and retain their settings.

There is a problem: some of these materials also absorb light. When many components are placed one after another, the losses add up. Shen and colleagues study an alternative, antimony selenide (Sb₂Se₃), which has low losses at wavelengths used in telecommunications.

Their proposal divides a small area of material into 32 segments whose states could be changed with a visible laser. This changes how the light is distributed between the component’s outputs. The authors calculate that 32 adjustment levels could be achieved.

The paper combines calculations of the material with simulations of the device. In other words, the results show what the design predicts; the component would still need to be fabricated and tested, including whether it can be programmed reliably many times.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

When a camera captures an image, it normally sends the information to another circuit for processing. This paper explores another possibility: letting the sensor retain a signal from the light it has just received for a while.

Li and colleagues combine two materials, GeSe and CsPbBr₃, in one device. With no external voltage, the sensor responds quickly and the signal soon fades. With a small applied voltage, some charge becomes trapped in the material and the signal lasts much longer.

That persistence can help record how an object has moved. In one test, the team used the sensor signal to collect information about a path, then passed it to a neural network to predict the trajectory. The sensor did not predict where the object would go on its own.

The result is a laboratory demonstration. Moving towards a camera with many sensors would require studying how they work together and under real operating conditions.

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

A memristor is a device whose response depends on its previous state. Gao and colleagues present one made from thin-film lithium niobate and controlled with light. I include it as a brief mention because I have not found an openly available full text.
