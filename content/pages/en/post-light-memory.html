A memory that slowly fades

Lam and colleagues · Nature Communications · 7 February 2026

I would start with Figure 2. There are four silicon rings, each with a capacitor next to it. The charge on the capacitor changes how much light the ring lets through. In a neural network, that setting acts as a weight: it determines how much a signal counts towards the result.

The charge leaks away. In the prototype, the characteristic time for that decay was about 0.83 milliseconds. Is that short? It depends on how long the calculation takes and how often the capacitor needs recharging. That is the part I find interesting.

The chip has four rings. The team used measurements from it to emulate a larger network. The reported power saving of more than 26-fold comes from comparing architectures on paper.

Changing phase without losing the light

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

In Figure 4, a patch of antimony selenide is divided into 32 regions. The idea is to use a visible laser to change how the atoms are arranged in some of them. Which regions are written determines how light is shared between the two outputs.

What I like is the use of light for two different jobs. The material absorbs visible light, so the laser can write to it. At telecommunications wavelengths it lets the signal through with little loss. That matters when many components sit in a row.

The 32 levels come from a simulation. Once the device is built, I would like to see whether two neighbouring levels are still easy to tell apart after a few rounds of writing and erasing.

A sensor that remembers for a moment

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

There is a toy car in Figure 5. Its path gets the idea across better than the word “neuromorphic”: when a signal is slow to fade, it leaves a trace of where the car has been.

The team combines GeSe and CsPbBr₃. With no external voltage, the signal drops quickly. Apply a small voltage and some charge gets trapped, so the signal lasts longer. Something that would normally blur an image helps preserve the path here.

A neural network took that trace and made the prediction. The test left me wondering what would happen if two objects crossed paths: could you tell their traces apart?

An optical memristor in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

A memristor is a device whose response depends on its previous state. Gao and colleagues present one made from thin-film lithium niobate and controlled with light. I include it as a brief mention because I have not found an openly available full text.
