Week of July 15, 2026

A waveguide that processes light, flexible transistors and a field-responsive glass

What part is actually doing the work in a waveguide, a flexible inverter or a piece of glass? In the waveguide the edges matter, in the inverter an ultrathin layer decides which carriers conduct, and in the glass the molecules keep some freedom even after they lose the order of the crystal.

Photonics

A MoS₂ waveguide that also processes light

[1] X. Zhang et al., “Integrated van der Waals waveguides for all-optical nonlinear photonic circuits,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6549, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73107-y.

We usually draw the optical waveguide of a chip as a line joining two components, assuming that all the action occurs at their ends. What if doubling the input intensity allowed more than twice as much light to pass through? This dependency is precisely what Zhang and colleagues is looking for so that the waveguide itself intervenes in the calculation.

The team manufactures molybdenum disulfide waveguides without resorting to subsequent transfer and studies their saturable absorption in telecommunications bands. This property makes the response of the material depend on the intensity of the light, so that a signal can undergo a transformation that would be difficult to achieve in a completely linear waveguide. The authors relate this response to states associated with edges and defects, and study response times of the order of ten picoseconds.

From KAUST, in Saudi Arabia, and Zhejiang University, in China, they go one step further and test in image classification models what happens when using the measured response as an activation function. The distinction matters. The waveguide has been manufactured and measured, while connecting many of them and knowing how much energy the entire network would use remains an experimental task.

Related reading. Photonic van der Waals integration from 2D materials to 3D nanomembranes. Nature Reviews Materials (2023).

Electronics

Two types of transistor with the same flexible semiconductor

[2] Q. T. Phùng, K. M. Nguyen, J.-Y. Park, and A. Daus, “Seed-assisted polarity control of flexible WSe2 transistors and demonstration of CMOS inverter,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 88, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00621-w.

One of the simplest drawings of electronics is an inverter with two transistors, although the drawing solves the difficult part. As the input voltage increases, one branch must conduct and the other stop. If we want to manufacture both on a flexible sheet using a single semiconductor, how do we tell each transistor what its role is?

Phùng and colleagues use ultrathin layers deposited before the gate dielectric to control the polarity of WSe₂ transistors. Depending on the material of that initial layer, WOₓ or aluminum, they obtain p-type or n-type devices and combine them to build flexible inverters. In the devices studied they report a voltage gain of up to 65.

At the universities of Stuttgart and Freiburg, in Germany, and at Ajou University, in South Korea, they are also testing material grown by chemical vapor deposition, an attractive way to cover larger surfaces. Precisely one of those inverters loses stability when the power increases. It is a detail that should be kept together with the gain, because it indicates where the transition from a pair of transistors to a large sheet begins to get complicated.

Related reading. Experimental demonstration of high-gain CMOS inverter operation at low V dd down to 0.5 V consisting of WSe2 n/p FETs. Japanese Journal of Applied Physics (2022).

Materials

A molecular glass that responds to light and field

[3] H. Liu, Y. Xiong, T. Xie, C. Gong, and S. Ren, “Relaxor-like ferroelectric response and achiral–chiral switching in an amorphous molecular solid,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8806, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75838-4.

If we melt a crystal and cool it quickly, its molecules may run out of time to recover their initial order. It might seem like a bad recipe for a collective electrical response. However, each molecule retains its own dipole, and Liu and colleagues, from University of Maryland, in the United States, they ask how far this set can be oriented in a glass.

To prepare it, the team melts a molecular derivative and rapidly cools it to form a glass. In this state it measures a switchable polarization and a frequency-dependent dielectric response, similar in character to that of a relaxor ferroelectric. The team also observes that the recovery from the photoactivated state is faster than in the crystalline phase of the same compound, and that the response depends on the helicity of the circularly polarized light.

There is a particularly clean comparison because you can work with the same molecule in two states. After illuminating it with ultraviolet, the glass recovers its previous state sooner than the crystal. Thus, when the recovery time changes, we must also look at how the neighbouring molecules arrange themselves, even if the chemical formula is the same.

Related reading. Reversible Ferroelectric Polarization Modulation of Chiral Molecular Ferroelectrics by Circularly Polarized Light. Advanced Science (2025).
