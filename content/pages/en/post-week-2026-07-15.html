Week of July 15, 2026

A waveguide that processes light, flexible transistors and a field-responsive glass

A MoS₂ sheet can guide light and take part in processing it. In the other studies, a single flexible semiconductor provides the basis for two types of transistor, and a molecular glass responds to both illumination and an applied field.

Photonics

A MoS₂ waveguide that also processes light

[1] X. Zhang et al., “Integrated van der Waals waveguides for all-optical nonlinear photonic circuits,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6549, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73107-y. Open access.

In a photonic circuit, waveguides are usually responsible for transporting light between components that perform different functions. Zhang and colleagues explore a more ambitious possibility, using the waveguide material itself to introduce a nonlinear response that can be used in an optical network.

The team manufactures molybdenum disulfide waveguides without resorting to subsequent transfer and studies their saturable absorption in telecommunications bands. This property makes the response of the material depend on the intensity of the light, so that a signal can undergo a transformation that would be difficult to achieve in a completely linear waveguide. The authors relate this response to states associated with edges and defects, and study response times of the order of ten picoseconds.

The collaboration includes KAUST, in Saudi Arabia, and Zhejiang University, in China. In addition to characterising the waveguides, the authors evaluate using representative networks how the measured response could be used as an activation function to classify images. It remains to be demonstrated how many of these waveguides would be integrated with the rest of the elements of a photonic network and what the energy cost of the complete system would be.

Electronics

Two types of transistor with the same flexible semiconductor

[2] Q. T. Phùng, K. M. Nguyen, J.-Y. Park, and A. Daus, “Seed-assisted polarity control of flexible WSe2 transistors and demonstration of CMOS inverter,” npj Flex Electron, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 88, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00621-w. Open access.

To build complementary logic, devices are needed that conduct through electrons and others that conduct mainly through holes. Achieving both behaviors in the same two-dimensional semiconductor and on a flexible support is not easy, because the treatment of the contacts and interfaces can alter other properties of the transistor.

Phùng and colleagues use ultrathin layers deposited before the gate dielectric to control the polarity of WSe₂ transistors. Depending on the material of that initial layer, WOₓ or aluminum, they obtain p-type or n-type devices and combine them to build flexible inverters. In the devices studied they report a voltage gain of up to 65.

The collaboration brings together the universities of Stuttgart and Freiburg, in Germany, and Ajou University, in South Korea. The group also tested material grown by chemical vapor deposition, although it notes that in that case one of the inverters lost stability at higher supply voltages. This difference reminds us that demonstrating logical function and achieving a large-scale reproducible process are different stages of development.

Materials

A molecular glass that responds to light and field

[3] H. Liu, Y. Xiong, T. Xie, C. Gong, and S. Ren, “Relaxor-like ferroelectric response and achiral–chiral switching in an amorphous molecular solid,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8806, Jul. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75838-4. Open access.

Ferroelectricity is usually associated with materials with an ordered crystalline structure, although molecules can also retain local polar responses when they lose that order over long distances. Liu and colleagues, from University of Maryland, in the United States, are investigating this possibility in an amorphous molecular solid that also changes its chiral response when it receives ultraviolet light.

To prepare it, the team melts a molecular derivative and rapidly cools it to form a glass. In this state it measures a switchable polarization and a frequency-dependent dielectric response, similar in character to that of a relaxor ferroelectric. The team also observes that the recovery from the photoactivated state is faster than in the crystalline phase of the same compound, and that the response depends on the helicity of the circularly polarized light.

The combination of disorder, polarization and optical response offers an interesting platform to study reconfigurable functional materials. For now it is an investigation into the behavior of the material, so its stability, its interfaces and its integration would have to be studied before attributing memory or sensor capabilities to it in a complete device.
