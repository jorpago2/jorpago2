Week of April 15, 2026

Continuous terahertz waves, an ionic photosynapse and domain walls

Two infrared lasers can generate a tunable terahertz signal once there's somewhere for their frequencies to mix. We'll also see how to read a current when light controls the passage of ions, and how electric polarisation arranges itself along the narrow boundary between two crystal domains.

Photonics

Two infrared lasers cover 1 to 11 terahertz

[1] J. H. Krakofsky et al., “Generation of continuous-wave 1–11 THz radiation with intersubband polaritonic metasurfaces,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 699–704, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01889-0.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

If you have two infrared lasers and are missing a terahertz source, it is worth starting by doing a subtraction. The two frequencies separately are very far from the band you want, but their difference can fall just inside. The metasurface of this work provides a nonlinear medium in which to generate this difference and concentrates the fields in a very thin layer.

The team tunes the source from 1 to 11 THz and obtains up to 14 µW of continuous output in the 6–11 THz band. That is a small power in absolute terms. The interest is in reaching it in a band where continuous emission and tunability are hard to combine.

There is an advantage to using continuous lasers that is well appreciated if you think about measuring a sample. You can shift the emitted frequency by adjusting one of the lasers and then stay as long as you need on an absorption line. When preparing that measurement I would also ask how much power the source gives right there. The 14 µW is the maximum reported in one part of the path, and a tunable source does not have to give the same power at all points.

Related reading. Room temperature continuous wave, monolithic tunable THz sources based on highly efficient mid-infrared quantum cascade lasers. Scientific Reports (2016).

Electronics

A photosynapse that moves ions

[2] W. Liu et al., “Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronic device with integrated photoreceptor and photosynaptic functions,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3523, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70337-y.

In a photodiode we usually follow electrons and holes. But if what is between the contacts is a channel with ions, light can also change which ones pass through and where the current goes. Liu and colleagues at Southern University of Science and Technology, in China, and University of Tsukuba, in Japan, take advantage of this possibility to test two visual responses in the same nanofluidic structure.

The structure links carbon nanotubes and molybdenum disulfide, so that illumination generates an electrical response at their interface and modifies ion transport. By varying the applied voltage, the researchers observe that the transient response changes sign and speed, a property that they then use to construct demonstrations of orientation recognition and fingerprint detection under different illumination angles.

The first thing I would look at when turning off the light is if the current immediately returns to the previous value. A detector that follows the flash moment by moment and a synapse that keeps the trail of the flash require very different temporal responses. Having ions that take time to redistribute offers a way to go from one to the other, and explains why measuring only the peak current would count for a small part of the experiment.

Related reading. Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronics. Science (2021).

Materials

Two kinds of electric order in one domain wall

[3] I. N. Ushakov et al., “Hybrid antiferroelectric–ferroelectric domain walls in noncollinear antipolar oxides,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 5, pp. 648–654, Apr. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02139-8.

Participating institutions
Norwegian University of Science and Technology (Norway)

Imagine several arrows spread across a table whose sum equals zero. They can point in pairs in opposite directions or draw a route that ends where it started. Something similar happens with the dipoles of a crystal, and a total polarisation measurement will not tell you which neighbours have agreed. In this potassium boroniobate the distinction is seen especially well when approaching a domain wall.

Electron microscopy reveals walls several micrometres long where the structure changes over just a few atoms. The changes in ferroelectric and antiferroelectric order are intertwined there, giving a reason to examine the boundary as well as the domains it divides.

The wall can extend several micrometres and yet change structure in just a few atoms when crossing it from side to side. That's why it matters which direction you look at the image. Following the local orientations we see how the ferroelectric and antiferroelectric order are found in an area that would completely disappear in the average of a large sample.

Related reading. Noncollinear ferroelectric and screw-type antiferroelectric phases in a metal-free hybrid molecular crystal. Nature Communications (2024).
