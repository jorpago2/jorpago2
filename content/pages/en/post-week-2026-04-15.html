Week of April 15, 2026

Continuous terahertz waves, an ionic photosynapse and domain walls

Combining light from two lasers creates a tunable signal in a difficult band. In the other two papers, the response takes shape inside the material, through ion motion in a photosynapse and dipole order along a boundary.

Photonics

Two infrared lasers cover 1 to 11 terahertz

[1] J. H. Krakofsky et al., “Generation of continuous-wave 1–11 THz radiation with intersubband polaritonic metasurfaces,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 699–704, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01889-0. Open access.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

Mixing two infrared beams in a nonlinear material can generate radiation at their difference frequency. Here the material is a very thin metasurface designed to strengthen the interaction that generates the wave.

The team tunes the source from 1 to 11 THz and obtains up to 14 µW of continuous output in the 6–11 THz band. That is a small power in absolute terms. The interest is in reaching it in a band where continuous emission and tunability are hard to combine.

Electronics

A photosynapse that moves ions

[2] W. Liu et al., “Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronic device with integrated photoreceptor and photosynaptic functions,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3523, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70337-y. Open access.

Participating institutions include the Southern University of Science and Technology, in China, and the University of Tsukuba, in Japan.

Optoelectronic devices are often described in terms of the movement of electrons and holes, while in biological systems ions are also involved in signal transmission. Liu and his collaborators combine both ideas in a light-sensitive nanofluidic device, with which they seek to reproduce some elementary functions of a photoreceptor and a synapse.

The structure links carbon nanotubes and molybdenum disulfide, so that illumination generates an electrical response at their interface and modifies ion transport. By varying the applied voltage, the researchers observe that the transient response changes sign and speed, a property that they then use to construct demonstrations of orientation recognition and fingerprint detection under different illumination angles.

The comparison with biological vision helps to understand what functions are intended to be imitated, although the device does not reproduce a complete retina nor does it demonstrate an autonomous visual system on its own. Its interest for neuromorphic electronics is in the coupling between light, interface and ionic transport, whose temporal evolution provides new ways to modulate the signal before sending it to other processing stages.

Materials

Two kinds of electric order in one domain wall

[3] I. N. Ushakov et al., “Hybrid antiferroelectric–ferroelectric domain walls in noncollinear antipolar oxides,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 5, pp. 648–654, Apr. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02139-8. Open access.

Participating institutions
Norwegian University of Science and Technology (Norway)

In some antipolar materials, neighbouring dipoles point in directions that cancel without lining up on one axis. In a potassium boroniobate, this order coexists with a piezoelectric response and domains that can be modified by applying voltage.

Electron microscopy reveals walls several micrometres long where the structure changes over just a few atoms. The changes in ferroelectric and antiferroelectric order are intertwined there, giving a reason to examine the boundary as well as the domains it divides.
