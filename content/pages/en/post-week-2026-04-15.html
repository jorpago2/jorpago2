Week of April 15, 2026

Continuous terahertz waves, an ionic photosynapse and domain walls

Illuminated by two lasers, a metasurface emits at a frequency neither laser reaches on its own. Light then takes on a different role in regulating an ionic current, while a crystal whose dipoles point in several directions invites a closer look at what happens where two domains meet.

Photonics

Two infrared lasers cover 1 to 11 terahertz

[1] J. H. Krakofsky et al., “Generation of continuous-wave 1–11 THz radiation with intersubband polaritonic metasurfaces,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 699–704, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01889-0.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

When two infrared lasers illuminate a nonlinear medium together, their frequency difference can fall in the terahertz band. Here the material is a very thin metasurface designed to strengthen the interaction that generates the wave.

The team tunes the source from 1 to 11 THz and obtains up to 14 µW of continuous output in the 6–11 THz band. That is a small power in absolute terms. The interest is in reaching it in a band where continuous emission and tunability are hard to combine.

Tuning either laser shifts the emitted frequency without requiring either laser to operate in the terahertz band. Continuous operation also allows the source to dwell on an absorption line. For spectroscopy, then, the accessible frequency range matters alongside the power actually reaching the sample at the chosen frequency.

Related reading. Room temperature continuous wave, monolithic tunable THz sources based on highly efficient mid-infrared quantum cascade lasers. Scientific Reports (2016).

Electronics

A photosynapse that moves ions

[2] W. Liu et al., “Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronic device with integrated photoreceptor and photosynaptic functions,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3523, Apr. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70337-y.

Electrons and holes carry the current in a photodiode, but light can also be used to control ions passing through a tiny channel. Liu and his collaborators combine both ideas in a light-sensitive nanofluidic device, with which they seek to reproduce some elementary functions of a photoreceptor and a synapse.

The structure links carbon nanotubes and molybdenum disulfide, so that illumination generates an electrical response at their interface and modifies ion transport. By varying the applied voltage, the researchers observe that the transient response changes sign and speed, a property that they then use to construct demonstrations of orientation recognition and fingerprint detection under different illumination angles.

The bias determines the sign of the transient response to light. That changes what a pair of light pulses can do, since the effect of the second pulse also depends on how the ions were redistributing after the first.

Participating institutions include the Southern University of Science and Technology, in China, and the University of Tsukuba, in Japan.

Related reading. Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronics. Science (2021).

Materials

Two kinds of electric order in one domain wall

[3] I. N. Ushakov et al., “Hybrid antiferroelectric–ferroelectric domain walls in noncollinear antipolar oxides,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 5, pp. 648–654, Apr. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02139-8.

Participating institutions
Norwegian University of Science and Technology (Norway)

A crystal’s net polarization can vanish for several different arrangements of its dipoles, so the sum alone says little about how neighbouring dipoles are oriented. In a potassium boroniobate, this order coexists with a piezoelectric response and domains that can be modified by applying voltage.

Electron microscopy reveals walls several micrometres long where the structure changes over just a few atoms. The changes in ferroelectric and antiferroelectric order are intertwined there, giving a reason to examine the boundary as well as the domains it divides.

An average polarization measurement can give the same answer for dipoles turning in different directions as for dipoles that simply cancel in pairs. Atomic-scale imaging resolves their relative orientations on either side of the wall. This makes it possible to see how such a narrow boundary can have an electrical structure of its own within a much larger crystal.

Related reading. Noncollinear ferroelectric and screw-type antiferroelectric phases in a metal-free hybrid molecular crystal. Nature Communications (2024).
