Week of May 6, 2026

Shrinking a pattern, separating noise and bringing an exciton into view

One way to make features small enough to control visible light is to draw them large and then shrink the whole structure. The other experiments ask how a memory can help recognise sounds in noise and why a normally dark electronic state starts to emit when its neighbour changes.

Photonics

Draw large and shrink later

[1] Q. Yang et al., “Isotropic shrinkage of patterned vacancies enables three-dimensional nanoprecise metastructures for visible light applications,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 653–663, May 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01896-1.

A three-dimensional structure for visible light can require features well below a micrometre, including in places where they are difficult to write directly. Quansan Yang and his collaborators propose a solution that changes the usual order of the problem, since they first draw the structure at a more comfortable scale and then reduce its size.

The team, with researchers at Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States, works with a swollen hydrogel and uses light to break the material at chosen positions within its volume. This creates gaps whose design can be controlled in three dimensions. The hydrogel is then dehydrated, contracting in all directions and reducing the dimensions of the shapes written inside it by more than ten times in some of the formulations studied.

The authors call the method implosion carving and show that it allows producing structures with details of tens of nanometers to manipulate visible light. Isotropic reduction is important because it helps preserve the shape of the pattern by reducing its size, although in practice deformation must be controlled and cracks avoided during treatment.

As the hydrogel shrinks in all three dimensions, the pattern retains voids at different depths. Each void changes the refractive index along the path taken by light. The team tests that control in an optical computing device operating at visible wavelengths.

Electronics

Memristors to separate a signal from noise

[2] Z. Wang et al., “Biomimetic-voltage nitride-enhanced memristor arrays for femtojoule in-memory biosignal processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6072, May 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72740-x.

When an acoustic signal arrives mixed with noise, recognising it depends on retaining the relevant part of its variation over time. If this requires constantly transferring data between memory and processor, part of the energy is consumed in the movement of information, a difficulty that is especially relevant in small battery-powered devices.

Zijian Wang and his collaborators, including researchers from the Zhejiang University in China, propose a 32 by 32 memristor array that uses two modes of operation. In one, the resistance change is transient and helps to suppress weak components of the noise. In the other, states are more persistent and are used to store weights intended for a recognition task.

Each element combines layers of IGZO and silicon nitride between metal electrodes. The authors observe a switching voltage of 19 mV in one of the device characterizations, while in the test with the array they apply signals of 40 to 60 mV to promote consistent operation. To evaluate the proposal they use ten classes of audio signals and 500 samples, on which they report 100% recognition after noise treatment.

It is a promising test, although this precision corresponds to the set and the conditions chosen, and the energy of 1.44 femtojoules highlighted by the authors corresponds to a noise elimination operation, not to the consumption of the entire system. In fact, the experimental platform incorporates acquisition, conversion and control electronics. The work shows how the same matrix can distribute temporal and memory tasks, taking advantage of the fact that its elements do not have to maintain the state for the same time.

Materials

A ferroelectric layer lights up a dark exciton

[3] X. Wang et al., “Ferroelectric brightening of spin‑forbidden dark excitons in a WSe2/hybrid-perovskite heterostructure,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6285, May 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72143-y.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

In a WSe₂ monolayer an electron and a hole can remain bound yet have little chance of emitting a photon because of their spins. The authors place a ferroelectric perovskite alongside it, changing the symmetry that determines which transitions can emit.

The previously dark exciton appears in the spectrum without an external magnetic field. Twisting one layer relative to the other changes the coupling strength, making the stacking angle another way to control the response.
