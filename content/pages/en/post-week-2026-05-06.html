Week of May 6, 2026

Shrinking a pattern, separating noise and bringing an exciton into view

How do you draw a hole smaller than your tool can manage? One team starts large and shrinks the whole piece afterwards. The other papers also recover something hard to pick out at first, a sound buried in noise and light from an exciton that ordinarily stays dark.

Photonics

Draw large and shrink later

[1] Q. Yang et al., “Isotropic shrinkage of patterned vacancies enables three-dimensional nanoprecise metastructures for visible light applications,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 653–663, May 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01896-1.

If you try to directly carve a three-dimensional structure for visible light, sooner or later you will find a detail smaller than the stroke of your tool. You can look for a better tool, or you can start with a larger piece than you need and reduce it once it's written. This second option also has the advantage of allowing you to reach places that will be buried in the finished device.

The team, with researchers at Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States, works with a swollen hydrogel and uses light to break the material at chosen positions within its volume. This creates gaps whose design can be controlled in three dimensions. The hydrogel is then dehydrated, contracting in all directions and reducing the dimensions of the shapes written inside it by more than ten times in some of the formulations studied.

What has to survive drying is the relationship between all those holes, including those at different depths. If they were deformed in any way we would have a smaller piece, but with different optical behaviour. That is why the final test with visible light deserves attention. The team uses the pattern in an optical calculating device, where the internal distribution of indices counts as much as the dimensions seen from the outside.

Related reading. 3D nanofabrication by volumetric deposition and controlled shrinkage of patterned scaffolds. Science (2018).

Electronics

Memristors to separate a signal from noise

[2] Z. Wang et al., “Biomimetic-voltage nitride-enhanced memristor arrays for femtojoule in-memory biosignal processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6072, May 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72740-x.

When a recording arrives mixed with noise, sending the entire recording to another circuit to clean it also consumes power. Zijian Wang and colleagues at Zhejiang University in China are preparing a 32 × 32 memristor array that can use persistence current to work with the signal before classifying it. Some settings serve to deal with evolution over time and others to preserve information.

Each element combines layers of IGZO and silicon nitride between metal electrodes. The authors observe a switching voltage of 19 mV in one of the device characterizations, while in the test with the array they apply signals of 40 to 60 mV to promote consistent operation. To evaluate the proposal they use ten classes of audio signals and 500 samples, on which they report 100% recognition after noise treatment.

There are two scales that should not be lost sight of when reading the paper. The 1.44 fJ is the energy of a denoising operation on the device. To count how much it costs to recognize a complete recording, you would have to add up how the data is acquired, how it is converted, and how the outputs are read. The success of these 500 samples, for its part, corresponds to the functioning of the set.

Related reading. Dynamic memristor-based reservoir computing for high-efficiency temporal signal processing. Nature Communications (2021).

Materials

A ferroelectric layer lights up a dark exciton

[3] X. Wang et al., “Ferroelectric brightening of spin‑forbidden dark excitons in a WSe2/hybrid-perovskite heterostructure,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6285, May 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72143-y.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

An exciton can be in the sample even if we barely see its light. In a WSe₂ monolayer, the orientation of the spins can make it very unlikely that the bound electron and hole will emit a photon. Instead of applying a magnet, these researchers try changing what is next to the monolayer. They place a ferroelectric perovskite next to it that breaks part of its symmetry.

The previously dark exciton appears in the spectrum without an external magnetic field. Twisting one layer relative to the other changes the coupling strength, making the stacking angle another way to control the response.

I find it especially illuminating to be able to rotate one layer relative to the other. You still have the same semiconductor, but you change how it senses the presence of its neighbour. If the intensity of the dark line that is now visible then varies, the probability of emission can be discussed without assuming that, each time the brightness increases, the number of excitons has also increased.

Related reading. On-site enhancement and control of spin-forbidden dark excitons in a plasmonic heterostructure. Nature Photonics (2025).
