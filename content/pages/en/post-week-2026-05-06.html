Week of May 6, 2026

Shrinking a pattern, separating noise and bringing an exciton into view

Making a pattern at a large scale and shrinking it changes which details can be fabricated. The other two papers concern details that are hard to see, from a signal buried in noise to an optical transition revealed by adding a ferroelectric layer.

Photonics

Draw large and shrink later

[1] Q. Yang et al., “Isotropic shrinkage of patterned vacancies enables three-dimensional nanoprecise metastructures for visible light applications,” Nat. Photon., vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 653–663, May 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01896-1. Open access.

To control visible light with a three-dimensional structure, details must be defined comparable to or smaller than its wavelength, something that can greatly complicate manufacturing when shapes extend into three dimensions. Quansan Yang and his collaborators propose a solution that changes the usual order of the problem, since they first draw the structure at a more comfortable scale and then reduce its size.

The team, with researchers at Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States, works with a swollen hydrogel and uses light to break the material at chosen positions within its volume. This creates gaps whose design can be controlled in three dimensions. The hydrogel is then dehydrated, contracting in all directions and reducing the dimensions of the shapes written inside it by more than ten times in some of the formulations studied.

The authors call the method implosion carving and show that it allows producing structures with details of tens of nanometers to manipulate visible light. Isotropic reduction is important because it helps preserve the shape of the pattern by reducing its size, although in practice deformation must be controlled and cracks avoided during treatment. The procedure thus offers a complementary route to fabricate geometries that are difficult to obtain directly at that scale.

The value of the approach extends beyond making small versions of larger objects. By modifying the three-dimensional layout of materials and voids, you also modify how light propagates through them, opening up opportunities to design photonic components. It remains to be seen how these structures integrate with other materials and processes, and what tolerances can be maintained in a repeatable manner when manufacturing more complex devices.

Electronics

Memristors to separate a signal from noise

[2] Z. Wang et al., “Biomimetic-voltage nitride-enhanced memristor arrays for femtojoule in-memory biosignal processing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6072, May 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72740-x. Open access.

A sensor listening in a noisy environment must distinguish which signal variations contain information and which should be discarded. If this requires constantly transferring data between memory and processor, part of the energy is consumed in the movement of information, a difficulty that is especially relevant in small battery-powered devices.

Zijian Wang and his collaborators, including researchers from the Zhejiang University in China, propose a 32 by 32 memristor array that uses two modes of operation. In one, the resistance change is transient and helps to suppress weak components of the noise. In the other, states are more persistent and are used to store weights intended for a recognition task.

Each element combines layers of IGZO and silicon nitride between metal electrodes. The authors observe a switching voltage of 19 mV in one of the device characterizations, while in the test with the array they apply signals of 40 to 60 mV to promote consistent operation. To evaluate the proposal they use ten classes of audio signals and 500 samples, on which they report 100% recognition after noise treatment.

It is a promising test, although this precision corresponds to the set and the conditions chosen, and the energy of 1.44 femtojoules highlighted by the authors corresponds to a noise elimination operation, not to the consumption of the entire system. In fact, the experimental platform incorporates acquisition, conversion and control electronics. The work shows how the same matrix can distribute temporal and memory tasks, taking advantage of the fact that its elements do not have to maintain the state for the same time.

Materials

A ferroelectric layer lights up a dark exciton

[3] W. Xinyun et al., “Ferroelectric brightening of spin‑forbidden dark excitons in a WSe2/hybrid-perovskite heterostructure,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6285, May 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72143-y. Open access.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

In a WSe₂ monolayer, some bound states of an electron and a hole barely emit light because of their spin orientation. The authors place a ferroelectric perovskite alongside it, changing the symmetry that determines which transitions can emit.

The previously dark exciton appears in the spectrum without an external magnetic field. Twisting one layer relative to the other changes the coupling strength, making the stacking angle another way to control the response.
