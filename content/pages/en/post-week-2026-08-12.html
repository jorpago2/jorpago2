Week of August 12, 2026

Two materials to send information above one hundred gigahertz

From August 12 to 18, 2026

[1] L. Wang et al., “Heterogeneously integrated photonic terahertz transmitter on thin-film lithium niobate,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9797, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76733-8. Open Access.

A very high frequency wireless link needs to generate and modulate fast signals without component losses making the system unviable. Photonics offers a way to produce these signals by mixing two optical tones, although integrating a good modulator and a sufficiently fast photodetector into the same platform remains complicated.

Researchers from ShanghaiTech University, Zhejiang University and City University of Hong Kong in China have coupled a lithium niobate thin film modulator with an indium phosphide-based photodiode. The combination attempts to take advantage of the electro-optic response of the former and the ability of the latter to transform fast optical signals into a high-frequency current.

The team reports a bandwidth of 240 GHz for the integrated photodiode and tests wireless transmission around 150 and 200 GHz with different modulation formats. These are tests of a transmitter and its components in an experimental setup, not of a complete network ready to be deployed. Still, the result illustrates an important strategy in integrated photonics, where different materials come together to solve functions that would be difficult to obtain with just one.

Another use of lithium niobate modulators, this time with individual photons (Light: Science & Applications).

An electrical delay that stores a weight

[2] Y. S. Shin et al., “Memristive‐Gated RC‐Delay Synaptic Transistors for Time‐Encoded Analog in‐Memory Computing,” Adv Funct Materials, Art. no. e77694, Aug. 13, 2026, doi 10.1002/adfm.77694. Open Access.

In an analog operation within memory, it is important that the output maintain a predictable relationship with the input. However, the current of some memristors does not increase linearly with the applied voltage, which can introduce errors when trying to use their conductance as a calculation weight.

Yun-Seo Shin and his collaborators, from Kyung Hee University, in South Korea, propose a structure in which a memristor in the gate of a transistor modifies the response time of a resistive and capacitive circuit. Instead of relying directly on an ideal current-voltage relationship, the device uses that delay to encode the operation, while the programmed resistance preserves the state used as a weight.

The authors present eight analog states and study the linearity of the read charge for different inputs. That demonstration allows us to examine a route to shifting part of the problem from amplitude to time, although an evaluation of larger systems will also have to include the cost of timing, readout, and programming circuits. The interest is in how the functions are distributed between devices and circuit dynamics, and not only in an isolated property of the material.

Boundaries between domains also generate photocurrent

[3] R. Nanae et al., “Domain Engineering and Anisotropic Domain‐Wall Photovoltaic Effects in Ferroelectric SnS,” Adv Funct Materials, vol. 36, no. 75, Art. no. e77741, Aug. 16, 2026, doi 10.1002/adfm.77741. Open access.

In a ferroelectric, neighboring regions can have different polarization orientations and be separated by domain walls. These boundaries are not simple dividing lines, since their local properties can contribute to the electrical response of the material when it receives light.

Ryo Nanae and his collaborators, including researchers from the University of Tokyo in Japan, are studying this effect in tin sulfide, a layered ferroelectric semiconductor. The team obtains crystals with different domains, characterizes their structure and examines the current generated by illumination by changing the arrangement of the walls with respect to the electrodes.

The measurements indicate that the contribution of these walls depends on their orientation within the device, so that the same sample can respond differently depending on how the signal is collected. The authors combine observation with a model to interpret this anisotropy. Work remains to reproducibly control the domains in larger-scale devices, but the study shows why, when designing a photodetector, it matters not only what material is chosen but also how it is organized inside.
