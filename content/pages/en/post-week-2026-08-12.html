Week of August 12, 2026

Writing an infrared image, storing charge and thinning silver

A drawing of an infrared pixel array tends to leave something out. The heater and its wiring have to fit somewhere alongside the material that modulates the light. The other papers take us inside two solids, one that remembers an electrical pulse and another that doubles the frequency of light in just a few layers of silver.

Photonics

An infrared image written pixel by pixel

[1] C.-C. Popescu et al., “Two-dimensional pixel-level addressable mid-infrared metasurface spatial light modulator,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8393, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75346-5.

Drawing an infrared pattern with individual heaters seems simple until we try to turn on a pixel without writing to the one next to it. Popescu and colleagues build six rows by six columns and add a diode at each position to prevent current from finding unwanted paths through the array. Each connection that solves that problem takes up space that could transmit light.

Cosmin-Constantin Popescu and his collaborators, with the participation of Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States and KAIST in South Korea, have developed a transmissive array for the mid-infrared based on GSST, a phase change material. When passing between the amorphous and crystalline states, the GSST changes its refractive index and shifts a resonance of the structure, allowing the intensity of light transmitted by each pixel to be modified.

At 2.585 µm they measure a transmission contrast of 5.1 dB and can program simple patterns. The contrast of a pixel, however, says little about the brightness of the image if the active part occupies only a fraction of the surface. They also test how long the writing lasts. After about 16,700 stable cycles, a delamination failure appears, a concrete clue as to what should be improved in the next matrix.

Related reading. Electrically actuated phase-change pixels for transmissive and reflective spatial light modulators in the near and mid infrared. Applied Optics (2015).

Electronics

A memory based on charge density waves

[2] R. Venturini et al., “Room-temperature memristive switching between charge density wave states,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8360, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73267-x.

Writing data with electricity and erasing it by heating the solid seems like a somewhat peculiar combination. In EuTe₄ it makes sense because the electrons and atoms in the lattice can be arranged in more than one way, and each arrangement leaves a resistance that can be read after the excitation is removed.

Venturini and colleagues study the laminar compound EuTe₄ and show that electrical pulses can lead it to persistent resistance states in a range that includes room temperature. The resistance achieved depends on the pulse voltage and the state can be cleared by a thermal procedure, allowing the device to exhibit memristive behavior.

The team, with participation from Jožef Stefan Institute, in Slovenia, compares exfoliated devices with calculations that allow looking at the layers separately. According to these calculations, single layers admit two configurations of the charge density wave and bilayers retain their own. The pulse width is used to choose the resistance even at room temperature. The erasure follows a thermal path, a decisive detail if we wanted to know how long it would take a memory to write the same data again.

Related reading. Room-temperature non-volatile optical manipulation of polar order in a charge density wave. Nature Communications (2024).

Materials

When thinning silver changes its non-linear response

[3] P. K. Jenke et al., “Few-atom-thick silver films for enhanced nanoscale nonlinear optics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8214, Aug. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74804-4.

To generate light at twice the frequency of a laser, would you choose the thicker or the thinner silver film? The answer depends in part on what you are comparing. Leaving only a few atomic layers changes the space in which electrons can move and, with it, the states available to them perpendicular to the film.

Philipp Jenke and his collaborators, including researchers from the University of Vienna in Austria and the ICFO in Spain, have studied ultrathin crystalline films of silver grown on silicon. By illuminating them, they measure second harmonic generation, a nonlinear response that produces light at twice the excitation frequency, and compare how it changes with the number of layers.

In the few-layer regime, the thickness-normalized efficiency improves by almost two orders of magnitude. The team's calculations link this improvement to the confinement of electrons. So let's go back to the initial choice. If silver is scarce, it matters how much you get per unit of thickness. If what the experiment needs is power in the detector, it will also be necessary to count how much material the beam has seen.

Related reading. Nonlinear plasmonic response in atomically thin metal films. Nanophotonics (2021).
