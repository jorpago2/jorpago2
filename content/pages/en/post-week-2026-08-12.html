Week of August 12, 2026

Writing an infrared image, storing charge and thinning silver

An infrared image can be written by switching the phase of individual pixels. We then look at storing an electrical state in the collective order of electrons and at an experiment in which thinning a silver film actually strengthens the light it generates at twice the incident frequency.

Photonics

An infrared image written pixel by pixel

[1] C.-C. Popescu et al., “Two-dimensional pixel-level addressable mid-infrared metasurface spatial light modulator,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8393, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75346-5.

A metasurface sets an optical pattern through the shape and placement of its elements, but writing another image requires a way to change the local response. The difficulty increases when you want each area of ​​the surface to change independently, since the optical material, the heaters and the electrical connections have to coexist in a reduced space without the writing of one pixel accidentally altering the others.

Cosmin-Constantin Popescu and his collaborators, with the participation of Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States and KAIST in South Korea, have developed a transmissive array for the mid-infrared based on GSST, a phase change material. When passing between the amorphous and crystalline states, the GSST changes its refractive index and shifts a resonance of the structure, allowing the intensity of light transmitted by each pixel to be modified.

The prototype contains six rows and six columns of electrically addressable pixels, with a selector diode in each position to reduce unwanted currents through the array. The authors measure a transmission contrast of 5.1 dB at a wavelength of 2.585 µm and use the array to program simple patterns. In an endurance test, switching was stable for about 16,700 cycles before delamination failure occurred.

These figures put the progress in context. The device mainly modifies the transmitted amplitude, does not offer arbitrary control of all light properties, and its fill factor is still low. On the other hand, optical characterization provided relative estimates from images, a limitation that the authors acknowledge.

Electronics

A memory based on charge density waves

[2] R. Venturini et al., “Room-temperature memristive switching between charge density wave states,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8360, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73267-x.

In EuTe₄ the electron density varies periodically along with a lattice distortion, giving the same solid different ways to order. If there are multiple stable configurations, switching from one to another could modify the resistance and provide a way to store information. Until now, many electrical demonstrations of this type required very low temperatures.

Venturini and colleagues study the laminar compound EuTe₄ and show that electrical pulses can lead it to persistent resistance states in a range that includes room temperature. The resistance achieved depends on the pulse voltage and the state can be cleared by a thermal procedure, allowing the device to exhibit memristive behavior.

The work, carried out with the participation of Jožef Stefan Institute in Slovenia, combines measurements on exfoliated devices and calculations to examine the role of different layers of the material. In the calculations the single layers admit two charge-density-wave configurations, while the bilayers stay in the same one. In the device the electrical pulse amplitude selects the resistance. Returning to the starting state, however, requires heating the sample.

Materials

When thinning silver changes its non-linear response

[3] P. K. Jenke et al., “Few-atom-thick silver films for enhanced nanoscale nonlinear optics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8214, Aug. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74804-4.

In a silver film only a few atomic layers thick, the available thickness limits the states electrons can occupy perpendicular to the surface. Their motion is confined in one direction and discrete energy levels appear, a modification that can change the way the material responds to light.

Philipp Jenke and his collaborators, including researchers from the University of Vienna in Austria and the ICFO in Spain, have studied ultrathin crystalline films of silver grown on silicon. By illuminating them, they measure second harmonic generation, a nonlinear response that produces light at twice the excitation frequency, and compare how it changes with the number of layers.

The authors observe an improvement of almost two orders of magnitude in the thickness-normalized conversion efficiency when reducing the films to the regime of few atomic layers. Their calculations relate this evolution to the confined electronic states, which allows us to discuss the optical response based on the electronic structure of the film itself and not only the field concentrated by a plasmonic geometry.

Choosing a film for a second-harmonic source also requires knowing the total power delivered to the detector. Thickness-normalised efficiency indicates how effectively the available silver is used, while the collected power also depends on how much of it interacts with the beam.
