Week of August 12, 2026

Two materials to send information above one hundred gigahertz

[1] L. Wang et al., “Heterogeneously integrated photonic terahertz transmitter on thin-film lithium niobate,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9797, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76733-8. Open Access.

A very high frequency wireless link needs to generate and modulate fast signals without component losses making the system unviable. Photonics offers a way to produce these signals by mixing two optical tones, although integrating a good modulator and a sufficiently fast photodetector into the same platform remains complicated.

Researchers from ShanghaiTech University, Zhejiang University and City University of Hong Kong in China have coupled a lithium niobate thin film modulator with an indium phosphide-based photodiode. The combination attempts to take advantage of the electro-optic response of the former and the ability of the latter to transform fast optical signals into a high-frequency current.

The team reports a bandwidth of 240 GHz for the integrated photodiode and tests wireless transmission around 150 and 200 GHz with different modulation formats. These are tests of a transmitter and its components in an experimental setup, not of a complete network ready to be deployed. Still, the result illustrates an important strategy in integrated photonics, where different materials come together to solve functions that would be difficult to obtain with just one.

Another use of lithium niobate modulators, this time with individual photons (Light: Science & Applications).

A photodetector that picks out a flame by its ultraviolet light

[2] M. Zhang et al., “Isotropic-carrier-transport perovskite single crystal enables high-photoresponsivity solar-blind photodetectors for selective flame monitoring,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9779, Aug. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76846-0. Open access.

A flame detector has to pick out a weak signal in the presence of other light sources. Researchers at Zhengzhou University in China build a photodetector from a CsCdCl₃ single crystal. Its structure helps light-generated charges move in three dimensions and reach the contacts.

The team measures a dark current on the order of one picoampere and a response concentrated within a band around 20 nm wide. That selectivity helps distinguish the flames tested in the study by their ultraviolet emission. Tests against different lighting backgrounds and at longer distances would clarify how the detector works outside the laboratory.

What a ferroelectric layer passes on to graphene

[3] Y. Shao et al., “Ferroelectric-assisted charge-transfer plasmons in graphene/CuInP2S6,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10102, Aug. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76909-2. Open access.

When two materials are stacked with a clean interface, a transfer of charge can change how light travels along the surface. Researchers at Columbia and Penn State in the United States bring graphene into contact with the layered ferroelectric CuInP₂S₆ and use an infrared microscope to observe collective charge waves travelling through the graphene.

As the ferroelectric grows thicker, the graphene doping and the layer's polarization both rise and then level off. That shared trend helps explain the charge transfer. It also suggests that the optical response of the graphene could reveal the state of the underlying ferroelectric.
