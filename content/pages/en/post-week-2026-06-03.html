Week of June 3, 2026

Pulses in silicon nitride, stretchable memory and emitters acting together

A laser can deliver high peak power by concentrating its energy into femtosecond pulses. The other papers ask how long illumination leaves a trace in a stretchable memory and how the emission changes when many nanocrystals respond together.

Photonics

Intense pulses from a silicon nitride circuit

[1] Z. Qiu et al., “High-pulse-energy integrated mode-locked laser using a Mamyshev oscillator,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8117, pp. 57–63, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10517-4.

A nanojoule sounds modest until it is concentrated into a femtosecond pulse, whose peak power can drive nonlinear optical processes. Bringing that type of source to an integrated circuit is attractive, although the pulse energies obtained on chips had been a limitation for some of these applications. Qiu and colleagues address the problem with an erbium-implanted silicon nitride waveguide that acts as a gain medium.

The cavity follows the principle of a Mamyshev oscillator, in which non-linear broadening of the spectrum and two shifted filters favor the formation of pulses. The device generates a train near 176 MHz and achieves energies on the order of one nanojoule per pulse at each output under the main pumping conditions. After external compression, the pulses reached a duration of 147 femtoseconds and allowed the generation of a supercontinuum in another silicon nitride waveguide without subsequent amplification.

The authors further used the source in a terahertz spectroscopy demonstration. The result brings several functions closer to the scale of the chip, although the assembly still involves pump sources and external components, and the compression and guidance for the supercontinuum are not integrated together with the laser in a single circuit. The article initially appeared under subscription and became open access in October 2026.

Participants include École polytechnique fédérale de Lausanne in Switzerland and Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf in Germany.

Related reading. High-pulse-energy III-V-on-silicon-nitride mode-locked laser. APL Photonics (2021).

Electronics

A light-written memory that can stretch

[2] S. H. Park et al., “Reconfigurable skin electronics enabled by intrinsically stretchable photoelectric memory transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5049, Jun. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71589-4.

Participating institutions
Kyung Hee University (South Korea)

In this stretchable transistor, charge trapped in the gate dielectric changes the current subsequently read from the channel. Combining visible light with a gate voltage writes the memory. To erase it, the team changes both the wavelength and the voltage polarity.

The transistor remains functional in tests at 30% strain in two directions. Programming it to keep different states changes which logic operation the same transistor network performs.

Electronic skin deforms while collecting data, so it matters both whether a transistor still conducts and whether the state written before stretching remains distinguishable. Fabricating an array on a four-inch elastomer substrate lets the team test memory and logic in connected devices as well as characterising a transistor on its own.

Related reading. Intrinsically Stretchable Floating Gate Memory Transistors for Data Storage of Electronic Skin Devices. ACS Nano (2024).

Materials

When many emitters of a perovskite respond together

[3] Q. Wei et al., “Chiral superfluorescence from perovskite superlattices at room temperature,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8118, pp. 369–374, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10637-x.

When several emitters radiate in a coordinated way, the observed pulse can differ substantially from what the same emitters would produce independently. Wei and colleagues explore this cooperative response in chiral perovskite superlattices, whose structure can occur in two mirror-related forms, like a left hand and a right hand.

In samples more than one hundred micrometres across, they observed a collective emission with circular polarization at room temperature. The degree of polarization reached approximately 14% and changed sign by reversing the chirality of the material. Furthermore, the team found that a weak magnetic field could modify both the intensity and the polarization, and compared the results with quantum optics calculations.

A telling check is to reverse the handedness of the material. The circular polarization of the emission then changes sign, with the degree of polarization reaching about 14% at room temperature. That reversal links a measurable property of the light to the arrangement of emitters in the crystal.

Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in Hong Kong, China, and Harvard University, in the United States, participate.

Related reading. Superfluorescence from lead halide perovskite quantum dot superlattices. Nature (2018).
