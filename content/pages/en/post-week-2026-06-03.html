Week of June 3, 2026

Pulses in silicon nitride, stretchable memory and emitters acting together

Spread a nanojoule over a second and it barely registers. Squeeze it into a femtosecond pulse and the peak power is enough to generate a broad spread of colours. We'll also see what light leaves behind in a stretchable transistor and which polarisation emerges when many perovskite emitters radiate together.

Photonics

Intense pulses from a silicon nitride circuit

[1] Z. Qiu et al., “High-pulse-energy integrated mode-locked laser using a Mamyshev oscillator,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8117, pp. 57–63, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10517-4.

In a pulse laser, saying how much energy comes out tells only half the story. To know if it will serve to excite a non-linear process, it is also necessary to know how long it takes. In this work, with the participation of École polytechnique fédérale de Lausanne in Switzerland and Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf in Germany, each pulse carries on the order of one nanojoule per output under the main pumping conditions. And about 176 million arrive per second.

The cavity follows the principle of a Mamyshev oscillator, in which non-linear broadening of the spectrum and two shifted filters favor the formation of pulses. The device generates a train near 176 MHz and achieves energies on the order of one nanojoule per pulse at each output under the main pumping conditions. After external compression, the pulses reached a duration of 147 femtoseconds and allowed the generation of a supercontinuum in another silicon nitride waveguide without subsequent amplification.

Once off the chip they compress them up to 147 fs and send them to another silicon nitride waveguide. Without adding an amplification stage, they achieve a supercontinuum. That second waveguide makes a pretty convincing test of the peak power one can actually use in an experiment. The article was initially published under subscription and became open access in October 2026.

Related reading. High-pulse-energy III-V-on-silicon-nitride mode-locked laser. APL Photonics (2021).

Electronics

A light-written memory that can stretch

[2] S. H. Park et al., “Reconfigurable skin electronics enabled by intrinsically stretchable photoelectric memory transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5049, Jun. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71589-4.

Participating institutions
Kyung Hee University (South Korea)

A memory for an electronic skin will have to remember what was written yesterday even if you stretch it to put it on today. It is not enough for the film to lead to the end of the stretch. The two states must continue to give distinguishable readings. In this transistor, visible light and gate voltage write the memory by leaving charge trapped in the insulator. To erase it, the colour of the light and the sign of the voltage are changed.

The transistor remains functional in tests at 30% strain in two directions. Programming it to keep different states changes which logic operation the same transistor network performs.

The team fabricates the array on a four-inch elastic substrate. Working at that scale allows us to test something that escapes when stretching an isolated transistor. By changing saved states, you can change the logical function of multiple cells that are already connected. Thus, the skin-friendly design can continue to do different operations even after you have decided where the connections will go.

Related reading. Intrinsically Stretchable Floating Gate Memory Transistors for Data Storage of Electronic Skin Devices. ACS Nano (2024).

Materials

When many emitters of a perovskite respond together

[3] Q. Wei et al., “Chiral superfluorescence from perovskite superlattices at room temperature,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8118, pp. 369–374, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10637-x.

Put many emitters together and, in principle, you would expect a brighter version of the light each gives off. Something else happens in Wei and colleagues's perovskite superlattices. The emitters can coordinate and the order of the supernetwork distinguishes between two configurations that are mirror images. One way to check if that order reaches the light is to prepare both and compare.

At room temperature, in samples larger than one hundred micrometres per side, they observe collective emission with a degree of circular polarisation of around 14%. By inverting the chirality, the sign of that polarisation is also inverted.

There is the experimental control that seems most direct to me. It would be difficult to attribute the change to a simple increase in brightness when the two samples return opposite preferences. Participating in the study are Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in Hong Kong, China, and Harvard University, in the United States.

Related reading. Superfluorescence from lead halide perovskite quantum dot superlattices. Nature (2018).
