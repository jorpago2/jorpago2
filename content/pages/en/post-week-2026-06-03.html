Week of June 3, 2026

A compact laser, a remembering oxide and collective light

Photonics

Intense pulses from a silicon nitride circuit

[1] Z. Qiu et al., “High-pulse-energy integrated mode-locked laser using a Mamyshev oscillator,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8117, pp. 57–63, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10517-4.

Participants include École polytechnique fédérale de Lausanne in Switzerland and Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf in Germany.

A very brief pulse of light can concentrate substantial power in an instant, which is especially useful for driving nonlinear optical processes. Bringing that type of source to an integrated circuit is attractive, although the pulse energies obtained on chips had been a limitation for some of these applications. Qiu and colleagues address the problem with an erbium-implanted silicon nitride waveguide that acts as a gain medium.

The cavity follows the principle of a Mamyshev oscillator, in which non-linear broadening of the spectrum and two shifted filters favor the formation of pulses. The device generates a train near 176 MHz and achieves energies on the order of one nanojoule per pulse at each output under the main pumping conditions. After external compression, the pulses reached a duration of 147 femtoseconds and allowed the generation of a supercontinuum in another silicon nitride waveguide without subsequent amplification.

The authors further used the source in a terahertz spectroscopy demonstration. The result brings several functions closer to the scale of the chip, although the assembly still involves pump sources and external components, and the compression and guidance for the supercontinuum are not integrated together with the laser in a single circuit. The article initially appeared under subscription and became open access in October 2026.

Electronics

A resistive memory with calcium zirconate

[2] V. Raja, S. Nehra, A. Dogra, and A. Rastogi, “Bipolar resistive switching in amorphous calcium zirconate memristors,” Sci Rep, vol. 16, no. 1, Art. no. 24022, Jun. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41598-026-51653-1.

The study brings together Vellore Institute of Technology and CSIR National Physical Laboratory, in India.

A resistive memory retains information because its conductance can change when a voltage is applied and be maintained after it is removed. Raja and his collaborators study this possibility in an amorphous calcium zirconate film placed between two electrodes, seeking to understand both the repeatability of the switching and the mechanism that produces it.

The devices show bipolar switching with voltages of the order of one volt, and the authors report tests of more than 120,000 cycles and retention of more than 5,500 seconds. To interpret current curves and chemical measurements, they propose that conductive pathways rich in oxygen vacancies are formed and interrupted. Furthermore, the temperature dependence of resistance suggests that these paths have a semiconducting character, rather than behaving like conventional metallic filaments.

The interest is in adding a relatively simple oxide to the repertoire of materials that can be studied for memories and logic circuits. However, the tests refer to the specific devices and conditions of the article, and are not a substitute for an evaluation of matrices, large-scale variability, or retention for much longer storage times. The journal first published an unedited version and then the definitive text that is now being consulted.

Materials

When many emitters of a perovskite respond together

[3] Q. Wei et al., “Chiral superfluorescence from perovskite superlattices at room temperature,” Nature, vol. 654, no. 8118, pp. 369–374, Jun. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10637-x.

Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in Hong Kong, China, and Harvard University, in the United States, participate.

When a set of emitters interact collectively, the light produced can have different properties than what we would expect by adding the independent emission of each one. Wei and colleagues explore this cooperative response in chiral perovskite superlattices, whose structure can occur in two mirror-related forms, like a left hand and a right hand.

In samples more than one hundred micrometres across, they observed a collective emission with circular polarization at room temperature. The degree of polarization reached approximately 14% and changed sign by reversing the chirality of the material. Furthermore, the team found that a weak magnetic field could modify both the intensity and the polarization, and compared the results with quantum optics calculations.

The combination of cooperative emission and chirality is suggestive for studying light sources with new forms of control, although the applications mentioned in the work still remain to be developed. It is also worth keeping the experimental result in context, since the polarization percentage and its response to the field are obtained for the samples and excitations tested, not for any chiral perovskite. A later correction updated the affiliation of one of the authors.
