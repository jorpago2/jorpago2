Imaging deep in tissue, stacked transistors and nanoparticles under light

Photonics

Illuminating a plane to see inside a tissue

[1] K. Keomanee-Dizon et al., “Depth-enhanced molecular imaging with two-photon oblique plane microscopy,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11798, Nov. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78668-6. Open access.

Light scattering in thick tissue makes small structures harder to distinguish. A team at Princeton University in the United States combines detection through a single objective with two-photon excitation in an oblique plane. That gives them the benefits of infrared excitation while avoiding the need to scan the entire volume one point at a time.

The microscope reaches resolutions of about 300 nm laterally and 650 nm axially. In the multicellular samples tested, two-photon excitation improves contrast relative to linear excitation. The team demonstrates that it can track RNA molecules in living samples, although performance will still depend on how strongly each tissue scatters light.

Electronics

Two stacked transistors and a cleaner interface

[2] N. Yang et al., “Low-power 2D CFETs using single-crystal high-κ dielectrics,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8141, pp. 657–665, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11272-2. Open Access.

By reducing the size and operating voltage of a transistor, the boundary between the semiconductor and the gate insulator becomes increasingly important. If charges are trapped there, the response is no longer as precise as one would expect. In a work involving the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the University of Hong Kong, in China, researchers prepare a single-crystalline titanium dioxide membrane and transfer it onto two-dimensional semiconductors to improve that interface.

They then build complementary inverters by stacking n-type and p-type transistors, an arrangement designed to make better use of circuit area. At 0.25 V they measure a static power of 38 femtowatts per inverter, but that figure does not include all the energy associated with switching the circuit. Moreover, the possibility of reaching dimensions comparable to a 1 nm technology node comes from simulations, not from a fabricated device. The experimental contribution is to show that a transferable crystalline dielectric can be used to build complementary circuits with low leakage.

Materials

How gold and silicon nanoparticles differ when we measure them

[3] M. Weimar et al., “Controlling the flow of information in optical metrology,” Nat. Mater., Nov. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02789-y. Open access.

When a nanoparticle is illuminated to locate it, each detector collects some of the scattered light. A team including TU Wien in Austria and the University of Southampton in the United Kingdom compares gold and silicon nanospheres. Using the scattered field, they calculate which directions show the greatest change in signal when each sphere moves.

Their maps show that optical energy and information about position can travel in different directions. The material and wavelength also determine which resonances contribute to the measurement. This gives an experimentalist a way to choose the illumination and detector positions. The nanosphere examples are calculations, so the precision of a real setup will also depend on its noise and the amount of light it collects.
