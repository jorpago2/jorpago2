Week of November 11, 2026

Light and sound on a chip, stacked transistors and an infrared image

Where does sound escape from an optical waveguide? One team removes the material underneath to find out which vibration stays behind. We'll also see how little power a stacked inverter draws while idle, and why a thermal camera can reveal a pattern between two regions at the same temperature.

Photonics

A suspended waveguide gives sound room to resonate

[1] L.-S. Haerteis et al., “High‐Frequency Stimulated Brillouin Scattering in Suspended Thin‐Film Lithium Niobate Waveguides,” Nanophotonics, vol. 15, no. 22, Art. no. e70338, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1002/nap2.70338.

Mix two optical waves in a lithium niobate waveguide and their beat can excite a vibration. To keep exchanging energy with the light, that vibration has to travel with the right phase. In a supported guide some of the sound escapes into the substrate. Researchers at Adelaide University in Australia and Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden try the most direct remedy, removing the material beneath a section of the guide.

In the suspended waveguide, the two optical waves driving the vibration are 16.8 GHz apart. Measuring how energy transfers between them gives a Brillouin gain coefficient of 23.8 W⁻¹ m⁻¹. That tells us how strongly the signal grows under the conditions of the experiment.

What I particularly like here is the independent check on the gain measurement. Microscopy shows which acoustic mode is actually vibrating, something you would want to know before asking the waveguide to select a microwave frequency.

Related reading. Integrated Brillouin photonics in thin-film lithium niobate. Science Advances (2025).

Electronics

Two stacked transistors and a cleaner interface

[2] N. Yang et al., “Low-power 2D CFETs using single-crystal high-κ dielectrics,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8141, pp. 657–665, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11272-2.

With a quarter-volt supply there is little room for error in a transistor’s turn-on voltage. A small amount of charge trapped between the channel and the insulator can shift the threshold. A team at National University of Singapore in Singapore and University of Hong Kong in China prepares the insulator separately, as a single-crystal titanium dioxide membrane, then transfers it onto a two-dimensional semiconductor.

Using that interface, the researchers stack an n-type transistor and a p-type transistor to build an inverter. At 0.25 V they measure 38 femtowatts of static power per inverter. That tells us what the circuit draws while it waits. Working out how much it costs to switch would take a different measurement.

The paper also explores dimensions comparable to a 1 nm technology node. That part comes from simulation, whereas the power measurement comes from the inverter on the bench.

Related reading. What makes contacts and dielectrics difficult to integrate in 2D transistors (Nature Communications).

Materials

Heating a film reveals a hidden infrared pattern

[3] X. Chen, J. Huang, S. Zheng, K. Yu, M. Qian, and Y. Liu, “Nonvolatile multistate phase-change emitter for selective-access visible–infrared information encryption,” APL Photonics, vol. 11, no. 11, Art. no. 116106, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1063/5.0358648.

Could a thermal camera pick out a drawing on a surface whose temperature is the same everywhere? It could if the ink and the background emitted differently in the camera’s infrared band. A team at Henan Normal University in China makes the equivalent of that ink by varying the thickness of a Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ film from one place to another.

When the film is freshly prepared it is amorphous, and the infrared pattern is hard to distinguish. Heating crystallises the film and brings out the contrast. The drawing is still there when the heat is removed, even though the sample looks much the same in visible light.

This leaves an interesting distinction between writing and revealing the information. The design was already present in the thickness profile chosen during fabrication. Heating makes that choice legible to the thermal camera.

Related reading. Thermal information encryption based on composite metasurfaces comprising volatile and nonvolatile phase-change materials. Optics & Laser Technology (2026).
