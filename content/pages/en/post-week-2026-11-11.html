Week of November 11, 2026

Light and sound on a chip, stacked transistors and an infrared image

Sometimes the way to make light excite a high-frequency vibration on a chip is to take material away. This week, a suspended waveguide shows what happens when the substrate is removed, while a pair of stacked transistors owes part of its performance to a layer added at the gate. The third paper varies the thickness of a film to hide a pattern that only shows up in infrared after heating.

Photonics

A suspended waveguide gives sound room to resonate

[1] L.-S. Haerteis et al., “High‐Frequency Stimulated Brillouin Scattering in Suspended Thin‐Film Lithium Niobate Waveguides,” Nanophotonics, vol. 15, no. 22, Art. no. e70338, Nov. 13, 2026, doi 10.1002/nap2.70338. Open access.

When two light waves travelling through a waveguide differ by the right frequency, they can drive a mechanical vibration and exchange energy through it. In lithium niobate waveguides, some of that vibration can leak into the substrate. To keep more of the sound in the guide, a team including researchers from Adelaide University in Australia and Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden removes the material beneath a section of the guide.

In the suspended guide, the team measures a Brillouin shift of 16.8 GHz and a gain of 23.8 per watt per metre. An independent microscopy measurement also confirms that the acoustic mode behind the result is present in the waveguide. That combination of frequency and gain could help in designing filters that use light to select a narrow band of microwaves, a step that still needs to be demonstrated in a complete device.

Another use of lithium niobate modulators, this time with individual photons (Light: Science & Applications).

Electronics

Two stacked transistors and a cleaner interface

[2] N. Yang et al., “Low-power 2D CFETs using single-crystal high-κ dielectrics,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8141, pp. 657–665, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11272-2. Open Access.

By reducing the size and operating voltage of a transistor, the boundary between the semiconductor and the gate insulator becomes increasingly important. If charges are trapped there, the response is no longer as precise as one would expect. In a work involving the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the University of Hong Kong, in China, researchers prepare a single-crystalline titanium dioxide membrane and transfer it onto two-dimensional semiconductors to improve that interface.

They then build complementary inverters by stacking n-type and p-type transistors, an arrangement designed to make better use of circuit area. At 0.25 V they measure a static power of 38 femtowatts per inverter, but that figure does not include all the energy associated with switching the circuit. Moreover, the possibility of reaching dimensions comparable to a 1 nm technology node comes from simulations, not from a fabricated device. The experimental contribution is to show that a transferable crystalline dielectric can be used to build complementary circuits with low leakage.

What makes contacts and dielectrics difficult to integrate in 2D transistors (Nature Communications).

Materials

Heating a film reveals a hidden infrared pattern

[3] X. Chen, J. Huang, S. Zheng, K. Yu, M. Qian, and Y. Liu, “Nonvolatile multistate phase-change emitter for selective-access visible–infrared information encryption,” APL Photon., vol. 11, no. 11, Art. no. 116106, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1063/5.0358648. Open access.

A thermal camera can distinguish two areas at the same temperature if one emits more infrared radiation than the other. Working from that idea, researchers at Henan Normal University in China encode a pattern by varying the thickness of a film of Ge₂Sb₂Te₅, a material that can crystallise when heated. While the film is amorphous, the areas look much alike in infrared. Once it crystallises, however, the difference in emission is large enough for a thermal camera to read the pattern.

The contrast remains after the heat that triggered crystallisation is removed, while the sample looks much the same in visible light. This makes it possible to design the visible colour and the infrared pattern separately on the same surface. In the prototype, the geometry of the film fixes the pattern, and heating reveals it.
