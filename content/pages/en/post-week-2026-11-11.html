Week of November 11, 2026

Receiving light on a lithium niobate chip

Photonics

[1] W. Tang et al., “Thin-film lithium niobate waveguide-integrated p-i-n Tungsten selenide photodiodes with 10Gbit/s data reception,” Nat Commun, Nov. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-79595-2. Open access, accepted version published early.

Thin-film lithium niobate has become an attractive platform for photonic circuits because it allows light to be guided and modulated in very small spaces. However, getting that signal to an integrated detector remains a practical problem. A team involving Huazhong University of Science and Technology and Tsinghua University, in China, proposes combining the niobate guide with a tungsten diselenide photodiode, a semiconductor that can be prepared in very thin layers.

The detector's p-i-n junction is formed through localized, self-aligned doping, designed to reduce processes that slow down the carriers. The measured bandwidth is 1.12 GHz, and the device receives data at up to 10 Gbit/s using visible light. These are different quantities, so the data rate should not be confused with the bandwidth. In a separate measurement at 638 nm, the authors report a responsivity of about 492 mA/W. The result brings detection into the waveguide itself, although integration into a complete photonic system and testing at a larger scale remain to be addressed.

The same lithium niobate platform can generate ultraviolet light on a chip (Harvard SEAS).

Electronics

Two stacked transistors and a cleaner interface

[2] N. Yang et al., “Low-power 2D CFETs using single-crystal high-κ dielectrics,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8141, pp. 657–665, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11272-2. Open Access.

By reducing the size and operating voltage of a transistor, the boundary between the semiconductor and the gate insulator becomes increasingly important. If charges are trapped there, the response is no longer as precise as one would expect. In a work involving the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the University of Hong Kong, in China, researchers prepare a single-crystalline titanium dioxide membrane and transfer it onto two-dimensional semiconductors to improve that interface.

They then build complementary inverters by stacking n-type and p-type transistors, an arrangement designed to make better use of circuit area. At 0.25 V they measure a static power of 38 femtowatts per inverter, but that figure does not include all the energy associated with switching the circuit. Moreover, the possibility of reaching dimensions comparable to a 1 nm technology node comes from simulations, not from a fabricated device. The experimental contribution is to show that a transferable crystalline dielectric can be used to build complementary circuits with low leakage.

Materials

A clay to make insulators at room temperature

[3] W. Li et al., “Low-thermal-budget solution-processed montmorillonite dielectric for low-voltage 2D electronics,” Nat Commun, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-79139-8. Open access, accepted version published early.

Montmorillonite is a layered clay and, as researchers at Tsinghua University in China show, it can also serve a less common role as a gate dielectric for two-dimensional electronics. Their proposal is based on an aqueous ink without organic ligands or surfactants, with which they deposit the material at room temperature. By avoiding these auxiliary molecules, they also avoid the subsequent heat treatment that would normally be required to remove them.

The advantage goes beyond simpler preparation. Solution-processed molybdenum disulfide transistors operate at 1 V and show small hysteresis, down to 1 mV in the reported measurements. The authors also test compatibility with other semiconductors and build logic gates and static memory cells. How the process would perform outside the laboratory and in more complex manufacturing remains to be studied, but the work offers an interesting alternative when temperature limits the choice of materials or substrates.
