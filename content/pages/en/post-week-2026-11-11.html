Week of November 11, 2026

Light and sound on a chip, stacked transistors and an infrared image

Removing the support under a waveguide, inserting an insulator between two layers or changing a film’s thickness may look like modest edits to a fabrication drawing. Here they determine whether a vibration stays confined, how much current leaks from a transistor and which pattern an infrared camera sees.

Photonics

A suspended waveguide gives sound room to resonate

[1] L.-S. Haerteis et al., “High‐Frequency Stimulated Brillouin Scattering in Suspended Thin‐Film Lithium Niobate Waveguides,” Nanophotonics, vol. 15, no. 22, Art. no. e70338, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1002/nap2.70338.

Two optical waves can drive a vibration in a waveguide at their beat frequency, provided the vibration propagates with the phase needed to exchange energy with the light. In lithium niobate waveguides, some of that vibration can leak into the substrate. To keep more of the sound in the guide, a team including researchers from Adelaide University in Australia and Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden removes the material beneath a section of the guide.

In the suspended guide the optical waves that excite the vibration differ in frequency by 16.8 GHz. The team measures a Brillouin gain coefficient of 23.8 W⁻¹ m⁻¹ and independently identifies the acoustic mode by microscopy. That last check helps establish which vibration a microwave filter based on this guide would need to select.

Related reading. Integrated Brillouin photonics in thin-film lithium niobate. Science Advances (2025).

Electronics

Two stacked transistors and a cleaner interface

[2] N. Yang et al., “Low-power 2D CFETs using single-crystal high-κ dielectrics,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8141, pp. 657–665, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11272-2.

In a transistor that switches with a small voltage swing, the interface between channel and insulator deserves particular attention because a small additional charge can shift the conduction threshold. If charges are trapped there, the response is no longer as precise as one would expect. In a work involving the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the University of Hong Kong, in China, researchers prepare a single-crystalline titanium dioxide membrane and transfer it onto two-dimensional semiconductors to improve that interface.

They then build complementary inverters by stacking n-type and p-type transistors, an arrangement designed to make better use of circuit area. At 0.25 V they measure a static power of 38 femtowatts per inverter, but that figure does not include all the energy associated with switching the circuit. Moreover, the possibility of reaching dimensions comparable to a 1 nm technology node comes from simulations, not from a fabricated device.

Related reading. What makes contacts and dielectrics difficult to integrate in 2D transistors (Nature Communications).

Materials

Heating a film reveals a hidden infrared pattern

[3] X. Chen, J. Huang, S. Zheng, K. Yu, M. Qian, and Y. Liu, “Nonvolatile multistate phase-change emitter for selective-access visible–infrared information encryption,” APL Photonics, vol. 11, no. 11, Art. no. 116106, Nov. 11, 2026, doi 10.1063/5.0358648.

Two surfaces at the same temperature can give different readings on a thermal camera if their emissivities differ in the infrared band seen by its detector. Working from that idea, researchers at Henan Normal University in China encode a pattern by varying the thickness of a film of Ge₂Sb₂Te₅, a material that can crystallise when heated. While the film is amorphous, the areas look much alike in infrared. Once it crystallises, however, the difference in emission is large enough for a thermal camera to read the pattern.

The contrast remains after the heat that triggered crystallisation is removed, while the sample looks much the same in visible light. This makes it possible to design the visible colour and the infrared pattern separately on the same surface. In the prototype, the geometry of the film fixes the pattern, and heating reveals it.

Related reading. Thermal information encryption based on composite metasurfaces comprising volatile and nonvolatile phase-change materials. Optics & Laser Technology (2026).
