Gates of light and ferroelectric memories

Week of 11–17 March 2026

An optical gate that changes its function

[1] Y. Ge et al., “All-optical logic processing unit using Kerr nonlinearity of MXene,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4078, Mar. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70834-0. Date in Crossref Open access.

For a logic operation to use light as both input and output, one optical signal must be able to influence another, which does not happen appreciably in most materials at low intensities. Ge and colleagues explore an alternative based on the nonlinear optical response of a MXene, one of a family of layered materials whose composition and surface offer many opportunities for adjustment.

Their approach uses a high-entropy MXene and electrically modifies the groups on its surface to change the device response. In this way the authors demonstrate seven Boolean operations with the same optical configuration, including AND, OR and XOR. Optical here refers to the processing of the signals, while reconfiguration of the function requires electrical control.

The team, which includes researchers from Shenzhen University in China, also presents a digit-recognition application using a network based on logic gates. The demonstration is suggestive for imagining programmable systems that exploit two-dimensional materials, although integration of the whole setup and the energy consumed by the complete system remain important questions when comparing this architecture with available electronics.

A memristor that also rectifies

[2] Y. Kim et al., “Programmable ferroelectric rectifier for reliable and efficient neuromorphic crossbar array,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6377, Mar. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70727-2. Date in Crossref Open access.

Arranging resistive memories in an array brings a problem that is not apparent when a single cell is studied. Current can travel along unwanted paths through neighbouring cells and affect the readout. A common solution is to add a selector element, although that makes the array more complex.

Kim and colleagues investigate an alternative in which the memristor itself combines resistance switching with a strong rectifying response. The device uses an epitaxial film of barium-modified bismuth ferrite, involving both ferroelectric polarization and the movement of oxygen vacancies. The authors report a rectification ratio above one million and endurance beyond ten million cycles under the tested conditions.

Participating institutions include Seoul National University and Kookmin University in South Korea, as well as the University of Southern California in the United States. The combination is attractive for neuromorphic arrays because it seeks to store a conductance state and reduce parasitic currents in the same element. It remains to be seen, however, how those advantages hold up as arrays grow and are integrated with their control circuits.

Changing the direction of polarization

[3] S. Zhou et al., “Templated perpendicular ferroelectricity in textured Aurivillius oxide-based thin films,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3890, Mar. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70676-w. Date record Open access.

In a ferroelectric film, the orientation of polarization matters as much as its existence, because that direction affects how the material can be written and read using electrodes. Bismuth tungstate belongs to a family of layered oxides whose polarization tends to lie in the plane of the layers, a feature that can limit certain device geometries.

The study by Zhou and colleagues, involving Zhejiang University and the Institute of Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, proposes using the material’s own structure as a template to stabilize a metastable phase of tungsten oxide. The result is a film with out-of-plane polarization, whose structure is analysed using atomic-resolution microscopy and calculations.

The authors also fabricated test ferroelectric transistors and memristors, observing switching ratios above one million and thermal stability up to 350 °C in the tests described. The interest goes beyond those figures because it shows how a crystal structure can help stabilize a phase offering a different polarization orientation, although its behaviour in manufacturing processes and more complex circuits still needs to be evaluated.
