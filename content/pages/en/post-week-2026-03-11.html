Week of March 11, 2026

Mixing light and magnetism, storing charge and watching an LED age

Will a larger array of resonators still couple as strongly to a magnet? This week also brings two questions about operating devices. How much power does it take to set an optical processor, and where does an LED start to deteriorate while it is still producing light?

Photonics

When a flat band helps mix light and magnetism

[1] Q. Hong, J. Qian, F. Chen, Y. Yang, and Y.-P. Wang, “Strong magnon–photon coupling enhanced by photonic lattice flat-bands,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2438, Mar. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69326-y.

A microwave can transfer energy to a magnet when its frequency matches that of the collective precession of the spins. Much more delicate is to make this exchange intense enough so that both stop behaving as independent systems and form hybrid states. The difficulty increases when the photonic structure grows, since the field tends to spread out and the local interaction can weaken.

A team from Zhejiang University and East China Normal University, both in China, has studied a way to address this problem using a network of resonators with a flat band. Many modes with similar frequencies appear in it that, when combined, can concentrate their interaction with a small sphere of yttrium iron garnet. The researchers tune the magnetic response of the sphere with an external field and observe the coupling with the electromagnetic modes of the lattice.

With twelve cells, the hybrid modes split by 180 MHz, compared with 92 MHz in the reference structure. Adding cells might be expected to spread the field over a larger region and weaken the local coupling. In the microwave measurements, the flat band lets the coupling persist as the lattice grows.

Related reading. Observation of a Localized Flat-Band State in a Photonic Lieb Lattice. Physical Review Letters (2015).

Electronics

Electronic memory beside photonic computing

[2] S. Lam et al., “Neuromorphic photonic computing with an electro-optic analog memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2472, Mar. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69084-x.

Participating institutions
University of British Columbia (Canada)

Even when a processor uses light for multiplication, maintaining its electrical settings still contributes to the power bill. The team puts a capacitor-based analogue memory on the same chip so each value can be kept next to the unit using it.

Capacitors lose charge over time, so the authors examine how much the values can drift before digit classification suffers. Their energy analysis estimates how much shortening that path could save compared with digital memory and converters.

In practice, the question is how often the memory needs refreshing. If the capacitor loses little charge during an inference, the optical setting can remain accurate enough to recognise a digit. Wait too long and the weight will have changed even for an identical input. The ratio of computation time to discharge time therefore sets the scope for saving energy on conversions.

Related reading. A full spectrum of computing-in-memory technologies. Nature Electronics (2023).

Materials

Watching a working LED degrade under a microscope

[3] X. Li et al., “Multimodal electron microscopy of halide perovskite interfacial dynamics,” Nature, vol. 651, no. 8106, pp. 614–620, Mar. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10238-8.

Participating institutions
University of Cambridge (United Kingdom)

To follow a perovskite LED as it ages, the researchers operate it inside an electron microscope, where they can track changes in both composition and structure. It can then see where the first changes appear as the device operates.

The changes cluster near the charge transport layers. Lead-rich regions form and grains fragment, while part of the aluminium contact turns into an insulating compound. The interior of the emitting layer holds up better, directing attention to the interfaces.

An LED can dim because its emitter converts current into light less efficiently or because the contacts no longer supply the charge. Brightness alone cannot distinguish the two. Tracking composition and strain across the layers under bias helps locate the failure even when some of the emitting material still retains its original structure.

Related reading. In Situ TEM Analysis of Organic–Inorganic Metal-Halide Perovskite Solar Cells under Electrical Bias. Nano Letters (2016).
