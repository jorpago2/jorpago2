Week of March 11, 2026

Mixing light and magnetism, storing charge and watching an LED age

A magnet can exchange energy with a microwave field, but scaling up the resonators that bring them together changes the picture. We'll also look at what it costs an optical processor to hold an electrical setting, and what an LED can tell us about its own failure while it still gives off light.

Photonics

When a flat band helps mix light and magnetism

[1] Q. Hong, J. Qian, F. Chen, Y. Yang, and Y.-P. Wang, “Strong magnon–photon coupling enhanced by photonic lattice flat-bands,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2438, Mar. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69326-y.

First you match a microwave resonance to the frequency at which a magnet's spins precess. If the energy exchange is sufficiently intense, two hybrid modes appear in the spectrum. Now, if you add resonators and the field is spread over a larger region, will the magnet still find enough field right in its position?

A team from Zhejiang University and East China Normal University, both in China, has studied a way to address this problem using a network of resonators with a flat band. Many modes with similar frequencies appear in it that, when combined, can concentrate their interaction with a small sphere of yttrium iron garnet. The researchers tune the magnetic response of the sphere with an external field and observe the coupling with the electromagnetic modes of the lattice.

With twelve cells, the two modes are separated by 180 MHz, compared to 92 MHz in the comparison structure. This separation allows us to read in a spectrum how much both systems have been coupled. The attraction of the flat band is precisely that you can expand the network while preserving a local concentration of field, when at first glance it would be reasonable to expect that each added cell would dilute the interaction a little more.

Related reading. Observation of a Localized Flat-Band State in a Photonic Lieb Lattice. Physical Review Letters (2015).

Electronics

Electronic memory beside photonic computing

[2] S. Lam et al., “Neuromorphic photonic computing with an electro-optic analog memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2472, Mar. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69084-x.

Participating institutions
University of British Columbia (Canada)

A processor can do the multiplication with light and spend a good part of the energy moving the number that tells it how much to multiply by. If that number is in a digital memory, it must be read, converted into a voltage and taken to the modulator. Here we try to leave the voltage on a capacitor on the same chip, close to where it is used.

The question then is whether the load can hold until the operation is completed. The team takes a specific task, recognizing digits, and studies how the classification changes by letting the analog weights decay. This way you can estimate what consumption you would avoid compared to an architecture that uses converters.

If you are going to upload a lot of images with the same weights, it is worth asking yourself how many will fit between two reloads. That comparison, between the time it takes for an inference and the time it takes for the capacitor to discharge, says much more than the word "analog" alone.

Related reading. A full spectrum of computing-in-memory technologies. Nature Electronics (2023).

Materials

Watching a working LED degrade under a microscope

[3] X. Li et al., “Multimodal electron microscopy of halide perovskite interfacial dynamics,” Nature, vol. 651, no. 8106, pp. 614–620, Mar. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10238-8.

Participating institutions
University of Cambridge (United Kingdom)

If an LED loses brightness while you have it on, from the outside it is difficult to know which part is failing. Perhaps the emitting material is still fine, but fewer charges are arriving from one of the contacts. To see where the problem begins, this team runs a perovskite LED inside the electron microscope and follows its composition and structure while having voltage applied.

The changes cluster near the charge transport layers. Lead-rich regions form and grains fragment, while part of the aluminium contact turns into an insulating compound. The interior of the emitting layer holds up better, directing attention to the interfaces.

What I would take away from this job if I were to make the next diode is where to look first. Changing the composition of the entire emitting layer may seem like the natural way out when brightness drops. However, if there is still intact crystal inside and an insulating barrier is already forming at the interface, trying another contact becomes a much more informative experiment.

Related reading. In Situ TEM Analysis of Organic–Inorganic Metal-Halide Perovskite Solar Cells under Electrical Bias. Nano Letters (2016).
