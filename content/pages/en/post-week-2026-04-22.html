Week of April 22, 2026

Photonics for listening to the spectrum, current for switching a magnet and gold between layers

When a short radio signal arrives, there is little time to find out which frequencies it contains. An optical circuit reads them out, while the other papers ask how to switch a small magnet without an external field and how to tell whether an ultraviolet detector still works after irradiation.

Photonics

A chip that scans the spectrum in near real time

[1] Y. Tao et al., “Integrated photonic ultrawideband real-time spectrum sensing for 6G wireless networks,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3666, Apr. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70389-0.

Participating institutions
City University of Hong Kong (Hong Kong, China)

To identify the frequencies present in a short radio signal, the team encodes it onto light travelling through a lithium niobate chip. A ring filter bank separates components so different frequencies are registered at different times.

The system analyses a 57.5 GHz band with latency below 110 ns. A test with two signals sharing the spectrum shows why that delay matters when interference changes over time.

Electronics

Switching a film’s magnetisation with a current

[2] F. Gao et al., “Polarity-tunable field-free room-temperature spin orbit torque switching via topological symmetry breaking in an all-vdW heterostructure for spin logic applications,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3826, Apr. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70590-1.

Participating institutions
Beihang University (China)

Switching a film’s perpendicular magnetization with a current poses a symmetry problem, since something has to favour one of the two possible directions. Here the team stacks Bi₂Te₃ and Fe₄GeTe₂ and uses the in-plane magnetic component to break that symmetry.

Switching works at room temperature, and changing the in-plane component reverses the response to a pulse. That gives the researchers a way to test different logic operations in the same element.

Materials

Gold atoms between the layers of an oxide

[3] X. Feng et al., “Engineering temperature- and radiation-resistant van der Waals oxide optoelectronics via heteroatom-intercalation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5691, Apr. 24, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72314-x.

Participating institutions
Wuhan University (China)

For an ultraviolet detector intended to withstand radiation, measuring its current before irradiation tells only half the story. Defects and interface changes can modify its behavior with use, so designing the material also involves asking what will happen when conditions deviate from those of the usual laboratory.

Xiaoqiang Feng and colleagues study a layered oxide, potassium niobate KNb₃O₈, in which they introduce gold atoms between the layers using a process assisted by ultraviolet light. The authors combine measurements and calculations to investigate how this incorporation alters defects, electronic structure and charge separation, before fabricating UV-sensitive devices.

The team tests two demands that a detector would face in space. It measures the response from about 10 to 573 K and, in a separate test, exposes the device to gamma radiation up to 200 kGy at 6 Gy per second. Both the dose and the dose rate matter when comparing radiation tolerance across detectors.
