Week of April 22, 2026

Photonics for listening to the spectrum, current for switching a magnet and gold between layers

If two radio signals cross briefly in frequency, an analyser that looks a moment later may miss the encounter. A lithium niobate chip tries to catch it in time. We'll also see a current reverse a film's magnetisation and an ultraviolet detector tested under conditions where getting any response at all is quite a feat.

Photonics

A chip that scans the spectrum in near real time

[1] Y. Tao et al., “Integrated photonic ultrawideband real-time spectrum sensing for 6G wireless networks,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3666, Apr. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70389-0.

Participating institutions
City University of Hong Kong (Hong Kong, China)

Imagine that you are interested in knowing which radio frequencies are busy right now and that one of the transmitters appears for just a moment. Sweeping the spectrum slowly would give you a very detailed measure of a moment that has already passed. This chip prints the radio signal on light and uses a bank of rings so that different frequencies leave their mark at different times of the output.

The system analyses a 57.5 GHz band with latency below 110 ns. A test with two signals sharing the spectrum shows why that delay matters when interference changes over time.

The useful thing about translating frequency into time is that, once the path is calibrated, you can recognize a component by when it arrives. The 57.5 GHz window tells how far apart two frequencies can be and still enter the analysis. Less than 110 ns indicates how soon the response can be available. To detect an emitter that appears and disappears, both pieces of information are needed.

Related reading. Spectrum Sensing: Analog (or Partially Analog) CMOS Real-Time Spectrum Sensing Techniques. IEEE Microwave Magazine (2019).

Electronics

Switching a film’s magnetisation with a current

[2] F. Gao et al., “Polarity-tunable field-free room-temperature spin orbit torque switching via topological symmetry breaking in an all-vdW heterostructure for spin logic applications,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3826, Apr. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70590-1.

Participating institutions
Beihang University (China)

To reverse with current a magnet that points perpendicular to the film there is an awkward question. If up and down are equally probable, who decides which way it will be after the pulse? An external field usually intervenes. In the Bi₂Te₃ and Fe₄GeTe₂ stack, the film itself has an in-plane magnetization component that breaks that symmetry.

Switching works at room temperature, and changing the in-plane component reverses the response to a pulse. That gives the researchers a way to test different logic operations in the same element.

You can think of it as a reference that is already incorporated into the device. If you prepare that component in the opposite direction and apply current again in the same direction, the preference will have changed even though the wires are still exactly where they were. That's the freedom the group takes advantage of to make reconfigurable logic at room temperature, a possibility quite different from using the junction solely to store a bit.

Related reading. Field-free deterministic switching of all–van der Waals spin-orbit torque system above room temperature. Science Advances (2024).

Materials

Gold atoms between the layers of an oxide

[3] X. Feng et al., “Engineering temperature- and radiation-resistant van der Waals oxide optoelectronics via heteroatom-intercalation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5691, Apr. 24, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72314-x.

Participating institutions
Wuhan University (China)

A photodetector intended to work under radiation has not proved itself just because it responds well on the first day. It would be necessary to illuminate it again after irradiating it and see if the same light continues to give a recognizable current. For this test, Xiaoqiang Feng and colleagues prepare a layered oxide of potassium niobate, KNb₃O₈, with gold atoms interspersed between layers.

In a thermal test they follow the response from about 10 to 573 K. In another they expose the device to gamma rays up to 200 kGy at a rate of 6 Gy per second. It is advisable to read them as two separate tests, because passing the temperature test and the radiation test separately is not equivalent to having passed them at the same time.

In both cases it matters what happens to the defects and to the interfaces, where it is decided whether a charge created by the light finally reaches the contact. That's where the authors look for the effect of inserting gold atoms between layers.

Related reading. Effect of temperature on photoresponse properties of solar-blind Schottky barrier diode photodetector based on single crystal Ga 2 O 3. Chinese Physics B (2019).
