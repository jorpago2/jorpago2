Week of October 21, 2026

A silicon modulator and two ways to use perovskites

Send symbols too close together down an optical link and the tail of one spills into the next. How much can the modulator itself put right? We'll also see why two memories made from the same film can work differently, and how to delay perovskite growth until you're ready to make the emitting pixel.

Photonics

A modulator that prepares the signal before sending it

[1] K. Kawahara et al., “Reduced-DSP 200G/λ low-voltage slow-light silicon modulator enabled by built-in EO equalizer,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10446, Oct. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78128-1.

When a modulator has barely finished responding to a symbol and the next one is already arriving, it is difficult for the receiver to decide where one ends and the other begins. We could entrust this cleaning to a digital processor, although it is worth asking if the signal could come out a little cleaner from the chip itself.

A team from Yokohama National University and the Tokyo Institute of Science, in Japan, proposes incorporating part of the equalization into the silicon modulator itself. To do this, it combines a slow light region, which intensifies the electro-optical interaction, with a design that compensates for part of the response limitations before the signal leaves the chip. In tests they communicate 140 gigabits per second with on-off modulation and 180 gigabits per second with PAM4 without digital signal processing.

When going up to 200 Gbit/s, help is once again needed, specifically an 11-coefficient digital equalizer in the receiver. 16 fJ per bit is also quoted for the modulator in an operating condition. If we want to compare two links end to end, it is advisable to follow the energy budget a little further than that component and add up what has been spent by the laser, the transmitter and the electronics that recover the symbols.

Related reading. High-speed, low-voltage, low-bit-energy silicon photonic crystal slow-light modulator with impedance-engineered distributed electrodes. Optica (2024).

Electronics

Two ways of remembering in the same perovskite

[2] I. Fernandez‐Guillen, O. E. Solis, P. F. Betancur, R. Abargues, and P. P. Boix, “Polarity‐Controlled Coexistence of Electrochemical Metallization and Valence Change Mechanisms in a Unified Metal Halide Perovskite Memristor,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78211, Oct. 26, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78211.

When connecting the meter we see two well separated resistances. Is there a tiny silver thread running through the film or have its ions moved around? In a halide perovskite both stories are plausible and the value read at the terminals alone will hardly tell us which one occurred.

A work with the participation of the Institute of Materials Science of the University of Valencia, the Institute of Chemical Technology, in Spain, and the Université Côte d’Azur, in France, compares two mechanisms within the same memristor architecture. By changing the polarity of the initial electroforming stage, the authors select a response associated with the formation of metallic filaments from the silver electrode or another governed by the movement of ions and changes in the active material. Comparison with inert electrodes helps to distinguish both contributions.

Choosing the mechanism has quite practical consequences. With silver filaments, the device switches at a lower voltage and exceeds ten thousand cycles. In ionic mode it can withstand fewer rewrites, although the resistance levels are better distinguished and the retention is around 7000 seconds. Depending on whether we want to update a piece of information over and over again or leave it waiting for hours to read, we could choose different ways to start the same movie.

Related reading. A Comprehensive Review of Electrochemical Metallization and Valence Change Mechanisms in Filamentary Resistive Switching of Halide Perovskite-Based Memory Devices. ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces (2025).

Materials

Giving the perovskite time to crystallize

[3] Y. Liu et al., “Regulating the crystallization of thermally evaporated perovskites for efficient near-infrared light-emitting diodes,” Nature Photonics, Oct. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-02036-5.

When evaporating a film for an LED we usually monitor how much material falls at each point. Just as decisive can be when it is allowed to crystallize. In Nanjing University, in China, and Linköping University, in Sweden, they incorporate two amide-type additives to delay the appearance of the perovskite during deposition and then stop its growth during annealing.

The 805nm LED reaches a maximum external quantum efficiency of 25%. To get an idea of how much it lasts, we have another measurement, the 23 hours it takes for the brightness to drop by half with a current density of 100 mA/cm².

The usefulness of controlling each pixel is seen when placing 759 and 805 nm emitters on the same substrate. This small difference allows us to see how the attenuation of light changes as it passes through tissue, which is what the oximetry test in the article takes advantage of.
