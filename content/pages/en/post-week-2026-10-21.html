Week of October 21, 2026

A silicon modulator and two ways to use perovskites

To transmit at 200 Gbit/s, a modulator corrects part of the distortion at the point where it writes the signal. The first voltage applied to a memory studied in Valencia determines how it will store data, while giving a perovskite crystal time to grow changes the efficiency of the resulting LED.

Photonics

A modulator that prepares the signal before sending it

[1] K. Kawahara et al., “Reduced-DSP 200G/λ low-voltage slow-light silicon modulator enabled by built-in EO equalizer,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10446, Oct. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78128-1.

As the interval between symbols shrinks, a modulator’s output can retain part of the response to the previous symbol, making the next one harder to read. A common solution is to correct the signal using digital processing, although this correction also has a cost that needs to be reduced.

A team from Yokohama National University and the Tokyo Institute of Science, in Japan, proposes incorporating part of the equalization into the silicon modulator itself. To do this, it combines a slow light region, which intensifies the electro-optical interaction, with a design that compensates for part of the response limitations before the signal leaves the chip. In tests they communicate 140 gigabits per second with on-off modulation and 180 gigabits per second with PAM4 without digital signal processing.

At 200 Gbit/s the receiver still needs an 11-tap digital equalizer to separate the symbols. The quoted 16 fJ per bit applies to the modulator at a particular operating point. An end-to-end energy budget would also have to include the laser and the electronics at both ends.

Electronics

Two ways of remembering in the same perovskite

[2] I. Fernandez‐Guillen, O. E. Solis, P. F. Betancur, R. Abargues, and P. P. Boix, “Polarity‐Controlled Coexistence of Electrochemical Metallization and Valence Change Mechanisms in a Unified Metal Halide Perovskite Memristor,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78211, Oct. 26, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78211.

Measuring two resistance values in the same film shows that two states can be distinguished, but leaves a physical question open. What has actually changed inside the device? In halide perovskites, where both charges and ions can move, different microscopic responses can produce electrical signals that at first glance look similar.

A work with the participation of the Institute of Materials Science of the University of Valencia, the Institute of Chemical Technology, in Spain, and the Université Côte d’Azur, in France, compares two mechanisms within the same memristor architecture. By changing the polarity of the initial electroforming stage, the authors select a response associated with the formation of metallic filaments from the silver electrode or another governed by the movement of ions and changes in the active material. Comparison with inert electrodes helps to distinguish both contributions.

With silver filaments the device withstands over ten thousand cycles and switches at lower voltage. With the ionic mechanism the resistance levels are further apart and persist for about 7000 seconds, although the device tolerates fewer cycles. A memory rewritten frequently and one left for hours between reads would ask different things of the same film.

Materials

Giving the perovskite time to crystallize

[3] Y. Liu et al., “Regulating the crystallization of thermally evaporated perovskites for efficient near-infrared light-emitting diodes,” Nature Photonics, Oct. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-02036-5.

Making emissive pixels by evaporation calls for separate control over how much material reaches the substrate and when it starts forming the crystals that will emit light. Researchers at Nanjing University in China and Linköping University in Sweden use two amide additives to delay formation of the perovskite phase during deposition and slow its growth during annealing.

The LEDs emitting at 805 nm reach a peak external quantum efficiency of 25%. The study also reports that brightness fell to half its initial value after 23 hours at 100 mA/cm², a measure that puts the efficiency figure in context. By producing emitters at two wavelengths on the same substrate, the team also demonstrates how the process can bring different pixels together.

Nanjing University explains the process and its applications to infrared emitters (in Chinese).
