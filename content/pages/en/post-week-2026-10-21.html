Week of October 21, 2026

A silicon modulator and two ways to use perovskites

A modulator can shape a signal so that rapid changes are easier to tell apart at the other end of the link. The two perovskite readings look at less visible processes, those that allow information to be stored in two ways and those that determine how the crystal in an LED forms.

Photonics

A modulator that prepares the signal before sending it

[1] K. Kawahara et al., “Reduced-DSP 200G/λ low-voltage slow-light silicon modulator enabled by built-in EO equalizer,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10446, Oct. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78128-1. Open access.

Increasing the speed of an optical link is not only about making light travel quickly, but also about ensuring that each change in intensity can be distinguished from the previous one when the modulator and electrical circuits begin to run out of bandwidth. A common solution is to correct the signal using digital processing, although this correction also has a cost that needs to be reduced.

A team from Yokohama National University and the Tokyo Institute of Science, in Japan, proposes incorporating part of the equalization into the silicon modulator itself. To do this, it combines a slow light region, which intensifies the electro-optical interaction, with a design that compensates for part of the response limitations before the signal leaves the chip. In tests they communicate 140 gigabits per second with on-off modulation and 180 gigabits per second with PAM4 without digital signal processing.

The result of 200 gigabits per second, which appears in the title of the article, was instead obtained with an 11-coefficient digital equalizer. The distinction matters, since the interest of the device is in reducing processing needs, not in claiming that they disappear under all conditions. Furthermore, the 16 femtojoules per bit correspond to a specific measurement condition, and not to the consumption of a complete link with its lasers and auxiliary circuits.

Electronics

Two ways of remembering in the same perovskite

[2] I. Fernandez‐Guillen, O. E. Solis, P. F. Betancur, R. Abargues, and P. P. Boix, “Polarity‐Controlled Coexistence of Electrochemical Metallization and Valence Change Mechanisms in a Unified Metal Halide Perovskite Memristor,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78211, Oct. 26, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78211. Open access.

If a device changes resistance when a voltage is applied, there is still an important question before using it as memory. What has actually changed inside the device? In halide perovskites, where both charges and ions can move, different microscopic responses can produce electrical signals that at first glance look similar.

A work with the participation of the Institute of Materials Science of the University of Valencia, the Institute of Chemical Technology, in Spain, and the Université Côte d’Azur, in France, compares two mechanisms within the same memristor architecture. By changing the polarity of the initial electroforming stage, the authors select a response associated with the formation of metallic filaments from the silver electrode or another governed by the movement of ions and changes in the active material. Comparison with inert electrodes helps to distinguish both contributions.

Each mechanism has its advantages and limits. The first withstood more than ten thousand cycles and operated with lower voltages under the conditions studied, while the second offered a greater difference between resistance states and a retention of about 7,000 seconds, at the cost of lower cycling resistance. Rather than presenting a memory ready to compete with commercial technologies, the study helps understand what is being measured and what trade-offs appear when choosing a form of switching.

Materials

Giving the perovskite time to crystallize

[3] Y. Liu et al., “Regulating the crystallization of thermally evaporated perovskites for efficient near-infrared light-emitting diodes,” Nat. Photon., Oct. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-02036-5. Open access.

Vacuum evaporation makes it possible to deposit perovskite pixels on a substrate, but LED performance depends on when and how the crystal forms. Researchers at Nanjing University in China and Linköping University in Sweden use two amide additives to delay formation of the perovskite phase during deposition and slow its growth during annealing.

The LEDs emitting at 805 nm reach a peak external quantum efficiency of 25%. The study also reports that brightness fell to half its initial value after 23 hours at 100 mA/cm², a measure that puts the efficiency figure in context. By producing emitters at two wavelengths on the same substrate, the team also demonstrates how the process can bring different pixels together.

Nanjing University explains the process and its applications to infrared emitters (in Chinese).
