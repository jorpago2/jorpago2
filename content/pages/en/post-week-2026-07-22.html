Week of July 22, 2026

A waveguide open to molecules, a self-regulating neuron and a monolayer laser

An optical measurement of a gas or liquid can only start once the sample reaches the light. A hollow waveguide with openings along its sides shows what happens when the molecules have a shorter route in. The other experiments unfold on very different timescales. A vanadium oxide neuron adjusts its response as pulses arrive, whereas an organic microlaser is put to the test by keeping it lasing for hours.

Photonics

A waveguide that lets molecules in through its walls

[1] W. Huang et al., “Photonic scaffolds as ultrahigh-openness on-chip hollow-core waveguides for quantum photonics and optofluidics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7563, Jul. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75873-1.

Participating institutions
Friedrich Schiller University Jena (Germany), University of Aveiro (Portugal), Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin (Germany)

If the only openings in a hollow waveguide are at its ends, a molecule entering from one end can take time to reach the region where its absorption will be measured. Windows along the wall shorten that journey, but how much of the wall can be removed before the light escapes?

Wenqin Huang and colleagues used three-dimensional nanoprinting to fabricate a structure of membranes and supports that leaves much of the cladding open. The light remains guided in a region dominated by air through a combination of antiresonant confinement and the periodic arrangement of the structure, while the openings provide access to the surrounding medium.

In the dye experiment the characteristic diffusion time was about 34 seconds inside the guide and 24 seconds in the liquid without a guide. The second measurement gives the first one a scale. Even with a structure in place to confine the light, the dye reaches the core almost as readily as it diffuses freely in this setup.

Related reading. 3D-Nanoprinted Antiresonant Hollow-Core Microgap Waveguide: An on-Chip Platform for Integrated Photonic Devices and Sensors. ACS Photonics (2022).

Electronics

A VO₂ neuron that adjusts its activity

[2] L. Zhang, T. Zhang, P. J. Tiw, X. Wu, Y. Su, and Y. Yang, “Homeostatic dendritic neuron based on co-integrated volatile and non-volatile memristors for neuromorphic processing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6918, Jul. 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73669-x.

When pulses arrive in quick succession, an artificial neuron can fire so often that one pattern becomes hard to distinguish from another. In this circuit, recent activity changes the response to subsequent pulses, while delays at the inputs help distinguish signals of similar strength arriving at different rates.

Researchers at Peking University in China are studying a system that combines vanadium dioxide devices with resistive memories and CMOS circuits. The resistance transition of VO₂ when exceeding a threshold voltage is used to build an artificial neuron, while other parts of the circuit allow programmable delays to be introduced in the signals reaching it.

The team tests devices and circuits and also uses network simulations to study tasks such as fault detection and speech recognition. Therefore, the classification results and energy figures must be interpreted with the conditions of that architecture and its models, without assuming that the entire system has been integrated on a single chip.

Related reading. DenRAM: neuromorphic dendritic architecture with RRAM for efficient temporal processing with delays. Nature Communications (2024).

Materials

An organic monolayer inside a microlaser

[3] L. Zhang et al., “Continuous-wave organic laser enabled by monolayer molecular crystal,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7061, Jul. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73984-3.

An organic laser can perform well during a brief pump pulse yet lose gain when the pump stays on. Long-lived molecular states called triplets accumulate as excitation continues, and the film also heats up. A geometry that encourages rapid emission therefore needs a way for heat to escape as well.

A team from Soochow University, Southeast University and Tsinghua University, in China, has placed a monolayer of organic molecular crystal between layers of hexagonal boron nitride and formed an optical microdisk with them. According to the authors, the organization of the molecules favors rapid emission, while the boron nitride acts as part of the cavity and helps manage heat.

Under continuous optical pumping the threshold, narrowing linewidth and build-up of coherence support lasing at room temperature. When the pump is kept on, the emission takes about eight hours to fall to half its initial value under the test conditions.

Related reading. Ultra‐Narrowband Photodetector with High Responsivity Enabled by Integrating Monolayer J‐Aggregate Organic Crystal with Graphene. Advanced Optical Materials (2021).
