Week of July 22, 2026

Open waveguides, computing in memory and a low-temperature photomemory

22–28 July 2026

A hollow waveguide usually takes in light at one end. This week we look at what becomes possible when light can enter from the side, then turn to a memristor array that helps distinguish languages and an indium selenide memory activated by light.

Photonics

A hollow waveguide with room to enter from the sides

[1] W. Huang et al., “Photonic scaffolds as ultrahigh-openness on-chip hollow-core waveguides for quantum photonics and optofluidics,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7563, Jul. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75873-1. Open access.

Participating institutions
Friedrich Schiller University Jena (Germany), University of Aveiro (Portugal), Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin (Germany)

When an optical waveguide carries light through a hollow region, gases or liquids can interact with it along the way. However, if the interior is accessible only at the ends, introducing and refreshing those substances can take time. Opening paths through the sides is therefore an attractive idea, provided the light remains confined.

Wenqin Huang and colleagues used three-dimensional nanoprinting to fabricate a structure of membranes and supports that leaves much of the cladding open. The light remains guided in a region dominated by air through a combination of antiresonant confinement and the periodic arrangement of the structure, while the openings provide access to the surrounding medium.

The team compares losses across several geometries and tests dye diffusion, spectroscopy and single-photon transmission. These experiments show what the platform might make possible, although each application will need its own balance of dimensions, losses and connections to other components. The appeal lies in bringing together two requirements that often compete in the same waveguide, confinement of light and access for the matter under study.

Electronics

Recognising languages with memristor arrays

[2] Y. Huang et al., “Hyperdimensional in-memory computing with analogue memristive crossbar arrays,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9162, Jul. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76067-5. Open access.

Participating institutions
University of Massachusetts Amherst (United States), University of Southern California (United States)

Identifying the language of a text offers one way to explore how an artificial intelligence task can be shared between an algorithm and the hardware available to it. On a resource-constrained device, it helps if that task does not require large amounts of data to move constantly between memory and processor.

Yi Huang and colleagues combine hyperdimensional computing with analogue memristor arrays. The former represents information using vectors with many dimensions, while the arrays perform operations between vectors and values stored in the conductances of their devices. The authors use this combination to encode features of the text before classifying it with a simple network.

In an experimental implementation using a system with several memristor cores, the study reports 95.24% accuracy in language identification and compares its resource use with that of other implementations. As with any comparison of architectures, the result depends on the task, the dataset and how supporting circuits are counted. Beyond the single figure, the work shows how a mathematical representation can be chosen with the variability and available states of a physical device in mind.

Materials

Light activates an indium selenide memory

[3] J. Wang et al., “Photo-activated digital resistive switching in a cryogenic photomemory,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9167, Jul. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76121-2. Open access.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China), Jilin University (China), Xidian University (China)

When a semiconductor device is cooled, some of the mechanisms that change its resistance no longer behave as they do at room temperature. It is therefore worth asking what happens when light and electrical voltage are combined in materials that might be integrated into cryogenic electronics.

Jiarui Wang and colleagues observed light-activated resistive switching in lateral α-In₂Se₃ devices. After illumination, an electrical signal can take the sample from a high-resistance to a low-resistance state, and the authors investigate how electrical pulses, additional illumination and an electrostatic gate can change the threshold. Their interpretation gives a central role to material defects and their evolution near the contacts.

Some measurements were made at 2 kelvin, and the behaviour changes as temperature rises, so this should not be presented as a general-purpose optical memory at room temperature. The study also observes variation between devices and cycles. Its interest lies in showing that light can prepare the electrical response of a structure for an extended period, raising new questions for optoelectronics and memories that already need to operate at very low temperatures.
