Week of April 8, 2026

Computing with light, switching functions with voltage and bending a compound eye

A chip with nine optical inputs and three outputs still needs plenty of help before it can recognise an image. This week's other papers investigate an interface that can do the jobs of three circuit elements, and a compound eye whose fragile pixels survive because the bridges between them can bend.

Photonics

An optical processor that connects to a neural network

[1] L. Meyer et al., “Deep neural network inference on an integrated, reconfigurable photonic tensor processor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3396, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71599-2.

A neural network in the computer has a table of weights. There are optical ports and voltage-responsive components on the chip. If you want to run the same network in both, someone has to do the translation between those two worlds, load the data, and collect the output. Meyer and colleagues at Heidelberg University, in Germany, also take care of that part that is usually left out of the chip photo.

The chip, built on imec's silicon photonics platform, combines nine inputs and three outputs in an array that accumulates weighted signals. The different wavelengths come from an optical microcomb, while electroabsorption modulators and photodiodes allow signals to be introduced and read out. The researchers evaluated classification networks with the system, obtaining accuracies of 98.1% on MNIST and 72.0% on CIFAR-10.

With the set they recognize MNIST digits with 98.1% correctness and obtain 72.0% in CIFAR-10. It is a good idea to save the names of the image sets along with the percentages. The difference says something about how hard it is for the network to classify more varied scenes. To attribute it to a specific optical piece, the errors would have to be followed through the rest of the system.

Electronics

A device that changes function as the voltage changes

[2] S. Pradhan et al., “Oxide interface-based polymorphic electronic devices for neuromorphic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3406, Apr. 10, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71642-2.

To draw a circuit we usually choose a transistor from one drawer and a capacitor from another. In this interface of LaAlO₃ and SrTiO₃, however, the interesting question is what role the same device will play when we change the voltage. Pradhan and colleagues at Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg in Germany and Universidade Federal de São Carlos in Brazil track both the current and the charge that is stored when remeasuring.

The team shows that the same type of device can behave like a transistor, memristor or memcapacitor depending on the applied bias. From these functions the team built circuits for logic operations with storage and for a demonstration of digit recognition based on reservoir computing. The possibility of changing functions during operation allows us to think about circuits that do not need to assign a different physical part to each task from the beginning.

To see the difference, imagine two readings with the same final voltage. If the current or load also depends on the route you took to get there, the device retains information from the previous operation. That dependency is precisely what is important if we want to use the interface as memory. To use it like a regular transistor we would look, instead, at how much immediate control the gate gives over the channel.

Related reading. Boolean Logic Computing Based on Neuromorphic Transistor. Advanced Functional Materials (2023).

Materials

Bending a fragile film to assemble a compound eye

[3] K. Zhang et al., “Strain-transformative integration of perovskite thin-film optoelectronics for in-plane multiaxial stretchable and 3D curvy artificial compound eye arrays,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 54, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00552-6.

Participating institutions
Beijing Institute of Technology (China)

Try covering a curved surface with a sheet of paper without making any cuts. A wrinkle will appear somewhere. Something more uncomfortable happens to a semiconductor film, because it can crack long before the elastic support underneath reaches its limit. The authors leave the detectors on islands connected by bridges to decide where they accept the warp.

They use the design to test perovskite photodetector arrays that stretch in several directions. They also assemble 185 pixels on a curved surface. The light-sensitive regions retain their response as the whole assembly changes shape.

To see why the layout works, look at where it gives way. When a bridge gives way, the distance between two pixels can change even though it barely changes the shape of the semiconductor that collects the light. To build something like a compound eye, where there is curvature in more than one direction, it is much more useful to be able to choose where the sheet bends than to make the material it detects flexible at all costs.

Related reading. Stretchable Thin-Film Transistors Based on Wrinkled Graphene and MoS 2 Heterostructures. Nano Letters (2024).
