Week of April 8, 2026

Computing with light, switching functions with voltage and bending a compound eye

Moving beyond a single device raises practical questions, from loading neural network weights onto an optical chip to stretching a circuit without tearing its semiconductor. An interface between two oxides offers another option by allowing one element to serve several circuit functions.

Photonics

An optical processor that connects to a neural network

[1] L. Meyer et al., “Deep neural network inference on an integrated, reconfigurable photonic tensor processor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3396, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71599-2.

Running a neural network written in software on a photonic chip means translating its weights into physical settings and bringing the data to the optical inputs. It also needs to receive data, adjust its parameters and communicate with the software used to describe neural networks. Meyer and colleagues address that broader set of tasks by integrating a reconfigurable photonic circuit into a system with input and output electronics and a connection to PyTorch.

The chip, built on imec's silicon photonics platform, combines nine inputs and three outputs in an array that accumulates weighted signals. The different wavelengths come from an optical microcomb, while electroabsorption modulators and photodiodes allow signals to be introduced and read out. The researchers evaluated classification networks with the system, obtaining accuracies of 98.1% on MNIST and 72.0% on CIFAR-10.

The demonstration is interesting for the effort to connect optical computing to infrastructure familiar to people working in machine learning. Even so, the system includes amplifiers, controllers and electronic conversion, so the results should not be mistaken for a measure of overall energy consumption attributable to the optical core alone. Calibration and noise also matter, especially when moving to more demanding tasks.

The collaboration includes Heidelberg University, Germany, alongside researchers from Volkswagen and Enlightra.

Electronics

A device that changes function as the voltage changes

[2] S. Pradhan et al., “Oxide interface-based polymorphic electronic devices for neuromorphic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3406, Apr. 10, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71642-2.

Changing the voltage at an oxide interface can alter both the current that flows through it and the charge it can store. Pradhan and colleagues study a heterostructure of LaAlO₃ and SrTiO₃, where a very thin conducting region forms at the interface and responds to voltages applied through lateral gates.

The team shows that the same type of device can behave like a transistor, memristor or memcapacitor depending on the applied bias. From these functions the team built circuits for logic operations with storage and for a demonstration of digit recognition based on reservoir computing. The possibility of changing functions during operation allows us to think about circuits that do not need to assign a different physical part to each task from the beginning.

What happens to the signal depends on the bias applied to the side gates. In one configuration the circuit uses its charge history to recognise digits. In another it performs a logic operation and stores the result in the same circuit.

The study brings together the Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg, in Germany, and the Universidade Federal de São Carlos, in Brazil.

Materials

Bending a fragile film to assemble a compound eye

[3] K. Zhang et al., “Strain-transformative integration of perovskite thin-film optoelectronics for in-plane multiaxial stretchable and 3D curvy artificial compound eye arrays,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 54, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00552-6.

Participating institutions
Beijing Institute of Technology (China)

Even on an elastic support, a semiconductor film can crack well before the support reaches its stretching limit. The researchers arrange the elements as islands and bridges, so part of that pull becomes a much smaller bending strain in the active regions.

They use the design to test perovskite photodetector arrays that stretch in several directions. They also assemble 185 pixels on a curved surface. The light-sensitive regions retain their response as the whole assembly changes shape.

The curvature of the array and the strain in each detector are different quantities. When a flat array is fitted over a dome, yielding bridges allow the pixel spacing to change while the semiconductor islands largely retain their shape. The compound eye provides a way to put that distinction to the test in two directions at once.
