Week of April 8, 2026

One photonic processor, three functions in one oxide and one low-loss memory

Photonics

An optical processor that connects to a neural network

[1] L. Meyer et al., “Deep neural network inference on an integrated, reconfigurable photonic tensor processor,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3396, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71599-2.

The collaboration includes Heidelberg University, Germany, alongside researchers from Volkswagen and Enlightra.

For a photonic processor to be useful beyond the optics lab, a chip that multiplies and adds light signals is not enough. It also needs to receive data, adjust its parameters and communicate with the software used to describe neural networks. Meyer and colleagues address that broader set of tasks by integrating a reconfigurable photonic circuit into a system with input and output electronics and a connection to PyTorch.

The chip, built on imec's silicon photonics platform, combines nine inputs and three outputs in an array that accumulates weighted signals. The different wavelengths come from an optical microcomb, while electroabsorption modulators and photodiodes allow signals to be introduced and read out. The researchers evaluated classification networks with the system, obtaining accuracies of 98.1% on MNIST and 72.0% on CIFAR-10.

The demonstration is interesting for the effort to connect optical computing to infrastructure familiar to people working in machine learning. Even so, the system includes amplifiers, controllers and electronic conversion, so the results should not be mistaken for a measure of overall energy consumption attributable to the optical core alone. Calibration and noise also matter, especially when moving to more demanding tasks.

Electronics

A device that changes function as the voltage changes

[2] S. Pradhan et al., “Oxide interface-based polymorphic electronic devices for neuromorphic computing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3406, Apr. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71642-2.

The study brings together the Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg, in Germany, and the Universidade Federal de São Carlos, in Brazil.

Transistors, memristors and capacitors with memory play different roles in a circuit, but their functions can converge at complex oxide interfaces. Pradhan and colleagues study a heterostructure of LaAlO₃ and SrTiO₃, where a very thin conducting region forms at the interface and responds to voltages applied through lateral gates.

The team shows that the same type of device can behave like a transistor, memristor or memcapacitor depending on the applied bias. From these functions the team built circuits for logic operations with storage and for a demonstration of digit recognition based on reservoir computing. The possibility of changing functions during operation allows us to think about circuits that do not need to assign a different physical part to each task from the beginning.

However, a multifunction device must also be reproducible and compatible with the processes necessary to build larger circuits. That is why the versatility observed at room temperature is a starting point to explore architectures, while its extension to dense systems will require studying manufacturing, variability and connections with the rest of the electronics.

Materials

Change the path of light without absorbing too much of it

[3] X. Shen, R. Chu, D. Xu, Y. Gao, W. Zhou, and W. Zhang, “Low-loss phase-change material-based programmable mode converter for photonic computing,” NPG Asia Mater, vol. 18, no. 1, Art. no. 22, Apr. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41427-026-00645-8.

The authors work at Xi’an Jiaotong University, in China.

Phase change materials offer a way to preserve an optical setting after removing the stimulus that produced it. However, a large difference between the refractive indices of their phases is of little use if one of them absorbs too much light, especially when many cells need to be connected in a circuit. Shen and colleagues study this trade-off for antimony selenide, Sb₂Se₃, and compare it with that of more absorbing chalcogenide materials.

The study combines atomic structure calculations with electromagnetic simulations to design a mode converter integrated into a waveguide. The proposal uses the index contrast between the amorphous and crystalline phases to redistribute light, and calculations suggest that different written patterns could provide up to 32 levels. By relying on the phase difference without requiring high absorption, the authors propose a possible route towards larger photonic arrays.

It should be emphasized that the architecture and its features are presented as simulation results, although the authors use optical properties obtained from previous measurements. Neither a physical 128-by-128-cell array nor the repeatability of those levels is demonstrated here. Writing, cycling endurance and the losses of a fabricated device remain to be tested experimentally.
