Week of January 21, 2026

Measuring a spectrum, orienting a monolayer and making gold porous

With a spectrometer on a detector, measuring light takes less space. In the other two papers, working on a smaller scale changes what the material itself does, whether by aligning a MoS₂ monolayer or letting pores alter the optical response of gold.

Photonics

A spectrometer that fits on a detector

[1] J. Wu et al., “Broadband and high-resolution snapshot spectroscopy with high-index transition metal dichalcogenides,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1955, Jan. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68685-w.

A conventional spectrometer separates light before measuring it, usually requiring an optical path and components that take up space. Wu and colleagues instead divide the task between a detector array and thin films that modify the light reaching each pixel in a different way. An algorithm then combines the signals to reconstruct the original spectrum.

The choice of materials matters here. Transition-metal dichalcogenides can have a high refractive index while transmitting light across a broad range of wavelengths. By varying the thickness of the films, the team obtains different spectral responses that act as a kind of fingerprint for the subsequent calculation. In their demonstration, InGaAs photodetectors operate in the short-wave infrared, a useful region for identifying substances from their absorption bands.

The participants include researchers from Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Westlake University, in China. The authors report a spectral resolution of 1 nm and wavelength accuracy of 0.02 nm under the conditions studied. The approach is attractive for compact sensors, although reconstruction depends on calibration and on the responses of the different channels remaining sufficiently distinct as measurement conditions change.

Electronics

A layer of MoS₂ finds its orientation

[2] Y. Sakuma et al., “Self-aligned and self-limiting van der Waals epitaxy of monolayer MoS2 for scalable 2D electronics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 602, Jan. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68320-8.

Making electronics from semiconductors only a few layers thick requires more than simply growing the material on a substrate. If the small regions that appear at the outset have different orientations, they can leave boundaries as they join, hindering the movement of charge carriers. This makes the self-alignment observed by Sakuma and colleagues during the growth of a molybdenum disulfide monolayer on sapphire particularly interesting.

The team, which includes researchers from the National Institute for Materials Science and the University of Tokyo, both in Japan, uses chemical vapour deposition with organometallic precursors. Although domains with several orientations initially appear, the authors observe that some reorient as they join and eventually form a film with a predominant crystal direction. The precursor also helps limit growth to a single layer.

The devices reach a carrier mobility of 66 cm²/(V·s) at room temperature. What is unusual about the growth process is when the crystal acquires its orientation. Individual domains can start out rotated and still line up as they grow into their neighbours.

Materials

When pores change gold's response to light

[3] T. Tapani et al., “Morphology-modified contributions of electronic transitions to the optical response of plasmonic nanoporous gold metamaterial,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 829, Jan. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68506-0.

Participating institutions, Umeå University in Sweden; Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, University of Milano-Bicocca, CNR NANOTEC, University of Salento, University of Rome Tor Vergata and University of Modena and Reggio Emilia in Italy.

Gold often appears in photonics because of the ease with which its electrons respond to light, but that response also depends on the shape the metal takes. If a film contains a network of nanometric holes, the energy it absorbs is distributed among less metal and the distribution of its electrons can change. Tapani and their collaborators study this effect by comparing two films about 26 nanometers thick, one compact and the other porous, which they excite with ultrashort pulses before measuring how the transmitted light varies.

At the same incident energy per unit area, the signal from the porous film recovered more slowly. When measured at 540 nanometers, fitting the data gave a relaxation time of about 3.1 picoseconds for porous gold, compared to 1 picosecond for compact gold. Furthermore, the negative transmission signal extended approximately between 540 and 670 nanometers in the porous sample, while in the compact one it appeared mainly below 570 nanometers.

To interpret this difference, the team used a model that estimates a transient electronic temperature of about 3200 K in porous gold and about 800 K in compact gold. This is not a direct measurement of the temperature of the entire film. According to the authors' interpretation, the redistribution of electrons associated with this higher temperature leaves available states that facilitate transitions between bands at lower energies, which helps explain the response observed in a broader range of wavelengths.

The result suggests that porosity may be a useful variable to design the interaction between light and matter, although the work has not yet demonstrated a photodetector or a catalytic process based on these films. It would be interesting to systematically study samples with different pore sizes and metal fractions to find out to what extent that response can be tuned.
