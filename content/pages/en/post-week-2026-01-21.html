Materials that guide light and current

Week of 21–27 January 2026

A spectrometer that fits on a detector

[1] J. Wu et al., “Broadband and high-resolution snapshot spectroscopy with high-index transition metal dichalcogenides,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1955, Jan. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68685-w. Date record Open access.

A conventional spectrometer separates light before measuring it, usually requiring an optical path and components that take up space. Wu and colleagues instead divide the task between a detector array and thin films that modify the light reaching each pixel in a different way. An algorithm then combines the signals to reconstruct the original spectrum.

The choice of materials matters here. Transition-metal dichalcogenides can have a high refractive index while transmitting light across a broad range of wavelengths. By varying the thickness of the films, the team obtains different spectral responses that act as a kind of fingerprint for the subsequent calculation. In their demonstration, InGaAs photodetectors operate in the short-wave infrared, a useful region for identifying substances from their absorption bands.

The participants include researchers from Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Westlake University, in China. The authors report a spectral resolution of 1 nm and wavelength accuracy of 0.02 nm under the conditions studied. The approach is attractive for compact sensors, although reconstruction depends on calibration and on the responses of the different channels remaining sufficiently distinct as measurement conditions change.

A layer of MoS₂ finds its orientation

[2] Y. Sakuma et al., “Self-aligned and self-limiting van der Waals epitaxy of monolayer MoS2 for scalable 2D electronics,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 602, Jan. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68320-8. Date in Crossref Open access.

Making electronics from semiconductors only a few layers thick requires more than simply growing the material on a substrate. If the small regions that appear at the outset have different orientations, they can leave boundaries as they join, hindering the movement of charge carriers. This makes the self-alignment observed by Sakuma and colleagues during the growth of a molybdenum disulfide monolayer on sapphire particularly interesting.

The team, which includes researchers from the National Institute for Materials Science and the University of Tokyo, both in Japan, uses chemical vapour deposition with organometallic precursors. Although domains with several orientations initially appear, the authors observe that some reorient as they join and eventually form a film with a predominant crystal direction. The precursor also helps limit growth to a single layer.

To assess electronic quality, the team fabricated devices and measured a mobility of around 66 cm²/Vs at room temperature. This does not mean that industrial manufacture of two-dimensional transistors has been fully solved, but it does offer a concrete way to address one of its difficulties, namely forming uniform films that can move from the laboratory to larger surfaces.

What happens when two lithium niobate crystals are twisted

[3] A. Rogers et al., “Polar discontinuities, emergent conductivity, and critical twist-angle-dependent behaviour at wafer-bonded ferroelectric interfaces,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1842, Jan. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68553-7. Date record Open access.

When two crystals are joined with a small relative twist, their boundary can acquire properties that neither crystal has on its own. Rogers and colleagues investigate this possibility in lithium niobate, a material familiar in photonics for its electro-optic properties, although here the focus is on the electrical behaviour of the interface.

The researchers join two ferroelectric crystals by thermocompression and examine the effects of their polarization directions and the angle between their lattices. Localized conductivity appears at many of the interfaces studied, associated with the discontinuity in polarization. At certain angles, however, they also observe a local rearrangement of dipoles, suggesting that the twist changes how the system compensates accumulated charge.

The study includes Queen’s University Belfast, in the United Kingdom, TU Dresden, in Germany, and the Complutense University of Madrid, in Spain, among other institutions. The authors propose an explanation connected to the electronic structure of the twisted interfaces, although that interpretation needs further work. The possibility of changing a boundary through the joining angle is particularly intriguing in a material already used to build optical waveguides and circuits.
