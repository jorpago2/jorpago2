Week of January 21, 2026

Measuring a spectrum, orienting a monolayer and making gold porous

A benchtop spectrometer has room to spread colours out. What if your entire instrument had to fit on the detector? Geometry matters in the other two papers as well, whether you're trying to grow two islands of MoS₂ in the same orientation or wondering what happens to light when you punch holes in gold.

Photonics

A spectrometer that fits on a detector

[1] J. Wu et al., “Broadband and high-resolution snapshot spectroscopy with high-index transition metal dichalcogenides,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1955, Jan. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68685-w.

Think about the space a diffraction grating needs. To distinguish two colours you have to let them travel a certain distance until they land in different places. Wu and colleagues wonder if they could skip that route and do the analysis on top of the detector. Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Westlake University, in China, participate.

Instead of assigning a location to each wavelength, they use transition metal chalcogenide structures whose optical response changes with colour. Each reading contains a different mixture and, with the calibrated responses, the spectrum that came in can be reconstructed. There you can understand why both the choice of material and the subsequent calculation are important.

The two numbers reported in the paper answer different questions. The resolution of 1 nm tells what separation between spectral features can be distinguished. The accuracy of 0.02 nm indicates how well a wavelength falls on the scale. Being able to place a line with that precision is not enough to resolve two lines separated only by that distance.

Related reading. Broadband perovskite quantum dot spectrometer beyond human visual resolution. Light: Science & Applications (2020).

Electronics

A layer of MoS₂ finds its orientation

[2] Y. Sakuma et al., “Self-aligned and self-limiting van der Waals epitaxy of monolayer MoS2 for scalable 2D electronics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 602, Jan. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68320-8.

The difficulty of growing a monolayer on sapphire is clearly seen when two MoS₂ islands that were born far from each other finally touch. If they arrive with different orientations, they can leave a border right where the loads will later have to circulate. Sakuma and colleagues follow the growth and find that the initial orientation does not always have the last word.

The team, which includes researchers from the National Institute for Materials Science and the University of Tokyo, both in Japan, uses chemical vapour deposition with organometallic precursors. Although domains with several orientations initially appear, the authors observe that some reorient as they join and eventually form a film with a predominant crystal direction. The precursor also helps limit growth to a single layer.

When they make transistors on the film, the mobility reaches 66 cm²/(V·s) at room temperature. For me, the detail that explains why it is worth watching how it grows is one step before that measurement. Inspecting only the finished crystal would show a dominant orientation, but would miss the moment when some islands turn when they meet. They are two very different growth stories that could leave a similar final appearance.

Related reading. Multi-terminal transport measurements of MoS2 using a van der Waals heterostructure device platform. Nature Nanotechnology (2015).

Materials

When pores change gold's response to light

[3] T. Tapani et al., “Morphology-modified contributions of electronic transitions to the optical response of plasmonic nanoporous gold metamaterial,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 829, Jan. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68506-0.

Drill enough holes in a gold film and a pulse of light no longer finds the same amount of metal, even though the film is still the same thickness. Tapani and colleagues compare two samples of about 26 nm, one porous and the other compact, and observe how long it takes for transmission to recover after the pulse.

At 540 nm, the fit gives 3.1 ps for the porous gold and 1 ps for the compact one. Furthermore, in the former the negative signal extends from 540 to 670 nm, while in the latter it appears mainly below 570 nm. Was porous gold hotter? According to the model, the electrons transiently reach about 3200 K compared to about 800 K in the compact. It would be a mistake to read those numbers as measured temperatures of the entire film.

Umeå University, in Sweden, shares the work with Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, University of Milano-Bicocca, CNR NANOTEC, University of Salento, University of Rome Tor Vergata and University of Modena and Reggio Emilia, in Italy. University of Rome Tor Vergata explains the study in an English-language news article.

Related reading. Morphological control and plasmonic tuning of nanoporous gold disks by surface modifications. Journal of Materials Chemistry C (2015).
