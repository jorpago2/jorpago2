A liquid-crystal shutter and two ways to use a magnet

Place a liquid crystal in front of quantum dots and you have a shutter that adjusts their brightness without touching the emitters. We'll then meet two very different uses for a magnet, letting it fluctuate in search of a solution and tuning spin waves electrically in a flake just three layers thick.

Photonics

A liquid crystal that controls how much light reaches quantum dots

[1] A. Ramadas, M. D. Patekari, S. H. Lee, and M. Kim, “Electrically tunable UV–visible modulation and voltage-controlled quantum dot emission via polymer network liquid crystals,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 74, Jun. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00578-w.

Participating institutions
Jeonbuk National University (South Korea)

If you want to lower the brightness of quantum dots, one option is to intervene before they become excited. The team places a liquid crystal film between the source and the dots. Without a difference in voltage across the electrodes, it allows a good part of the light to pass through. When voltage is applied, the molecules are reoriented and the beam is dispersed, so that less light reaches the emitters waiting behind.

For the green and red dots, centred near 530 and 630 nm, the measured modulation depths are about 92 to 97%. Switching is reversible and takes milliseconds. Since the voltage acts on the liquid crystal in front of the dots, the same shutter can be tested with two different emitter compositions without injecting current into either.

The detail I like about this assembly is that you can change the dots without having to redesign its power supply. The colour is decided by what dots you have placed behind them, while the shutter regulates the excitation they receive. If you wanted to scale it to many pixels, you would also want to measure how much of the scattered light ends up in a neighbouring pixel instead of being lost along the way.

Electronics

An Ising machine with memory and a fluctuating magnet

[2] M. A. Iftakher et al., “Intrinsic annealing in a hybrid memristor-magnetic tunnel junction Ising machine,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5246, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71844-8.

If to improve a solution you have to first go through a worse one, a system that only accepts improvements can get stuck. Ising machines take advantage of fluctuations to explore several options at first and then narrow the margin of maneuver. Iftakher and colleagues wonder if a tiny magnet can provide this random component along with a memory that preserves the relationships between variables.

Iftakher and his collaborators combine a hafnium oxide memristor array, which stores the couplings, with a stochastic magnetic tunnel junction, which provides the probabilistic response. Increasing the reading voltage also changes the bias of the junction, so that the same signal used to read the array also helps reduce randomness gradually.

In your circuit, reading memory and adjusting how much the magnetic junction fluctuates are related. Increasing the reading voltage changes the switching probability of the magnet. You can "cool" the model in this way without having lowered the temperature of the laboratory a single degree. Université Paris-Saclay and Université Grenoble Alpes, in France, participate.

Materials

Tuning spin waves in a three-layer magnet

[3] N. Stetzuhn et al., “Tunable magnons in a dual-gated 2D antiferromagnet,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5262, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74067-z.

Can a magnet be tuned with a gate voltage similar to how an instrument is tuned? In CrSBr, the frequency that you want to move corresponds to a collective oscillation of the spins. With only three layers, it matters how the middle one relates to the outer two, and placing a gate on each side allows you to try different combinations of voltages.

To answer it, they placed a sample of three layers between two gates, the combination of which allows the carrier density and the perpendicular electric field to vary. They excited the modes with laser pulses and measured their time-resolved optical response, finding frequency shifts on the order of 2 GHz. Furthermore, the two modes responded differently to the field, a behavior they interpreted using a model that distinguishes the individual layers.

The measurements are made at 10 K. At that temperature the team can follow two modes optically and compare how each one changes when the field is applied, an important detail to contrast the model layer by layer. The Freie Universität Berlin, in Germany, the Institute of Molecular Science of the University of Valencia, in Spain, and the Delft University of Technology, in the Netherlands, participate. If you intend to do the same at room temperature, you would have to check again what magnetic order is available to sustain those waves.
