Week of June 10, 2026

Optical chaos, GaN transistors and adjustable magnons

Photonics

An optical comb to generate many chaotic signals

[1] Z. Pan et al., “Large-scale integrated optoelectronic chaos for machine learning acceleration,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7488, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73440-2.

Among the participating institutions is Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics, in China.

Some machine learning tasks require signals that change in complex ways and allow many states to be explored. Optoelectronic chaos can provide such signals at high speed, although generating multiple simultaneous channels without each losing bandwidth is a practical difficulty. Pan and colleagues propose a source that combines an optical microcomb with a nonlinear optoelectronic cavity.

The different lines of the comb provide light channels that feed the system, and the equipment obtains parallel chaotic signals with a bandwidth of 25 GHz per channel measured at six decibels. From them it demonstrates random bit generation and learning tasks, with a reported aggregate rate of 32.768 terabits per second. The figure describes the joint output of the channels under the conditions of the experiment, and not the speed of a single channel.

The proposal is interesting because it takes advantage of wavelength parallelism to create a physical resource useful in computing, although the comb and cavity are part of an assembly that requires additional optical and electronic components. That is why the comparison of times for specific tasks should be read together with the description of the complete system and the reference platforms chosen by the authors.

Electronics

Tuning a gallium nitride transistor from its atomic structure

[2] Y. Shi et al., “Atomically confined insertion for 2D strain and polarization engineered GaN electronics,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7437, Jun. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74233-3.

The work includes researchers from University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China.

High mobility transistors based on gallium nitride are used when it is important to combine speed with power management. A design issue is to ensure that the device remains off when no voltage is applied to the gate, without sacrificing the current it can carry when turned on. Shi and colleagues study how to intervene in this response through the distribution of magnesium within the heterostructure.

Their procedure introduces confined magnesium layers at the atomic scale, which modify the local deformation and polarity of the structure. The authors attribute an increase in the effective hole concentration to the resulting polarization fields. In the transistor studied, the threshold voltage went from 1.5 to 4.3 volts with respect to the comparison device, while some common current and transconductance losses were moderated.

The work thus links a modification of a few layers with a parameter that matters when designing power circuits. The comparison concerns the structures and conditions investigated by the team, so the long-term behavior and industrial repeatability of such a process will require further testing before extrapolating the results.

Materials

Tuning spin waves in a three-layer magnet

[3] N. Stetzuhn et al., “Tunable magnons in a dual-gated 2D antiferromagnet,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5262, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74067-z.

The Freie Universität Berlin, in Germany, the Institute of Molecular Science of the University of Valencia, in Spain, and the Delft University of Technology, in the Netherlands, along with other centers, participate.

Collective spin waves, or magnons, offer another way to transport and manipulate information in magnetic materials. Stetzuhn and colleagues work with CrSBr, a laminar antiferromagnet whose magnetic excitations can be observed optically thanks to its interaction with other excitations of the material. The question is whether those frequencies can be tuned with electric gates in a few-layer device.

To answer it, they placed a sample of three layers between two gates, the combination of which allows the carrier density and the perpendicular electric field to vary. They excited the modes with laser pulses and measured their time-resolved optical response, finding frequency shifts on the order of 2 GHz. Furthermore, the two modes responded differently to the field, a behavior they interpreted using a model that distinguishes the individual layers.

The study connects electrical control with an optical reading of magnetic dynamics and, in addition, has the participation of the University of Valencia. Their measurements were made at 10 kelvin, so the demonstration does not establish room temperature operation. The possibility of designing magnonic devices closer to applications will depend, among other things, on how to preserve and control that response under less demanding conditions.
