A liquid-crystal shutter and two ways to use a magnet

In a quantum dot display, controlling how much light reaches the emitters gives another way to set the brightness. Voltage serves two quite different purposes in the other papers, adjusting fluctuations in an Ising circuit and tuning a spin wave in a three-layer magnet.

Photonics

A liquid crystal that controls how much light reaches quantum dots

[1] A. Ramadas, M. D. Patekari, S. H. Lee, and M. Kim, “Electrically tunable UV–visible modulation and voltage-controlled quantum dot emission via polymer network liquid crystals,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 74, Jun. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00578-w.

Participating institutions
Jeonbuk National University (South Korea)

While the electrodes are at the same voltage, the liquid crystal film is relatively transparent to the light that excites the quantum dots. A voltage reorients its molecules, creating refractive-index differences that scatter light in different directions.

The team places green- and red-emitting quantum dots behind the film. It can then dim their emission by adjusting how much light excites them. The mechanism controls the pump reaching the dots, while the emitting material determines the colour.

For the green and red dots, centred near 530 and 630 nm, the measured modulation depths are about 92 to 97%. Switching is reversible and takes milliseconds. Since the voltage acts on the liquid crystal in front of the dots, the same shutter can be tested with two different emitter compositions without injecting current into either.

Electronics

An Ising machine with memory and a fluctuating magnet

[2] M. A. Iftakher et al., “Intrinsic annealing in a hybrid memristor-magnetic tunnel junction Ising machine,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5246, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71844-8.

Assigning a set of elements to two groups can be difficult when each pair favours or penalises a different choice. Ising machines transfer that idea to a physical system with states equivalent to spins and connections between them. To prevent the search from becoming trapped too soon in a local solution, fluctuations can be introduced at the beginning and reduced later, much like a gradual cooling process.

Iftakher and his collaborators combine a hafnium oxide memristor array, which stores the couplings, with a stochastic magnetic tunnel junction, which provides the probabilistic response. Increasing the reading voltage also changes the bias of the junction, so that the same signal used to read the array also helps reduce randomness gradually.

The read voltage does a second job here. As well as interrogating the memory array, it biases the magnetic junctions and changes their probability of switching. Raising that voltage amounts to cooling the Ising model while the laboratory stays at the same physical temperature.

The team includes researchers from Université Paris-Saclay and Université Grenoble Alpes, in France.

Materials

Tuning spin waves in a three-layer magnet

[3] N. Stetzuhn et al., “Tunable magnons in a dual-gated 2D antiferromagnet,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5262, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74067-z.

In a magnet only a few layers thick, a spin wave can involve several layers, making its frequency sensitive to how they interact. Stetzuhn and colleagues work with CrSBr, a laminar antiferromagnet whose magnetic excitations can be observed optically thanks to its interaction with other excitations of the material. The question is whether those frequencies can be tuned with electric gates in a few-layer device.

To answer it, they placed a sample of three layers between two gates, the combination of which allows the carrier density and the perpendicular electric field to vary. They excited the modes with laser pulses and measured their time-resolved optical response, finding frequency shifts on the order of 2 GHz. Furthermore, the two modes responded differently to the field, a behavior they interpreted using a model that distinguishes the individual layers.

The study connects electrical control with an optical reading of magnetic dynamics and, in addition, has the participation of the University of Valencia. Their measurements were made at 10 kelvin, so the demonstration does not establish room temperature operation.

The Freie Universität Berlin, in Germany, the Institute of Molecular Science of the University of Valencia, in Spain, and the Delft University of Technology, in the Netherlands, along with other centers, participate.
