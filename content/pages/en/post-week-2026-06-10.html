A liquid-crystal shutter and two ways to use a magnet

A quantum dot’s colour comes from the material, but its brightness can be adjusted by changing the light reaching it. In the other papers, magnetic fluctuations help explore a computation, and interlayer coupling is used to tune spin waves.

Photonics

A liquid crystal that controls how much light reaches quantum dots

[1] A. Ramadas, M. D. Patekari, S. H. Lee, and M. Kim, “Electrically tunable UV–visible modulation and voltage-controlled quantum dot emission via polymer network liquid crystals,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 74, Jun. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00578-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
Jeonbuk National University (South Korea)

Without an electric field, the liquid-crystal film transmits much of the light. A voltage reorients its molecules, creating refractive-index differences that scatter light in different directions.

The team places green- and red-emitting quantum dots behind the film. It can then dim their emission by adjusting how much light excites them. The mechanism controls the pump reaching the dots, while the emitting material determines the colour.

Electronics

An Ising machine with memory and a fluctuating magnet

[2] M. A. Iftakher et al., “Intrinsic annealing in a hybrid memristor-magnetic tunnel junction Ising machine,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5246, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71844-8. Open access.

The team includes researchers from Université Paris-Saclay and Université Grenoble Alpes, in France.

Many optimisation problems can be expressed as a search for the configuration that minimises a function. Ising machines transfer that idea to a physical system with states equivalent to spins and connections between them. To prevent the search from becoming trapped too soon in a local solution, fluctuations can be introduced at the beginning and reduced later, much like a gradual cooling process.

Iftakher and his collaborators combine a hafnium oxide memristor array, which stores the couplings, with a stochastic magnetic tunnel junction, which provides the probabilistic response. Increasing the reading voltage also changes the bias of the junction, so that the same signal used to read the array also helps reduce randomness gradually.

The prototype found the optimum in a 24-vertex weighted MAX-CUT problem and in a ten-vertex coloring problem. This demonstration used an externally implemented feedback loop, so the prospect of integrating larger systems is not yet equivalent to having built a complete, self-contained solver on a single chip. The result shows, however, how two memory technologies can provide complementary pieces of the same calculation.

Materials

Tuning spin waves in a three-layer magnet

[3] N. Stetzuhn et al., “Tunable magnons in a dual-gated 2D antiferromagnet,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5262, Jun. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74067-z. Open access.

The Freie Universität Berlin, in Germany, the Institute of Molecular Science of the University of Valencia, in Spain, and the Delft University of Technology, in the Netherlands, along with other centers, participate.

Collective spin waves, or magnons, offer another way to transport and manipulate information in magnetic materials. Stetzuhn and colleagues work with CrSBr, a laminar antiferromagnet whose magnetic excitations can be observed optically thanks to its interaction with other excitations of the material. The question is whether those frequencies can be tuned with electric gates in a few-layer device.

To answer it, they placed a sample of three layers between two gates, the combination of which allows the carrier density and the perpendicular electric field to vary. They excited the modes with laser pulses and measured their time-resolved optical response, finding frequency shifts on the order of 2 GHz. Furthermore, the two modes responded differently to the field, a behavior they interpreted using a model that distinguishes the individual layers.

The study connects electrical control with an optical reading of magnetic dynamics and, in addition, has the participation of the University of Valencia. Their measurements were made at 10 kelvin, so the demonstration does not establish room temperature operation. The possibility of designing magnonic devices closer to applications will depend, among other things, on how to preserve and control that response under less demanding conditions.
