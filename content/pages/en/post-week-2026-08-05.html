Week of August 5, 2026

Spectra in a guide, visual memory and quantum geometry

Week of August 5 to 11, 2026.

Photonics

A guide with reflections to distinguish spectra

H. He et al., “Hyper-spectral photonic integrated circuits based on recursive inverse design”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 9613, 2026. Epub August 11. Open Access Article.

The work includes researchers from Peking University, in China.

To distinguish very close wavelengths, a spectrometer needs to generate different responses to very small changes in the spectrum. He and his collaborators explore how to do this in an integrated guide, introducing a sequence of partially reflective points that multiply the interference paths available for light.

The design is obtained with an inverse and recursive procedure that optimizes both the geometry of each point and the connections between them. The authors fabricated a silicon nitride device and reported that a guide with eleven nodes achieved a spectral complexity comparable to that of an architecture with at least one hundred Mach-Zehnder interferometer stages. With various guides and thermal adjustments they demonstrated spectral reconstruction with sub-picometer resolution and analysis of optically encoded radiofrequency signals.

The resolution figure describes a reconstruction that depends on calibration and signal processing, while the experimental system includes controllers, sources and an external photodetector. The article also identifies thermal drift and available calibration sources as practical limits, a reminder that the size of the photonic zone is only one part of the spectrometer.

Electronics

A zinc oxide memory that responds to movement

Z. Wang et al., “Wide field-of-view anisotropic optoelectronic resistive memory for monocular in-sensor 3D motion perception and localization system”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 9470, 2026. Epub August 5. Open Access Article.

Among the participating institutions is the Southern University of Science and Technology, in China.

The orientation and movement of an object leave traces in the light received by a sensor, but extracting three-dimensional information often requires combining several channels and calculation stages. Wang and his collaborators study an optoelectronic resistive memory built with zinc oxide nanowires whose response depends on the angle and dose of illumination.

The defect gradient of the nanowires mediates non-volatile optical switching that the team uses to obtain motion-related information. The authors combine the signal from a depth-sensitive channel with an adjacent array that collects spatial information and then use a recurrent network to recognize sequences. Under the reported evaluation conditions, the system achieved an accuracy of 95.41% in a noisy test.

It is important to attribute that figure to the sensor and processing suite, not to a single cell or any three-dimensional scene. The proposal illustrates how a directional property of the device can contribute to perception, although generalization to more varied environments and a complete comparison of system consumption require additional work.

Materials

A Hall response that changes with ferroelectric polarization

R. Wang et al., “Room-temperature ferroelectrically switchable quantum geometry in few-layer WTe₂ for complementary in-memory computing”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 9466, 2026. Epub August 6. Open Access Article.

Among the participants is Nanjing University, in China.

In a ferroelectric material, the polarization can retain an orientation after removing the field that changed it. Wang and his collaborators explore how this electrical memory is related to the nonlinear Hall response of a few layers of WTe₂, where the measured signal also depends on geometric properties of the electronic states.

By changing the polarization at room temperature, the team observes second- and third-order responses that switch in a correlated manner and with opposite signs. The authors study how to use these complementary signals to extract edges in different orientations, and describe a nine-element configuration for a convolution operation. They also report retention of the order of 100,000 seconds and tests of more than 10,000 cycles under work conditions.

In the texture recognition evaluation, the accuracy of 98% corresponds to an ideal simulation of the network, while when incorporating measured features and noise from the devices the figure drops to 94%. The distinction helps place the scope of the result, which combines an interesting physical answer with an analysis of how it could be used in in-memory computing, without that evaluation being equivalent to a complete processor already deployed.
