Week of August 5, 2026

Reflections for reading a spectrum, motion memory and two responses from one element

Two lasers of almost the same colour can leave quite different fingerprints if you give the light enough paths to take. We'll also ask what a nanowire remembers once the light goes out, and why two pieces of tellurium can respond in opposite ways to the same electrical adjustment.

Photonics

A guide with reflections to distinguish spectra

[1] H. He et al., “Hyper-spectral photonic integrated circuits based on recursive inverse design,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9613, Aug. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76328-3.

If two lasers emit at almost the same wavelength, looking at their intensity at the exit of a smooth waveguide will tell us quite little. He and colleagues, with the participation of Peking University, in China, introduce small reflectors along the path. Thus, before reaching the detector, each wave crosses copies of itself that have traveled different distances. A slight difference in wavelength can then leave a recognizable imprint.

The design is obtained with an inverse and recursive procedure that optimizes both the geometry of each point and the connections between them. The authors fabricated a silicon nitride device and reported that a guide with eleven nodes achieved a spectral complexity comparable to that of an architecture with at least one hundred Mach-Zehnder interferometer stages. With various guides and thermal adjustments they demonstrated spectral reconstruction with sub-picometer resolution and analysis of optically encoded radiofrequency signals.

What would we buy when we got such a spectrometer? The photonic region that produces interference is small, but reading the fingerprints requires calibration, sources, drivers, and an external photodetector. If you change the temperature, you can also change the pattern associated with each wavelength. That is why the resolution cited makes sense together with the procedure with which the spectrum is reconstructed.

Related reading. Compact spectrometer based on a disordered photonic chip. Nature Photonics (2013).

Electronics

A zinc oxide memory that responds to movement

[2] Z. Wang et al., “Wide field-of-view anisotropic optoelectronic resistive memory for monocular in-sensor 3D motion perception and localization system,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9470, Aug. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76261-5.

Let's imagine that the light has already gone out and someone asks us from what angle it came. In these zinc oxide nanowires we could still look for a clue by measuring the resistance. Wang and colleagues, including researchers from Southern University of Science and Technology in China, take advantage of the fact that the electrical fingerprint depends on both the dose of light and the angle at which the sensor was illuminated.

The defect gradient of the nanowires mediates non-volatile optical switching that the team uses to obtain motion-related information. The authors combine the signal from a depth-sensitive channel with an adjacent array that collects spatial information and then use a recurrent network to recognize sequences.

To go from remembering an exposure to recognizing movement, several successive measures must be put in relation. Hence, the 95.41% correctness in tests with noise should be read as the result of the entire chain. The nanowires provide a signal that lasts longer than the illumination, and the network interprets the order in which the signals appeared.

Related reading. Full hardware implementation of neuromorphic visual system based on multimodal optoelectronic resistive memory arrays for versatile image processing. Nature Communications (2023).

Materials

One element, two optical responses

[3] H. Liu et al., “Complementary nonlinear optics for polarimetric computing in tellurium,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8118, Aug. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75197-0.

Imagine two neighbouring areas of a photograph with the same brightness. The edge between them could reappear if the light coming from each side has different polarisation and we look with something that knows how to distinguish them. In this case it turns out that this "something" can be manufactured twice with the same element, tellurium, by changing the form we give it.

Hao Liu and his collaborators, with the participation of Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications and Nanjing University, in China, combine tellurium nanowires and nanosheets. Both structures present polarization-dependent responses and generate second harmonic light, but their response to an applied voltage changes in opposite directions. The team uses this complementarity to explore image coding and processing functions.

The authors propose taking advantage of the orientation of these pieces to select polarisations and electrical adjustment to extract, among other things, edges from an image. Because they read at twice the incident frequency, they can distinguish the generated signal from the light that entered. That detail also has a cost. To estimate what would be saved in a camera, the reading optics and electronics of the pixels will have to be taken into account along with the work that the nanostructures already do.

Related reading. Nonlinear Optics with 2D Layered Materials. Advanced Materials (2018).
