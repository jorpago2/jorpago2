Week of August 5, 2026

Reflections for reading a spectrum, motion memory and two responses from one element

Reflections inside a waveguide can reveal clues about the spectrum entering it. The other papers distinguish signals in the material itself, with a zinc oxide memory that responds to motion and an element that can give different optical responses.

Photonics

A guide with reflections to distinguish spectra

[1] H. He et al., “Hyper-spectral photonic integrated circuits based on recursive inverse design,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9613, Aug. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76328-3.

The work includes researchers from Peking University, in China.

To distinguish very close wavelengths, a spectrometer needs to generate different responses to very small changes in the spectrum. He and his collaborators explore how to do this in an integrated guide, introducing a sequence of partially reflective points that multiply the interference paths available for light.

The design is obtained with an inverse and recursive procedure that optimizes both the geometry of each point and the connections between them. The authors fabricated a silicon nitride device and reported that a guide with eleven nodes achieved a spectral complexity comparable to that of an architecture with at least one hundred Mach-Zehnder interferometer stages. With various guides and thermal adjustments they demonstrated spectral reconstruction with sub-picometer resolution and analysis of optically encoded radiofrequency signals.

The resolution figure describes a reconstruction that depends on calibration and signal processing, while the experimental system includes controllers, sources and an external photodetector. The article also identifies thermal drift and available calibration sources as practical limits, a reminder that the size of the photonic zone is only one part of the spectrometer.

Electronics

A zinc oxide memory that responds to movement

[2] Z. Wang et al., “Wide field-of-view anisotropic optoelectronic resistive memory for monocular in-sensor 3D motion perception and localization system,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9470, Aug. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76261-5.

Among the participating institutions is the Southern University of Science and Technology, in China.

The orientation and movement of an object leave traces in the light received by a sensor, but extracting three-dimensional information often requires combining several channels and calculation stages. Wang and his collaborators study an optoelectronic resistive memory built with zinc oxide nanowires whose response depends on the angle and dose of illumination.

The defect gradient of the nanowires mediates non-volatile optical switching that the team uses to obtain motion-related information. The authors combine the signal from a depth-sensitive channel with an adjacent array that collects spatial information and then use a recurrent network to recognize sequences.

Different angles of illumination leave different responses in the nanowires even after the light is removed. To recognise motion, the system combines that memory with a recurrent network that interprets the sequence over time. The 95.41% accuracy in the noisy test comes from the sensor and the network working together.

Materials

One element, two optical responses

[3] H. Liu et al., “Complementary nonlinear optics for polarimetric computing in tellurium,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8118, Aug. 11, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75197-0.

Polarization adds information to an image that does not always appear in its intensity, so it can help distinguish details or separate background signals. To take advantage of it, however, it is necessary that the material responds differently depending on the orientation of the light and that this difference can be incorporated into a useful operation.

Hao Liu and his collaborators, with the participation of Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications and Nanjing University, in China, combine tellurium nanowires and nanosheets. Both structures present polarization-dependent responses and generate second harmonic light, but their response to an applied voltage changes in opposite directions. The team uses this complementarity to explore image coding and processing functions.

In the processing proposal, the orientation of the nanostructures helps to filter signals with certain polarizations, while the electrically modulated response allows operations such as edge extraction. The reading is carried out at the second harmonic frequency, which separates this signal from the fundamental light, although it also requires more complex optical instrumentation than the direct electrical reading of a conventional photodetector.

The study is a proof of concept and not a comparison of consumption or performance of a complete system against existing cameras.
