Week of May 20, 2026

Trimming a finished chip, storing states in a molecule and watching quantum dots

Even after an optical circuit is fabricated, there is a way to adjust how much light passes between its guides. The other experiments look for changes on smaller scales, from steps in the current through a single molecule to blinking shared by many nanocrystals assembled together.

Photonics

Adjust a photonic circuit after manufacturing it

[1] R. Xu et al., “Localized carbon deposition enables trimming of photonic integrated circuits,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4562, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73411-7.

A small error in the spacing between two waveguides fabricated on the same chip can send light to the wrong output port. For a design to work as intended, components often need to be corrected after the chip is manufactured, taking care that the adjustment does not introduce too many losses or require keeping a heater on.

Xu and colleagues study a localized and non-volatile way to do this using carbon deposition assisted by a focused ion beam. By adding material to a specific region of the waveguide, they modify its optical properties and adjust the behavior of asymmetric directional couplers made of silicon nitride.

Heidelberg University, in Germany, and Oxford University, in the United Kingdom, participate in the collaboration. The authors estimate an additional loss close to 0.3 dB in the case of adjustment studied and observe stability for two months. The method offers a precise tool for experiments and device calibration, although the work itself indicates that the use of a focused ion beam poses practical challenges for its massive application in manufacturing.

Related reading. Post-fabrication resonance trimming of Si3N4 photonic circuits via localized thermal annealing of a sputter-deposited SiO2 cladding. Optical Materials Express (2021).

Electronics

A molecule with several memory states

[2] H. Zhang et al., “Single-molecule neuromorphic device with aJ-level power consumption per switching,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4655, May 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71127-2.

To represent the weight of a neural connection in one molecule, the junction must retain several conductance levels that can be distinguished by reading its current.

Hua Zhang and his collaborators, from the Xiamen University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, have studied a junction in which a molecule connects two gold electrodes within an electrolyte solution. By applying a gate voltage, the ions in the solution modify their interaction with the molecule and, thereby, change its conductance. The combination of measurements and calculations suggests that molecular reorganization and the movement of ions participate in the different observed states.

The team distinguishes over ten conductance levels, with transient and persistent changes tested in association and recognition tasks. At a bias of 1 mV with 1 ms pulses, the energy per event is estimated by adding channel and gate contributions.

The authors estimate 6.34 attojoules for one operation at the molecular junction. The electrochemical setup and the instruments that prepare and read the state sit outside that estimate. It gives a basis for comparing the energy to change the conductance of this element with the switching energy of other memory elements.

Related reading. Single‐Molecule Electrochemical Transistors. Advanced Materials (2021).

Materials

When a network of quantum dots flashes in unison

[3] Q. Tan et al., “Room temperature collective blinking and photon bunching from CsPbBr3 quantum dot superlattice,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4536, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70931-0.

A quantum dot can blink under steady illumination. When many dots form a superlattice and blink together, the question is whether excitations spread through the crystal or accumulate at a few sites.

Qiwen Tan and his collaborators, from the Institute of Science Tokyo in Japan and the KU Leuven University in Belgium, together with researchers from other institutions, have studied CsPbBr₃ perovskite quantum dot superlattices. At room temperature they observed an emission that alternates between intensity levels and a tendency for photons to arrive grouped over time, whose correlation intensity reaches a value of up to 3.9 in the measurements described.

Image and emission time observations suggest that the light comes mainly from an area much smaller than the entire superlattice. The authors propose that excitations created at different points in the structure migrate toward a low-energy region, where they can accumulate and give rise to a cascade emission that contributes to photon bunching. It's an interpretation supported by several measurements, although the observation of bunched photons alone does not prove that those photons are entangled.

Collective blinking is observed at room temperature. Using it in a light source would require control over where the emitting region forms and reproducibility from one crystal to the next.

Related reading. Weakly Confined Organic–Inorganic Halide Perovskite Quantum Dots as High-Purity Room-Temperature Single Photon Sources. ACS Nano (2024).
