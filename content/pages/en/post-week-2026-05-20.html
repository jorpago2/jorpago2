Tune circuits and arrange electrons

Week of May 20 to 26, 2026

Adjust a photonic circuit after manufacturing it

[1] R. Xu et al., “Localized carbon deposition enables trimming of photonic integrated circuits,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4562, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73411-7. Publication date record Open access.

In a photonic circuit, manufacturing differences that seem minute can change the distribution of light between two waveguides. For a design to work as intended, components often need to be corrected after the chip is manufactured, taking care that the adjustment does not introduce too many losses or require keeping a heater on.

Xu and colleagues study a localized and non-volatile way to do this using carbon deposition assisted by a focused ion beam. By adding material to a specific region of the waveguide, they modify its optical properties and adjust the behavior of asymmetric directional couplers made of silicon nitride.

Heidelberg University, in Germany, and Oxford University, in the United Kingdom, participate in the collaboration. The authors estimate an additional loss close to 0.3 dB in the case of adjustment studied and observe stability for two months. The method offers a precise tool for experiments and device calibration, although the work itself indicates that the use of a focused ion beam poses practical challenges for its massive application in manufacturing.

A memory based on charge density waves

[2] R. Venturini et al., “Room-temperature memristive switching between charge density wave states,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8360, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73267-x. Institutional publication record Open access.

In some solids the electrons and crystal lattice can be organized collectively into a charge density wave. If there are multiple stable configurations, switching from one to another could modify the resistance and provide a way to store information. Until now, many electrical demonstrations of this type required very low temperatures.

Venturini and colleagues study the laminar compound EuTe₄ and show that electrical pulses can lead it to persistent resistance states in a range that includes room temperature. The resistance achieved depends on the pulse voltage and the state can be cleared by a thermal procedure, allowing the device to exhibit memristive behavior.

The work, carried out with the participation of Jožef Stefan Institute in Slovenia, combines measurements on exfoliated devices and calculations to examine the role of different layers of the material. Although the phenomenon is attractive for memories based on collective electronic states, thermal erasure, measurement conditions and the fabrication of reproducible structures are part of the challenges that must be resolved before talking about a practical technology.

An organic interface that reinforces non-linear response

[3] Y.-T. Chen et al., “Interfacial dipolar interactions drive giant second-harmonic generation in 2D organic–inorganic heterostructures,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 6753, May 22, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73597-w. Open access.

A nonlinear optical material can convert part of the light it receives into another frequency, as occurs in second harmonic generation. In a monolayer the interaction path is very small, so reinforcing this conversion without losing control over the structure is an important question for integrated photonics.

Chen and colleagues stack an organic α-perylene crystal and a WS₂ monolayer to study how their joint response changes. The orientation of the molecules influences the coupling between both layers and allows the signal to be controlled through the polarization of the incident light. Under the conditions studied, the authors observe an increase in the second harmonic intensity close to 650 times compared to the WS₂ reference used in their measurements.

Researchers from Xiamen University and Renmin University of China participate. To examine the mechanism, the team also introduced an insulating separation between layers and compared different orientations, with results supporting a contribution from near-field dipolar coupling. Although work remains to incorporate these heterostructures into circuits, the study shows that the interface can be a design tool for optical response and not just a junction between materials.
