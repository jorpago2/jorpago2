Week of May 20, 2026

Trimming a finished chip, storing states in a molecule and watching quantum dots

Sometimes the most useful adjustment comes after the chip is finished. A small carbon deposit can correct a waveguide that splits light unevenly. In the other papers even the size of the active region takes some thinking about, from a molecule between contacts to an emitting patch much smaller than the superlattice feeding it.

Photonics

Adjust a photonic circuit after manufacturing it

[1] R. Xu et al., “Localized carbon deposition enables trimming of photonic integrated circuits,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4562, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-73411-7.

Suppose you have made two waveguides so that they share the light equally and the measurement returns a different distribution. A small separation deviation may be enough for that. Instead of throwing away the chip or keeping a heater on, you can try to correct only the section where the light jumps from one waveguide to the other.

Xu and colleagues study a localized and non-volatile way to do this using carbon deposition assisted by a focused ion beam. By adding material to a specific region of the waveguide, they modify its optical properties and adjust the behavior of asymmetric directional couplers made of silicon nitride.

In the trial, with the participation of Heidelberg University in Germany and Oxford University in the United Kingdom, they estimate an additional loss of close to 0.3 dB for the adjustment made. The correction remains for two months. For those who make many circuits on the same wafer, there is an interesting account. You have to compare what it costs to lose that small amount of light with what you gain by recovering a piece that had gone out of tolerance.

Related reading. Post-fabrication resonance trimming of Si3N4 photonic circuits via localized thermal annealing of a sputter-deposited SiO2 cladding. Optical Materials Express (2021).

Electronics

A molecule with several memory states

[2] H. Zhang et al., “Single-molecule neuromorphic device with aJ-level power consumption per switching,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4655, May 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71127-2.

How many different values can be written on a single molecule? Read quickly, the question seems to refer only to the molecule, but the contacts, the liquid that surrounds it and the voltage used to probe it are part of the experiment. Changing any of them can change the current we use to read the state.

Hua Zhang and his collaborators, from the Xiamen University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, have studied a junction in which a molecule connects two gold electrodes within an electrolyte solution. By applying a gate voltage, the ions in the solution modify their interaction with the molecule and, thereby, change its conductance. The combination of measurements and calculations suggests that molecular reorganization and the movement of ions participate in the different observed states.

The team distinguishes more than ten levels and tests short changes and others that last longer. With 1 mV bias and 1 ms pulses it estimates 6.34 aJ per operation adding the gate and the channel. It is an extraordinarily small energy for the junction. If you wanted to compare it to classifying a complete pattern on a processor, you would have to add who generates those pulses, who reads the current, and how many operations that pattern requests.

Related reading. Single‐Molecule Electrochemical Transistors. Advanced Materials (2021).

Materials

When a network of quantum dots flashes in unison

[3] Q. Tan et al., “Room temperature collective blinking and photon bunching from CsPbBr3 quantum dot superlattice,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4536, May 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70931-0.

A quantum dot can stop glowing for an instant even though the lamp that illuminates it remains on. The weird thing comes when you put a lot of them together and they flash in unison. Have they all started broadcasting at the same time or does a good part of the excitation end up gathered in a small region of the supernetwork? The two stories could begin with the same flash.

Qiwen Tan and his collaborators, from the Institute of Science Tokyo in Japan and the KU Leuven University in Belgium, together with researchers from other institutions, have studied CsPbBr₃ perovskite quantum dot superlattices. At room temperature they observed an emission that alternates between intensity levels and a tendency for photons to arrive grouped over time, whose correlation intensity reaches a value of up to 3.9 in the measurements described.

The team combines images with measurements of when the photons arrive. The data suggest that the light is concentrated in a small region and a cascade emission involving states with two excitations. This helps to understand why photons arrive in groups without having to assume that all nanocrystals shine exactly the same. To build a repeatable source, the next question would be how to choose where that region is formed.

Related reading. Weakly Confined Organic–Inorganic Halide Perovskite Quantum Dots as High-Purity Room-Temperature Single Photon Sources. ACS Nano (2024).
