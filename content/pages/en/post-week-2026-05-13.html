Week of May 13, 2026

A wireless link, a magnetic junction and photons from an edge

A radio receiver needs a reference to track a carrier whose phase wanders. This week that reference travels with the data. We'll also meet a magnetic junction that adds up the effects of several pulses, and a perovskite flake whose edge is an unexpected place to look for single photons.

Photonics

From photonic chip to wireless link

[1] Y. Guo et al., “Minimalist terahertz wireless transceiver in integrated photonics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4429, May 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71081-z.

In a very fast link, the receiver's job is complicated if the sources that generate the carrier change a little in frequency or phase. One possibility is to correct these fluctuations after digitizing the signal. Yijun Guo and colleagues wonder how much of that work they could save if they let a small reference travel through the air along with the data.

Yijun Guo and his collaborators, from Peking University and ShanghaiTech University, in China, propose an architecture that takes advantage of the relationship between an optical carrier and the signal it carries. Through residual carrier modulation and injection locking, they manage to recover frequency and phase information without resorting to the digital stages that would normally be dedicated to that task. The receiver uses a single photodetector, while the assembly incorporates a thin film lithium niobate modulator and a photodiode capable of generating the radio frequency signal.

On the optical table they place the antennas at about 20 cm and transmit at 144 Gbit/s with a 200 GHz carrier and commercial lasers of 4 MHz line width. Those centimeters matter. The test says a lot about signal recovery with real sources, but if you wanted to use the same idea between two buildings, the power reaching the second antenna would become another decisive part of the problem.

Electronics

A magnetic junction that builds up a response to pulses

[2] Z. Chen et al., “Nanoscale exchange-bias magnetic tunnel junctions enabled memristive synapse and leaky-integrate-fire neuron for neuromorphic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4362, May 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70802-8.

Participating institutions
Beihang University (China)

When you read a magnetic tunnel junction as a bit you usually expect a high or low resistance. To imitate the weight of a connection, it would be much better to be able to stay halfway. Here the coupling with an antiferromagnetic material allows a succession of pulses to change the state little by little in the same stack of materials.

Time-resolved measurements show gradual changes with 0.4 ns pulses. Depending on how it is used, the behaviour can represent a connection weight or accumulate input until the circuit emits a pulse of its own.

One way to understand the two functions is to look at when you decide to read the current. After each pulse you can track how much the weight of a synapse has shifted. If you let the changes accumulate and only pick up an output when crossing the threshold, the same type of junction can act as a neuron. The 0.4 ns pulses allow us to see the intermediate steps that would be hidden if we measured only before and after the entire sequence.

Related reading. Spin‐Torque Memristors Based on Perpendicular Magnetic Tunnel Junctions for Neuromorphic Computing. Advanced Science (2021).

Materials

Perovskite edges offer a route to single-photon emission

[3] G. Na, J. Y. Park, J.-P. So, P. Guo, L. Dou, and H.-G. Park, “Single-photon emission from two-dimensional perovskites channeled through low-energy edge states,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4317, May 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71000-2.

Participating institutions
Seoul National University (South Korea)

If you're looking for an emitter in a perovskite sheet, you might start focusing in the middle, where there is more material. The authors do something much less intuitive. They illuminate with photons whose energy is below the gap inside and go to the edge, where there are lower energy states. There they find points that return light from photon to photon.

Locating a bright spot is only the start of a single-photon test. An emitter that has just fired needs to be excited again before it can emit a second photon. Splitting its light between two detectors and counting nearly simultaneous arrivals reveals a shortage of pairs, something a merely brighter patch of edge would not explain.

They then create new edges by deformation and find individual emission again. That step changes what can be concluded. Finding an emitter by chance already allows you to study its light, but making an edge in a chosen location offers a clue to decide where to look for the next one. Before designing a matrix, of course, it would remain to know how many of these sites end up giving a usable emitter.

Related reading. Coherent single-photon emission from colloidal lead halide perovskite quantum dots. Science (2019).

Also worth a look

These two notes draw on the public abstracts of papers published under subscription.

  • Reversing a light beam to write antiferromagnetic domains. In LiNiPO₄, lighting from one side is not the same as lighting from the other. Magnetoelectric coupling allows you to choose which domain is written by changing the direction in which the beam arrives.

    [4] S. Toyoda et al., “All-optical control of antiferromagnetic domains via an inverse optical magnetoelectric effect,” Nature Materials, vol. 25, no. 9, pp. 1572–1579, May 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02608-4.

  • A photocurrent that reveals magnetic order. The CrSBr bilayer returns a photocurrent of different sign depending on how its magnetic moments are arranged. So you can read the configuration with a pair of contacts after illumination.
