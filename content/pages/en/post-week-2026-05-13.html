Week of May 13, 2026

A wireless link, a magnetic junction and photons from an edge

A 144 Gbit/s wireless link tests more than transmitter speed, since the receiver still has to recover the data. At much smaller scales, a magnetic junction keeps a record of successive pulses, and the edge of a crystal provides a place to look for single-photon emission.

Photonics

From photonic chip to wireless link

[1] Y. Guo et al., “Minimalist terahertz wireless transceiver in integrated photonics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4429, May 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71081-z.

Frequencies in the hundreds of gigahertz offer room for wide wireless channels, but the receiver must track fluctuations in the carrier. Could that job be simplified if the received signal carried a reference for recovering its phase?

Yijun Guo and his collaborators, from Peking University and ShanghaiTech University, in China, propose an architecture that takes advantage of the relationship between an optical carrier and the signal it carries. Through residual carrier modulation and injection locking, they manage to recover frequency and phase information without resorting to the digital stages that would normally be dedicated to that task. The receiver uses a single photodetector, while the assembly incorporates a thin film lithium niobate modulator and a photodiode capable of generating the radio frequency signal.

The demonstration uses a 200 GHz carrier and a pair of antennas about 20 cm apart. That short path lets the team study signal recovery with the transmitter and receiver under well-defined conditions.

With commercial lasers of 4 MHz linewidth, the link carries 144 Gbit/s. The receiver saves one particular piece of digital processing, carrier recovery. Retaining part of the carrier in the transmitted signal gives the receiver the reference it needs.

Electronics

A magnetic junction that builds up a response to pulses

[2] Z. Chen et al., “Nanoscale exchange-bias magnetic tunnel junctions enabled memristive synapse and leaky-integrate-fire neuron for neuromorphic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4362, May 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70802-8.

Participating institutions
Beihang University (China)

The resistance of a magnetic tunnel junction distinguishes layers whose magnetizations point in the same direction from layers whose magnetizations point in opposite directions. The team changes its coupling to antiferromagnetic material and obtains intermediate states that can be programmed with repeated pulses.

Time-resolved measurements show gradual changes with 0.4 ns pulses. Depending on how it is used, the behaviour can represent a connection weight or accumulate input until the circuit emits a pulse of its own.

Reading a synapse calls for knowing how far the resistance has moved after a pulse. Emulating a neuron also calls for knowing which sequence of pulses will cross a threshold. The time-resolved experiment connects the two questions by following the intermediate steps before switching is complete, information lost in a measurement of the two end states alone.

Related reading. Spin‐Torque Memristors Based on Perpendicular Magnetic Tunnel Junctions for Neuromorphic Computing. Advanced Science (2021).

Materials

Perovskite edges offer a route to single-photon emission

[3] G. Na, J. Y. Park, J.-P. So, P. Guo, L. Dou, and H.-G. Park, “Single-photon emission from two-dimensional perovskites channeled through low-energy edge states,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4317, May 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71000-2.

Participating institutions
Seoul National University (South Korea)

A photon with energy below the crystal’s band gap is unlikely to excite the material in the interior of an ideal flake. Lower-energy states at the edge can collect charge and channel it towards a localised emitter.

The team uses photon statistics to confirm single-photon emission from some points along the edge. It also obtains emitters by creating edges through strain, suggesting a way to choose where the source sits.

Locating a bright spot is only the start of a single-photon test. An emitter that has just fired needs to be excited again before it can emit a second photon. Splitting its light between two detectors and counting nearly simultaneous arrivals reveals a shortage of pairs, something a merely brighter patch of edge would not explain.

Related reading. Coherent single-photon emission from colloidal lead halide perovskite quantum dots. Science (2019).

Also worth a look

These two notes draw on the public abstracts of papers published under subscription.

  • Reversing a light beam to write antiferromagnetic domains. In LiNiPO₄, reversing the light propagation direction selects which domain is written. The experiment uses a magnetoelectric coupling that makes the two illumination directions distinct to the material.

    [4] S. Toyoda et al., “All-optical control of antiferromagnetic domains via an inverse optical magnetoelectric effect,” Nature Materials, vol. 25, no. 9, pp. 1572–1579, May 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02608-4.

  • A photocurrent that reveals magnetic order. Shining light on an antiferromagnetic CrSBr bilayer produces a photocurrent whose sign depends on the magnetic configuration. The relationship offers an electrical way to tell those configurations apart.

    [5] Y. Dong et al., “Layer photovoltaic effect in a two-dimensional antiferromagnet with parity–time symmetry,” Nature Materials, vol. 25, no. 10, pp. 1776–1782, May 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02593-8.
