Bringing computation closer to the sensor

Week from August 26 to September 1, 2026

A quantum emitter to introduce nonlinearity into an optical network

[1] Q. Zhou et al., “Quantum nonlinearity for optical neural computing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10241, Aug. 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77202-y. Open access.

Multiplying signals with light is one of the ideas driving photonic computing, but a neural network also needs nonlinear operations to learn complex relationships. The problem is that the nonlinear optical responses of many materials require high intensities. Zhou et al, from the University of Wisconsin-Madison and Stanford, in the United States, study whether a quantum emitter integrated into a nanophotonic structure could offer an alternative.

The emitter response saturates as the light intensity increases and that saturation provides the nonlinear function that the authors incorporate into their network model. Using simulations and training that takes into account the physics of the device, they test classification and reinforcement learning tasks. They also compare the intensity necessary to achieve a given expressive capacity with that of other platforms, including graphene and a silicon guide with Kerr response.

The decisive distinction is that the work is theoretical and numerical. Energy estimation refers to the optical power associated with non-linear activations, not including all the components necessary to build and operate a complete system. It's a useful way to identify where an advantage might exist, although it still doesn't prove that a manufactured network consumes less than an electronic processor.

Using the time a sensor takes to switch off

[2] M. Wang et al., “Single-device in-sensor computing for multi-channel multiply-accumulate operations,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10196, Aug. 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76950-1. Open access.

A sensor does not always immediately return to its initial state after receiving a signal. Normally this temporal response is considered a limitation, but Wang et al, from the Chinese University of Hong Kong, in China, propose using it to perform part of the calculation on the device itself that receives the information.

Their method relates the response decay to the way carries are propagated in a binary operation. By encoding the weights as sequences of pulses and combining the responses, the system can perform multiplication and accumulation, operations that frequently appear in signal processing. The authors test the principle in several devices, including photodetectors and an oxide transistor, and explore applications in image processing and near-infrared functional spectroscopy.

The idea is suggestive because it does not depend on a single new material, although it does not eliminate the electronics that surround the sensor. The tests used generators, oscilloscope reading and a decision protocol to recover the bits; Additionally, speed and accuracy depend on the response and noise of the device. Therefore, the integration of the entire system will be as important as the operation performed on the sensor.

A buried layer to care for a perovskite LED

[3] J. Wang et al., “Ultrathin nano-layered boron nitride stabilizing perovskite quantum dot-based light-emitting diodes,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10415, Sep. 1, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77166-z. Open access.

Perovskite quantum dots can emit light efficiently, but the lifespan of the devices remains a challenge for their use in displays and lighting. In an LED, the problem does not depend only on the emitting material, since the interfaces with neighboring layers also influence the movement of charges, the accumulation of defects and the migration of ions.

Wang et al, from Zhengzhou University and Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications, in China, introduce an ultrathin layer of boron nitride at one of these interfaces. With it they seek to reduce defects and hinder ionic migration, while preserving the light response when the temperature increases. The optimized LEDs have an average external quantum efficiency of 30.05%, higher than that of reference devices.

The authors also estimate a T₅₀ life of 25,263 hours for an initial luminance of 100 cd/m². It is advisable to read that figure carefully, because it comes from an extrapolation and not from keeping a device on for all that time. The improvement points to the importance of controlling the interfaces, although a prolonged and in-use evaluation would help determine how far it can be transferred to a real application.
