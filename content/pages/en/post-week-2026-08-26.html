Week of August 26, 2026

Polaritons along edges, languages in an array and light-activated memory

The edges of a perovskite microwire guide light with different polarizations. The second paper asks how much circuitry it takes to identify the language of a text, while the third cools an indium selenide memory to 2 K to see what changes under illumination.

Photonics

Two edges to conduct polaritons with different polarization

[1] M. Kędziora, A. Opala, M. Zaremba, H. Sigurðsson, and B. Piętka, “Chiral polariton transport enabled by optical spin Hall effect in perovskite waveguides,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9188, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75965-y.

In a perovskite microwire, light can remain confined long enough to couple to excitons and form hybrid states known as polaritons. These retain part of the optical character and part of the material, making them interesting for controlling signals over small distances. Kędziora and his collaborators use a lead halide perovskite microwire to study how these states propagate and how their direction is related to the polarization of the emitted light.

By exciting the crystal above a threshold, the team observes edge emission with polarization distributions characteristic of an optical spin Hall effect. The authors report chirality greater than 80% and polarized propagation over tens of micrometers, while the coherence between the edges helps to fix the relationship between the observed states.

The microwire functions as a natural cavity and avoids incorporating the distributed mirrors often used in other polaritonic platforms.

Among the participants is the University of Warsaw, in Poland.

Related reading. Polariton spin Hall effect in a Rashba–Dresselhaus regime at room temperature. Nature Photonics (2024).

Electronics

Recognising languages with memristor arrays

[2] Y. Huang et al., “Hyperdimensional in-memory computing with analogue memristive crossbar arrays,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9162, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76067-5.

Participating institutions
University of Massachusetts Amherst (United States), University of Southern California (United States)

To identify the language of a text in a memory array, its characters have to be represented in a form the circuits can compare. On a resource-constrained device, it helps if that task does not require large amounts of data to move constantly between memory and processor.

Yi Huang and colleagues combine hyperdimensional computing with analogue memristor arrays. The former represents information using vectors with many dimensions, while the arrays perform operations between vectors and values stored in the conductances of their devices. The authors use this combination to encode features of the text before classifying it with a simple network.

The system identifies the language correctly in 95.24% of the test cases. The encoding was chosen to fit vector–matrix multiplication, so the operations available in the memory can both prepare the representation and classify it. That choice accounts for the reduction in circuitry explored in the paper.

Related reading. Memristive crossbar arrays for brain-inspired computing. Nature Materials (2019).

Materials

Light activates an indium selenide memory

[3] J. Wang et al., “Photo-activated digital resistive switching in a cryogenic photomemory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9167, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76121-2.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China), Jilin University (China), Xidian University (China)

At 2 K there is little thermal energy available, so illuminating a semiconductor offers a way to study resistance changes that are difficult to activate in the dark. It is therefore worth asking what happens when light and electrical voltage are combined in materials that might be integrated into cryogenic electronics.

Jiarui Wang and colleagues observed light-activated resistive switching in lateral α-In₂Se₃ devices. After illumination, an electrical signal can take the sample from a high-resistance to a low-resistance state, and the authors investigate how electrical pulses, additional illumination and an electrostatic gate can change the threshold. Their interpretation gives a central role to material defects and their evolution near the contacts.

Some measurements were made at 2 kelvin, and the behaviour changes as temperature rises, so this should not be presented as a general-purpose optical memory at room temperature. The study also observes variation between devices and cycles.

Related reading. Forming and Resistive Switching of HfO₂-Based RRAM Devices at Cryogenic Temperature. IEEE Electron Device Letters (2024).
