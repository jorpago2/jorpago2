Week of August 26, 2026

Polaritons along edges, languages in an array and light-activated memory

What's the connection between the two edges of a microwire when the light escaping them has different polarisations? We'll follow that clue over a few tens of micrometres, then meet a memory that tells languages apart and a semiconductor that responds differently to an electrical signal after exposure to light at 2 K.

Photonics

Two edges to conduct polaritons with different polarization

[1] M. Kędziora, A. Opala, M. Zaremba, H. Sigurðsson, and B. Piętka, “Chiral polariton transport enabled by optical spin Hall effect in perovskite waveguides,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9188, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75965-y.

We could start at the ends of the microwire and leave its interior for later. If we collect the light that comes out from one edge and the other, its polarisation is related to the direction it has followed. To find out where this relationship comes from, Kędziora and colleagues, including researchers from University of Warsaw, in Poland, take advantage of the fact that the perovskite microwire itself encloses light like a cavity.

By exciting the crystal above a threshold, the team observes edge emission with polarization distributions characteristic of an optical spin Hall effect. The authors report chirality greater than 80% and polarized propagation over tens of micrometers, while the coherence between the edges helps to fix the relationship between the observed states.

Here a polariton propagates, a state in which light has been coupled to an excitation of the material. Hence we can follow part of its history by observing the light that escapes from the crystal. That the effect is sustained for tens of micrometres is especially interesting in this setup, because the cavity is already given by the geometry of the thread and the team can do without the distributed mirrors of other platforms.

Related reading. Polariton spin Hall effect in a Rashba–Dresselhaus regime at room temperature. Nature Photonics (2024).

Electronics

Recognising languages with memristor arrays

[2] Y. Huang et al., “Hyperdimensional in-memory computing with analogue memristive crossbar arrays,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9162, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76067-5.

Participating institutions
University of Massachusetts Amherst (United States), University of Southern California (United States)

We almost never read to the end of a page to find out what language it is written in. Some combinations of characters already give us enough clues. The problem for a circuit that must do something similar is how to save those tracks in a format that can be compared quickly, especially if you have little space to move data.

Yi Huang and colleagues combine hyperdimensional computing with analogue memristor arrays. The former represents information using vectors with many dimensions, while the arrays perform operations between vectors and values stored in the conductances of their devices. The authors use this combination to encode features of the text before classifying it with a simple network.

Yi Huang and colleagues's system identifies the language in 95.24% of test cases. In addition to the success, it is worth looking at how they have chosen the coding. Preparing the text and classifying it require operations between vectors and matrices, precisely those that memory knows how to do. This is how the interest of the chosen representation is understood even before asking how many languages the test could be extended to.

Related reading. Memristive crossbar arrays for brain-inspired computing. Nature Materials (2019).

Materials

Light activates an indium selenide memory

[3] J. Wang et al., “Photo-activated digital resistive switching in a cryogenic photomemory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9167, Aug. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76121-2.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China), Jilin University (China), Xidian University (China)

At two degrees from absolute zero, turning on the light before applying a voltage can greatly change the outcome of a measurement. Jiarui Wang and colleagues find that a lateral α-In₂Se₃ device, upon receiving light, can electrically transition from high to low resistance. To understand it, it is worth looking close to the contacts, where the authors place a good part of the evolution of the defects.

Electrical pulses, further illumination and an electrostatic gate allow the threshold at which the change occurs to be adjusted. That gives several ways to explore what the light prepares and what the electrical signal finishes doing.

A part of these measurements is taken at 2 K. If we raise the temperature the behaviour changes, and the same result is not always obtained between devices or cycles. To think about cryogenic electronics, knowing this dispersion is as important as having seen the first device switch.

Related reading. Forming and Resistive Switching of HfO₂-Based RRAM Devices at Cryogenic Temperature. IEEE Electron Device Letters (2024).
