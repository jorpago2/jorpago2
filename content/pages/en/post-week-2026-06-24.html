Week of June 24, 2026

A flat fibre and two devices for working with images

Try bending a ruler flat and then edgewise. That everyday difference helps explain a fibre designed to measure pressure. We'll also see what a transistor holds on to after the light goes out, and how to switch from an image of a cell to its outline without moving it under the microscope.

Photonics

A flat optical fibre for measuring pressure

[1] P. Maniewski et al., “High aspect-ratio quadrilateral flexible silica optical fibers for enhanced multi-parameter sensing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5636, Jun. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72416-6.

If you hold a flat fibre by one end you quickly find the direction in which it can bend. The interesting thing is that the light also feels that there are two different directions. Maniewski and colleagues take advantage of this difference to read pressure with a Bragg grating written inside the fibre.

Pawel Maniewski and his collaborators, from the universities of Southampton and Bristol in the United Kingdom and KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden, have manufactured quadrangular-section silica fibers whose width is much greater than their thickness. The structure is flexible in one direction and more rigid in the other, while its interior can contain additional channels and materials that modify the response to different magnitudes.

In some of their pressure tests they reach sensitivities up to a thousand times greater than those of the fibres with which they compare. Propagation losses of approximately 0.16 dB per meter must also be taken into account. And what happens if the liquid presses equally on all sides? The internal channels still cause the section to deform unevenly. The two polarisations find different rates and that difference appears in the light reflected by the network.

Related reading. Bend monitoring and refractive index sensing using flat fibre and multicore Bragg gratings. Measurement Science and Technology (2020).

Electronics

An IGZO transistor with light response and memory

[2] D. Kang et al., “Retinomorphic Visual Processing Enabled by Contact‐Engineered IGZO Optoelectronic Synaptic Memtransistors,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e76218, Jun. 25, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.76218.

Participating institutions
Sungkyunkwan University (South Korea), Chung-Ang University (South Korea)

When you close the shutter on a camera you expect the next exposure to start from scratch. Something happens in this transistor that would be annoying for an ordinary camera and useful for a memory. After you remove the light, the current still depends on what you just received. Donghyun Kang and colleagues take advantage of that trace in an indium gallium zinc oxide channel, the IGZO.

Donghyun Kang and his collaborators study a synaptic transistor whose channel is made of indium gallium zinc oxide, or IGZO. By designing the contact with a hafnium oxide layer, the authors obtain resistance states that can be modified with both optical and electrical stimuli. This combination allows them to investigate gradual changes in conductance that serve as a representation of weights for a neural network.

There are two experiments that should not be merged into a single mental image. In the laboratory, a 6 × 6 transistor array serves to enhance contrast. The measured response is then taken to a neural network simulation that recognizes digits with 92.17% correctness. The first image has passed through the hardware, while the percentage comes from a subsequent task that uses what was learned from measuring it.

Related reading. Ultra-low power IGZO optoelectronic synaptic transistors for neuromorphic computing. Science China Information Sciences (2024).

Materials

A ferroelectric film switches between showing an image and highlighting its edges

[3] W. Chen et al., “Reconfigurable ferroelectric chiral nanostructures enable fast-switchable optical spatial differentiation,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 285, Jun. 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02363-w.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China)

Sometimes you want to see the entire cell and other times just where its outline passes. Switching views without moving the sample would make it much easier to check which edge corresponds to which structure. This ferroelectric film allows you to electrically choose between an ordinary image and one that highlights areas where the intensity of light changes.

In the edge-enhancing mode, the device can take either the first or the second spatial derivative of the image. Switching times reach as low as 62 µs, and the team tests reversibility over more than 1.8 million cycles. Both measurements matter if the aim is to switch views repeatedly while observing the same specimen.

Why the border appears is understood by mentally running through a line of pixels. Within a uniform region, two neighbouring points give almost the same intensity. When crossing the contour, the difference can be large. The film can make that selection optically and then show the entire image again. This way you can check if the edge obtained actually follows the cell or a spot that was next to it.

Related reading. Electro-optics of ferroelectric and antiferroelectric liquid crystal helical nanostructures. Journal of Molecular Liquids (2022).
