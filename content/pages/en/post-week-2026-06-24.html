Week of June 24, 2026

A flat fibre and two devices for working with images

A silica fibre can measure pressure because deforming it changes the light it transmits. This week a detail that would be easy to overlook in an ordinary cable makes a difference to the measurement, namely the shape of its cross-section. Two imaging studies ask what information reaches a detector and what comes out of it. One transistor retains a trace of an earlier exposure, while a film lets a microscopist switch from viewing a whole cell to picking out its outline.

Photonics

A flat optical fibre for measuring pressure

[1] P. Maniewski et al., “High aspect-ratio quadrilateral flexible silica optical fibers for enhanced multi-parameter sensing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5636, Jun. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72416-6.

A thin ruler bends readily flatwise and resists bending edgewise. A strip of silica behaves much the same way, with the useful difference that light travelling through it can reveal the deformation. Turning that effect into a pressure measurement starts with the shape of the fibre.

Pawel Maniewski and his collaborators, from the universities of Southampton and Bristol in the United Kingdom and KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden, have manufactured quadrangular-section silica fibers whose width is much greater than their thickness. The structure is flexible in one direction and more rigid in the other, while its interior can contain additional channels and materials that modify the response to different magnitudes.

In pressure tests, some of these fibers reached sensitivities up to a thousand times greater than those of the commercial circular fibers used as reference. The researchers also introduced a tin alloy in another configuration to increase its sensitivity to temperature, so the geometry and composition can be chosen according to the desired measurement. The authors point out, however, that the test fiber presents propagation losses of approximately 0.16 dB per meter, associated partly with the geometry of the core and residual absorption in the material.

The hydrostatic pressure test shows how the deformation is read out. Although the liquid presses from all sides, the internal channels cause the fibre cross-section to deform unevenly. The two polarizations of light then experience different refractive indices, a difference that shows up in the light reflected by a Bragg grating written into the fibre.

Electronics

An IGZO transistor with light response and memory

[2] D. Kang et al., “Retinomorphic Visual Processing Enabled by Contact‐Engineered IGZO Optoelectronic Synaptic Memtransistors,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e76218, Jun. 25, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.76218.

Participating institutions
Sungkyunkwan University (South Korea), Chung-Ang University (South Korea)

A camera typically reads out an exposure and stores the result elsewhere. Kang and colleagues explore what happens when the transistor itself retains part of its response after the light goes out. The current measured later then carries information about an earlier exposure.

Donghyun Kang and his collaborators study a synaptic transistor whose channel is made of indium gallium zinc oxide, or IGZO. By designing the contact with a hafnium oxide layer, the authors obtain resistance states that can be modified with both optical and electrical stimuli. This combination allows them to investigate gradual changes in conductance that serve as a representation of weights for a neural network.

The persistent response of the IGZO after illumination is useful for this idea because the signal does not necessarily disappear as soon as the light is turned off. However, preserving a trace also requires controlling how it is written and erased, so contact engineering becomes part of the functional design of the device, along with the choice of semiconductor.

With a 6 × 6 device array, the authors test image contrast enhancement. They take a different route to handwritten digit recognition, feeding the measured transistor response into a neural network simulation. It is this simulation that classifies 92.17% of the digits correctly.

Materials

A ferroelectric film switches between showing an image and highlighting its edges

[3] W. Chen et al., “Reconfigurable ferroelectric chiral nanostructures enable fast-switchable optical spatial differentiation,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 285, Jun. 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02363-w.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China)

To see the outline of a cell under a microscope, it helps to emphasise where the image intensity changes. To examine the interior, it may be preferable to switch back to bright-field imaging. The authors introduce a ferroelectric film that lets them choose the view by changing the applied electric field.

In the edge-enhancing mode, the device can take either the first or the second spatial derivative of the image. Switching times reach as low as 62 µs, and the team tests reversibility over more than 1.8 million cycles. Both measurements matter if the aim is to switch views repeatedly while observing the same specimen.
