Week of June 24, 2026

A flat fibre and two devices for working with images

A flat fibre changes how a waveguide can fit into an assembly. The imaging papers change where processing happens. One moves it to the transistor that receives the light, while the other picks out edges in a ferroelectric film.

Photonics

An optical fiber that prefers to be flat

[1] P. Maniewski et al., “High aspect-ratio quadrilateral flexible silica optical fibers for enhanced multi-parameter sensing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5636, Jun. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72416-6. Open access.

Optical fiber is usually represented as a thread with a circular cross-section, although there is no reason to think that this is the only useful geometry. When what we want to measure is pressure or strain, the way a fiber bends can be as important as the way it guides light, and a flattened section opens up possibilities that don't appear as easily in a round fiber.

Pawel Maniewski and his collaborators, from the universities of Southampton and Bristol in the United Kingdom and KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden, have manufactured quadrangular-section silica fibers whose width is much greater than their thickness. The structure is flexible in one direction and more rigid in the other, while its interior can contain additional channels and materials that modify the response to different magnitudes.

In pressure tests, some of these fibers reached sensitivities up to a thousand times greater than those of the commercial circular fibers used as reference. The researchers also introduced a tin alloy in another configuration to increase its sensitivity to temperature, so the geometry and composition can be chosen according to the desired measurement. The authors point out, however, that the test fiber presents propagation losses of approximately 0.16 dB per meter, associated partly with the geometry of the core and residual absorption in the material.

The proposed fibres are of interest as a sensor platform capable of distinguishing how forces are applied. The manufacturing and stability of these designs in specific applications remains to be studied, where the sensor response must be separated from temperature changes and the mechanical conditions of the environment.

Electronics

An IGZO transistor with light response and memory

[2] D. Kang et al., “Retinomorphic Visual Processing Enabled by Contact‐Engineered IGZO Optoelectronic Synaptic Memtransistors,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e76218, Jun. 25, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.76218. Open access.

Participating institutions
Sungkyunkwan University (South Korea), Chung-Ang University (South Korea)

In a conventional vision system, the image is captured first and the operations that allow it to be recognized are done later. The retina carries out part of this processing near the place where light arrives, an idea that has driven the development of devices capable of detecting and preserving information in the same element.

Donghyun Kang and his collaborators study a synaptic transistor whose channel is made of indium gallium zinc oxide, or IGZO. By designing the contact with a hafnium oxide layer, the authors obtain resistance states that can be modified with both optical and electrical stimuli. This combination allows them to investigate gradual changes in conductance that serve as a representation of weights for a neural network.

The persistent response of the IGZO after illumination is useful for this idea because the signal does not necessarily disappear as soon as the light is turned off. However, preserving a trace also requires controlling how it is written and erased, so contact engineering becomes part of the functional design of the device, along with the choice of semiconductor.

The researchers use the measured features to simulate a network that recognizes handwritten digits and achieves an accuracy of 92.17% on that task. It is important to retain that distinction, since the figure comes from a simulation of the network and does not by itself prove that a complete autonomous camera has been manufactured. The article offers, however, a way to relate the physical behavior of an oxide film with neuromorphic vision operations.

Materials

A ferroelectric film switches between showing an image and highlighting its edges

[3] C. Wen et al., “Reconfigurable ferroelectric chiral nanostructures enable fast-switchable optical spatial differentiation,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, p. 285, Jun. 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02363-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
Nanjing University (China)

To highlight edges in an image, light can be filtered so abrupt intensity changes stand out. The team designs a ferroelectric nanostructure that changes the filter when the applied field reverses.

The same piece can show a bright-field image or emphasise its edges using two orders of spatial differentiation. Switching takes as little as 62 µs. Repeated switching tests show how well this flexibility holds up with use.
