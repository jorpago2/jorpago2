Week of June 24, 2026

Connect emitters, remember images and process polarization

From June 24 to 30, 2026

This week we look at a way to connect quantum dots to photonic waveguides, an IGZO transistor that responds to light, and tellurium nanostructures whose optical response depends on their geometry.

Photonics

Ten emitters facing ten waveguides

[1] J. O’Hara et al., “Site-controlled quantum dot arrays edge-coupled to integrated silicon nitride waveguides and devices,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 7945, Jun. 24, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74664-y.

A quantum dot can act as a very small source of light for quantum photonics experiments, but an isolated source is just the beginning. To build a circuit, its emission must be collected and directed towards other elements, an especially delicate operation when both the emitter and the waveguide have microscopic dimensions.

John O'Hara and his collaborators, at Tyndall National Institute and University College Cork in Ireland, have worked with two separate chips. One contains an array of gallium arsenide quantum dots, placed in defined positions and surrounded by nanopillars that help direct the emission. The other incorporates silicon nitride waveguides, whose entrances are placed in front of the emitters.

The geometry allows an array of ten emitters to be aligned with ten waveguides, rather than treating each combination as a completely independent assembly. In addition to straight waveguides, the circuit contains components such as a beam splitter, allowing exploration of how to bring the emitted light to additional photonic functions. Once alignment was achieved, the authors recorded a stable signal for 41 hours without readjusting the position between the chips.

The procedure still requires careful alignment and a cryostat, which keeps the setup around 10 K during measurements. Therefore, the observed stability does not mean that the connection can now be assembled without adjustment or operate at room temperature, but it does show a way to combine different materials and manufacturing processes without having to build the entire circuit on the same chip.

Electronics

An IGZO transistor with light response and memory

[2] D. Kang et al., “Retinomorphic Visual Processing Enabled by Contact‐Engineered IGZO Optoelectronic Synaptic Memtransistors,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e76218, Jun. 25, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.76218.

Participating institutions
Sungkyunkwan University (South Korea), Chung-Ang University (South Korea)

In a conventional vision system, the image is captured first and the operations that allow it to be recognized are done later. The retina carries out part of this processing near the place where light arrives, an idea that has driven the development of devices capable of detecting and preserving information in the same element.

Donghyun Kang and his collaborators study a synaptic transistor whose channel is made of indium gallium zinc oxide, or IGZO. By designing the contact with a hafnium oxide layer, the authors obtain resistance states that can be modified with both optical and electrical stimuli. This combination allows them to investigate gradual changes in conductance that serve as a representation of weights for a neural network.

The persistent response of the IGZO after illumination is useful for this idea because the signal does not necessarily disappear as soon as the light is turned off. However, preserving a trace also requires controlling how it is written and erased, so contact engineering becomes part of the functional design of the device, along with the choice of semiconductor.

The researchers use the measured features to simulate a network that recognizes handwritten digits and achieves an accuracy of 92.17% on that task. It is important to retain that distinction, since the figure comes from a simulation of the network and does not by itself prove that a complete autonomous camera has been manufactured. The article offers, however, a way to relate the physical behavior of an oxide film with neuromorphic vision operations.

Materials

One element, two optical responses

[3] H. Liu et al., “Complementary nonlinear optics for polarimetric computing in tellurium,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8118, Jun. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75197-0.

Polarization adds information to an image that does not always appear in its intensity, so it can help distinguish details or separate background signals. To take advantage of it, however, it is necessary that the material responds differently depending on the orientation of the light and that this difference can be incorporated into a useful operation.

Hao Liu and his collaborators, with the participation of Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications and Nanjing University, in China, combine tellurium nanowires and nanosheets. Both structures present polarization-dependent responses and generate second harmonic light, but their response to an applied voltage changes in opposite directions. The team uses this complementarity to explore image coding and processing functions.

In the processing proposal, the orientation of the nanostructures helps to filter signals with certain polarizations, while the electrically modulated response allows operations such as edge extraction. The reading is carried out at the second harmonic frequency, which separates this signal from the fundamental light, although it also requires more complex optical instrumentation than the direct electrical reading of a conventional photodetector.

The study is a proof of concept and not a comparison of consumption or performance of a complete system against existing cameras. Its interest lies in showing that the shape of the same material, combined with polarisation and voltage, can add functions to the process of interaction with light itself.

Measuring electrical capacitance on ultrashort timescales.
