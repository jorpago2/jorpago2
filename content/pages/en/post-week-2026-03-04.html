Small optical networks, ion channels and chiral perovskites

Photonics

Design an optical network letting light find its way

J. Sved et al., “Inverse-designed nanophotonic neural network accelerators for ultra-compact optical computing”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 1059, 2026. Published online March 4. Open access article.

The work was carried out at the University of Sydney, in Australia.

A neural network needs to transform many input signals before producing a response, and one way to do this is to let those signals propagate as light through a structure designed to mix them. Sved and his collaborators explore this idea through inverse design, a strategy in which they start from the desired transformation and calculate what material distribution could produce it.

The team designed regions of silicon that redirect and combine optical fields, and then fabricated two devices with surfaces measuring 20 by 20 and 30 by 20 square micrometers. In experimental tests they achieved accuracies of 89% on MNIST and 90% on MedNIST, two data sets used to evaluate classification tasks. The size is striking because a good part of the optical operation is carried out while the light passes through a very small area of ​​the chip.

It is advisable, however, to read these figures together with the complete architecture of the experiment. Input preparation, detection, and training require external resources, while the manufactured passive design cannot be reprogrammed by simply changing a voltage. The result shows what a specialized nanophotonic structure can do with specific problems, and leaves open the question of how to combine several blocks for more complex tasks without losing the advantage of size and consumption.

Electronics

A photosynapse that moves ions

W. Liu et al., “Bioinspired nanofluidic iontronic device with integrated photoreceptor and photosynaptic functions”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 3523, 2026. Published online March 5. Open Access Article.

Participating institutions include the Southern University of Science and Technology, in China, and the University of Tsukuba, in Japan.

Optoelectronic devices are often described in terms of the movement of electrons and holes, while in biological systems ions are also involved in signal transmission. Liu and his collaborators combine both ideas in a light-sensitive nanofluidic device, with which they seek to reproduce some elementary functions of a photoreceptor and a synapse.

The structure links carbon nanotubes and molybdenum disulfide, so that illumination generates an electrical response at their interface and modifies ion transport. By varying the applied voltage, the researchers observe that the transient response changes sign and speed, a property that they then use to construct demonstrations of orientation recognition and fingerprint detection under different illumination angles.

The comparison with biological vision helps to understand what functions are intended to be imitated, although the device does not reproduce a complete retina nor does it demonstrate an autonomous visual system on its own. Its interest for neuromorphic electronics is in the coupling between light, interface and ionic transport, whose temporal evolution provides new ways to modulate the signal before sending it to other processing stages.

Materials

A perovskite transmits its asymmetry to a molecule

G.-L. Chen et al., “Chirality transfer from chiral perovskite to molecular dopants via charge transfer states”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 3757, 2026. Published online March 10. Open access article.

Among other centers, the National Taiwan University, in Taiwan, and the Los Alamos National Laboratory, in the United States, participate.

There are materials that distinguish between two forms of circular polarization of light because their structure does not coincide with their mirror image. This property, called chirality, can be useful for detecting optical signals without resorting to all the external elements that a conventional setup would use. Chen and his collaborators study how to extend the response of a chiral perovskite towards the visible by incorporating an electron acceptor molecule.

When F4TCNQ is added to the film, states appear associated with the charge transfer between the molecule and the perovskite, which introduce absorption in the visible. What is notable is that this new absorption also presents a different response to opposite circular polarizations. To explain the result, the work combines spectroscopy and calculations, and contemplates both the electronic coupling between host and dopant and the organization of the molecules within the crystal.

The authors also fabricated photodetectors from the film and observed a selective response to circular polarization in the ultraviolet and visible. The experiment opens a path to jointly modify the absorption and conductivity of these materials, although the transfer mechanism is discussed through several possible contributions and there is still work to determine its relative importance in other compositions and devices.
