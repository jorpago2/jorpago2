Week of March 4, 2026

Networks for light and protons, and an insulator with grain boundaries

In one network this week, the calculation is laid out in silicon and light follows it whenever data arrive. In another, a pulse moves protons and changes the path the next pulse finds. An insulator raises the opposite question. If the idea is to keep current out, what happens where two grains meet?

Photonics

Design an optical network letting light find its way

[1] J. Sved, S. Song, L. Li, G. Li, D. Meng, and X. Yi, “Inverse-designed nanophotonic neural network accelerators for ultra-compact optical computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1059, Mar. 4, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68648-1.

If the calculation you need involves mixing several inputs with known weights, you could try writing those weights directly into the path the light follows. Sved and colleagues, at University of Sydney in Australia, start from the result they want in the outputs and let the inverse design find the shape of silicon that distributes the fields in that way.

The team designed regions of silicon that redirect and combine optical fields, and then fabricated two devices with surfaces measuring 20 by 20 and 30 by 20 square micrometers. In experimental tests they achieved accuracies of 89% on MNIST and 90% on MedNIST, two data sets used to evaluate classification tasks. The size is striking because a good part of the optical operation is carried out while the light passes through a very small area of ​​the chip.

The trade-off is immediately seen if the task changes tomorrow. That region of silicon still has exactly the same shape, so the weights that were saved by adjusting each operation cannot simply be updated there either. For a transformation that you are going to repeat thousands of times, it is an attractive choice. For one that continually learns, deciding which part to leave fixed is already part of the design.

Electronics

Moving protons give a nickelate network memory

[2] Y. Zhou et al., “Protonic nickelate device networks for spatiotemporal neuromorphic computing,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 4, pp. 579–587, Mar. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02133-0.

Participating institutions
University of California San Diego (United States)

Listen to two syllables in one order and then in the opposite. There are the same sounds, but to distinguish the two sequences you need that, when the second one arrives, there is still something left of the first. In this hydrogenated nickelate network, that “something” is protons that have been redistributed between nodes. That is why two equal pulses can give different currents.

The team also makes asymmetric junctions on the same wafer that retain resistance states for longer. The network and its output weights thus share a material. Classifying spoken digits is a test of how the two memory timescales work together.

The difference between both types of junction is understood when moving from one voice example to the next. The old sound trail has to fade away so it doesn't mix with the new one, while the weights you've learned to recognize digits should still be in place. The clever thing is to get those two time scales by changing the joint, without having to change the material.

Related reading. Spoken Digit Classification by In-Materio Reservoir Computing With Neuromorphic Atomic Switch Networks. Frontiers in Nanotechnology (2021).

Materials

An insulator whose response holds across grain boundaries

[3] K. Liu et al., “van der Waals grain boundaries with inert electrical behaviors in inorganic molecular dielectric film,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2257, Mar. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69066-z.

Participating institutions
King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (Saudi Arabia)

With a large electrode on top of an insulating film you can find out if too much or too little current is passing in total. What you will not know is if this current is distributed throughout the entire area or is almost entirely filtered through a few joints between grains. To test this in antimony oxide, the team takes a conductive tip to walk across the surface of Sb₂O₃.

The currents look much the same in both places. Calculations attribute the difference from other insulators to the way grains meet in this molecular crystal, leaving the middle of the electronic gap free of new states.

The contrast that would be expected would be a more intense streamline crossing from one grain to the next. By not finding it, the comparison between neighbouring points tells something that the large electrode would have hidden. This is good news for a transistor gate. It would be quite inconvenient to have to place each channel exactly over the centre of a grain for the insulator to do its job.

Related reading. A wafer-scale van der Waals dielectric made from an inorganic molecular crystal film. Nature Electronics (2021).
