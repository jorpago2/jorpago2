Week of April 29, 2026

Reflecting polaritons, a graphene gate and dipoles that turn

One wave travels towards the edge of a flake and another comes back. From a single snapshot, could you tell which was which? We'll follow them in motion, then turn to a transistor that sends current through a layer of molecules and a ferroelectric whose polarisation the team wants to turn out of the plane.

Photonics

Following a polariton until it reflects off an edge

[1] A. Ghosh et al., “Spatiotemporal visualization of long-range anisotropic plasmon polaritons in hyperbolic MoOCl2,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3884, Apr. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70565-2.

Participating institutions
The University of Chicago (United States)

Following the reflection of a wave in a pond is easy if you can wait for it to reach the edge. In a MoOCl₂ sheet, the wave of interest mixes light and charge oscillations and moves too fast for that. The team reconstructs its path with time-resolved photoemission microscopy. By changing the delay between pulses you can distinguish the advancing front from the one already coming back.

Some modes travel more than 10 µm, around three times the distance of more tightly confined modes reported in the same material. That comparison shows how much the choice of mode changes the distance a signal can travel within a single flake.

More than 10 µm seems like a small distance until you compare it to the size of the region where the field is confined. Not all modes win in both things at the same time. In a circuit in which it is interesting to carry the signal from one element to another, it may be worth releasing that confinement a little if in exchange the wave arrives with sufficient intensity.

Related reading. Manipulating Hyperbolic Plasmon Polaritons at Near-Infrared in an Anisotropic van der Waals Crystal. Nano Letters (2025).

Electronics

A molecular monolayer under a graphene gate

[2] Y. Xie et al., “High-performance microelectronic-integratable molecular transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5871, Apr. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72473-x.

Placing a molecule between two electrodes already makes it possible to measure a current. A transistor asks more of that junction. A nearby gate has to control how much current flows without disturbing the contact you have just made. Yu Xie and colleagues at Tsinghua University, in China, work with a vertical molecular monolayer and a graphene gate. With two types of molecules, they achieve on-off ratios above 10⁴ and test operation up to 350 K.

The team also fabricates about 8,000 structures on a two-inch wafer and obtains a functional transistor yield of 73% with the criteria it defines for evaluation. This figure must be distinguished from the manufacturing efficiency of the junctions, which is higher because not all the junctions that are formed later function as a transistor with the required performance.

The number of working devices deserves almost as much attention as the best current curve. An isolated junction can turn out well by chance or because it has been carefully selected. Repeating the manufacturing on a two-inch wafer shows how much of that response someone who is going to connect many junctions in the same circuit can expect to find.

Related reading. Realization of Molecular‐Based Transistors. Advanced Materials (2018).

Materials

Changing the direction of polarization

[3] S. Zhou et al., “Templated perpendicular ferroelectricity in textured Aurivillius oxide-based thin films,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3890, Apr. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70676-w.

With one electrode on top of a film and another underneath, it is quite convenient to read a polarisation that points from one face to the other. The difficulty is that the crystal may prefer another orientation. In bismuth tungstate, the team is looking at precisely how to change that preference, so that the simpler geometry of the contacts also works for reading and writing.

The study by Zhou and colleagues, involving Zhejiang University and the Institute of Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, proposes using the material’s own structure as a template to stabilize a metastable phase of tungsten oxide. The result is a film with out-of-plane polarization, whose structure is analysed using atomic-resolution microscopy and calculations.

In ferroelectric transistors and test memristors they observe switching ratios greater than one million, and study thermal stability up to 350 °C. It is convenient to separate these two tests. One tells to what extent the states are electrically distinguished. The other describes how much heating the phase that makes them possible supports. To decide if that film fits into a manufacturing process, you would be interested in both.

Related reading. Observation of Hidden Polar Phases and Flux Closure Domain Topology in Bi2WO6 Thin Films. Nano Letters (2023).
