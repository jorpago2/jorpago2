Week of April 29, 2026

Flat fibers, molecular transistors and gold nanocubes

From April 29 to May 5, 2026

This week three open access studies explore how geometry and interfaces change the behavior of optical, electronic and material devices.

An optical fiber that prefers to be flat

Optical fiber is usually represented as a thread with a circular cross-section, although there is no reason to think that this is the only useful geometry. When what we want to measure is pressure or strain, the way a fiber bends can be as important as the way it guides light, and a flattened section opens up possibilities that don't appear as easily in a round fiber.

Pawel Maniewski and his collaborators, from the universities of Southampton and Bristol in the United Kingdom and KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Sweden, have manufactured quadrangular-section silica fibers whose width is much greater than their thickness. The structure is flexible in one direction and more rigid in the other, while its interior can contain additional channels and materials that modify the response to different magnitudes.

In pressure tests, some of these fibers reached sensitivities up to a thousand times greater than those of the commercial circular fibers used as reference. The researchers also introduced a tin alloy in another configuration to increase its sensitivity to temperature, so the geometry and composition can be chosen according to the desired measurement. The authors point out, however, that the test fiber presents propagation losses of approximately 0.16 dB per meter, associated partly with the geometry of the core and residual absorption in the material.

The proposed fibres are of interest as a sensor platform capable of distinguishing how forces are applied. The manufacturing and stability of these designs in specific applications remains to be studied, where the sensor response must be separated from temperature changes and the mechanical conditions of the environment.

P. Maniewski et al., “High aspect-ratio quadrilateral flexible silica optical fibers for enhanced multi-parameter sensing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 5636, 2026. Full article. Epub April 29, 2026.

A molecular monolayer under a graphene gate

In molecular electronics, the goal is for the properties of a molecule to contribute directly to the operation of a circuit. However, placing a monolayer between two electrodes makes it difficult to control the molecules’ energy levels electrically, and the manufacturing of many similar devices introduces another complication.

Yu Xie and his collaborators, with the participation of the Tsinghua University in China, have studied vertical transistors in which a self-assembled monolayer is placed between a gold electrode and a graphene electrode. An ionic liquid on the graphene acts as a gate and allows the position of the molecular levels to be adjusted with respect to the energy of the electrons in the graphene. The authors test molecules of two types and find ratios between the on and off currents greater than 10⁴, even at temperatures up to 350 K.

The team also fabricates about 8,000 structures on a two-inch wafer and obtains a functional transistor yield of 73% with the criteria it defines for evaluation. This figure must be distinguished from the manufacturing efficiency of the junctions, which is higher because not all the junctions that are formed later function as a transistor with the required performance. Additionally, the devices in each cell share the ionic liquid gate, which makes individual addressing in dense circuits difficult.

The demonstration links the chemistry of the monolayer with a broader manufacturing strategy, but leaves pending the replacement of the liquid gate with a solution that allows each element to be individually controlled. That transition will be important in determining what role these transistors could play in practical integrated circuits.

Y. Xie et al., “High-performance microelectronic-integratable molecular transistors,” Nature Communications, 2026. Full article. Epub April 29, 2026.

A semiconducting shell for gold nanocubes

When a metal nanoparticle is illuminated, its electrons can respond collectively and concentrate the electromagnetic field near its surface. This plasmonic behavior is attractive for driving chemical reactions, although understanding what happens at the boundary between the metal and a semiconductor remains difficult, among other things because two apparently identical particles can behave differently.

Xiulin Fan and his collaborators have followed the light-induced growth of a cadmium sulfide shell on gold nanocubes. By illuminating the nanocubes in a solution with the appropriate precursors, they promote the deposition of the semiconductor and control the thickness of the layer, while observing the optical changes of individual particles and their activity in a test reaction.

The result does not improve indefinitely by adding more semiconductor. Among the thicknesses studied, a coating of about 8 nm offers the highest catalytic activity, approximately 2.62 times that of uncoated gold nanocubes in the chosen test. Photoelectrochemical measurements and simulations support the idea that this thickness favors charge separation and transport, although the authors themselves also consider the possible contribution of the local optical field to the reaction.

The advantage of looking particle by particle is that it allows you to see variations that an averaged measurement can hide. The improvement figure, for its part, corresponds to this reaction and these lighting conditions, so it is not enough to predict the performance of any other photocatalytic process.

X. Fan et al., “Single-particle catalytic monitoring of interfacial charge dynamics during light-driven CdS shell growth on Au nanocubes,” Nature Communications, 2026. Full article. Epub May 4, 2026.

Also worth a look

Polarization waves in a ferroelectric. Jeongheon Choe and collaborators describe the generation and coherent propagation of polarization excitations in laminar ferroelectrics using laser pulses. The editorial summary, published on May 4 in Nature Materials, relates these excitations to emission in terahertz. The page previews subscription content.

Heat also depends on orientation. Haidong Wang and collaborators study asymmetric thermal transport in a three-layer heterostructure of two-dimensional materials, whose response depends on the twist between the layers. The Nature Electronics article was published electronically on May 5 and the editorial page does not identify it as open.
