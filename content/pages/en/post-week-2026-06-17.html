Steering a signal, counting three states and reading spin with light

From June 17 to 23, 2026

Photonics helps direct various radio beams

An antenna made up of several elements can direct its signal without moving if the moment at which the information reaches each of them is controlled. The problem becomes more complicated when you want to transmit several beams at the same time and maintain their direction over a wide frequency range, since small delay mismatches alter the radiation pattern.

Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University in China and collaborators at Huawei have built a network that uses optical delay lines embedded in silicon to govern an array of antennas. Each optical channel carries radio information and goes through adjustable delays before being converted back into an electrical signal, allowing the direction of the beams to be controlled.

Eight antenna elements are used in the demonstration and the authors measure direction changes with switching times of about 60 nanoseconds. They also carry out a communication test that combines approximately one kilometer of fiber optics with a wireless link of about 20 meters. It is important to retain that description of the setup, because the delay chip is part of a system that includes external sources, amplifiers, detectors, and antennas. The contribution shows how integrated photonics can solve a particular function within a broader communication chain.

Z. Ni et al., «Fully-connected microwave photonic multi-beamformer with fast beam-steering for broadband wireless communication», Nature Communications, published electronically June 19, 2026. Open Access. DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-74485-z. The first electronic version precedes the definitive version used for this reading.

A transistor that responds in three levels to light

Most digital circuits represent information through two states, although there are proposals that use three levels to encode more than one possibility in each element. Building devices that clearly distinguish these levels is a necessary condition so that this idea can be transferred from logic to hardware.

A team at Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in South Korea has studied a heterojunction transistor that combines electrical control with illumination. The device presents three distinguishable current states, and the authors explore how to use that response to perform ternary logic operations with optical and electrical inputs.

The demonstration allows us to examine what a single transistor can contribute when light participates directly in the selection of states. However, converting an experimental device into a complex circuit would also require characterizing the variability between elements, the concatenation of operations and the cost of generating optical signals. The work invites us to consider where the detection of a light signal ends and its digital representation begins.

C. Lee et al., «A light-driven multi-state heterojunction transistor for optoelectronic ternary logic circuits», Nature Communications, published electronically June 19, 2026. Open Access. DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-74238-y. The first electronic version precedes the definitive version used for this reading.

A perovskite that preserves spin information for longer

Circularly polarized light can generate populations of states with different spin orientations in certain semiconductors. To read or manipulate this information, the polarization must survive long enough, something that depends on the structure of the material and the processes that cause the spins to lose their initial orientation.

A team from the Institute of Chemistry of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences in China has studied perovskite nanocrystals containing lead, magnesium, zinc and cadmium. The researchers relate the mixing of elements and the distortion of the lattice with changes in the electronic structure that prolong the relaxation of the spin with respect to the comparison samples.

By excitation and reading with circularly polarized light, they observe a signal associated with spin at room temperature and study its response to a magnetic field. The reported relaxation time is a few tens of picoseconds, a short scale in everyday terms that, for these processes, allows the optical manipulation to be examined before the initial orientation disappears. The study provides a way to investigate the relationship between composition and spin response, although it remains to be determined how it would translate to more complex devices and operating conditions.

Y. Zhang et al., «Optical access of spin-polarized excited states in Cs(PbMgZnCd)Br₃ nanocrystals», Nature Communications, published electronically June 18, 2026. Open Access. DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-74479-x. The first electronic version precedes the definitive version used for this reading.
