Week of June 17, 2026

Optical cells, deflected currents and light taking a different path

Light can program a cell that acts as a neuron or a synapse. In the other experiments, what matters is how a signal is deflected, whether it is a current at an altermagnet interface or light passing through a low-loss material.

Photonics

A cell for light-controlled neurons and synapses

[1] C. Lu et al., “Accelerating spiking neural networks with photonic reconfigurable devices,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5367, Jun. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72119-y.

Among the participants is Fudan University, in China.

In a spiking neural network, the elements that accumulate signals and those that pass signals between them usually occupy different places. Lu and colleagues investigate what happens when a single cell can perform both functions, combining a transient electrical response with a second response programmed by light.

The device incorporates hafnium zirconium oxide along with titanium dioxide. According to the authors, the electrical response allows us to emulate the behavior of a neuron that integrates pulses until a threshold is reached, while illumination provides another way to modify the state associated with a synapse. In addition, they tested optical addressing in a 16 by 16 cell submatrix, with the intention of changing the function of specific elements without altering their neighbors.

The latency and energy improvements presented in the article for vision networks come from an architectural evaluation under comparison assumptions defined by the authors, and should not be read as measured consumption of a complete network already manufactured. The experimental contribution is in the cell and its addressing, while the simulations allow us to explore what might be gained by transferring that behaviour to a larger system.

Electronics

An interface that changes transport in an altermagnet

[2] P. Chen et al., “Tailoring topological altermagnetic spin texture via interfacial exchange coupling in quasi-2D CrSb/(Bi, Sb)2Te3 thin film,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5350, Jun. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72021-7.

Participating institutions
Massachusetts Institute of Technology (United States)

A 2.4 nm CrSb film is coupled to a topological insulator. To see what changes in such a thin layer, the team measures how current deflects in the plane as it rotates the field and changes the neighbouring layer.

An anomalous Hall signal appears whose magnitude varies with the insulator’s thickness and electronic level. The study links it to magnetic coupling at the interface, helping separate the film’s contribution from what changes when another material is placed on top.

Materials

Change the path of light without absorbing too much of it

[3] X. Shen, R. Chu, D. Xu, Y. Gao, W. Zhou, and W. Zhang, “Low-loss phase-change material-based programmable mode converter for photonic computing,” NPG Asia Materials, vol. 18, no. 1, Art. no. 22, Jun. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41427-026-00645-8.

The authors work at Xi’an Jiaotong University, in China.

Phase change materials offer a way to preserve an optical setting after removing the stimulus that produced it. However, a large difference between the refractive indices of their phases is of little use if one of them absorbs too much light, especially when many cells need to be connected in a circuit. Shen and colleagues study this trade-off for antimony selenide, Sb₂Se₃, and compare it with that of more absorbing chalcogenide materials.

The study combines atomic structure calculations with electromagnetic simulations to design a mode converter integrated into a waveguide. The proposal uses the index contrast between the amorphous and crystalline phases to redistribute light, and calculations suggest that different written patterns could provide up to 32 levels. By relying on the phase difference without requiring high absorption, the authors propose a possible route towards larger photonic arrays.

Optical properties measured in earlier work are the starting point for the calculation. The 128 × 128 array is a model prediction. Establishing how many weight levels a fabricated array could use would mean writing them repeatedly, measuring their spread and tracking the optical loss through successive cells.
