Week of June 17, 2026

Optical cells, deflected currents and light taking a different path

How much space could you save if you could decide which cells would be neurons and which would be synapses after fabrication? We'll also look at a magnetic film just 2.4 nm thick and an optical element that keeps steering light after the stimulus used to set it has been switched off.

Photonics

A cell for light-controlled neurons and synapses

[1] C. Lu et al., “Accelerating spiking neural networks with photonic reconfigurable devices,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5367, Jun. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72119-y.

When you draw a network in silicon, dedicating a fixed piece to neurons and another to synapses seems reasonable until a different network arrives. You can be short on one thing while having cells left over on the other. Lu and colleagues, with the participation of Fudan University in China, are trying to make the cells remappable using electrical and optical signals.

The device incorporates hafnium zirconium oxide along with titanium dioxide. According to the authors, the electrical response allows us to emulate the behavior of a neuron that integrates pulses until a threshold is reached, while illumination provides another way to modify the state associated with a synapse. In addition, they tested optical addressing in a 16 by 16 cell submatrix, with the intention of changing the function of specific elements without altering their neighbors.

The 16 × 16 submatrix is where they verify that the cell to act on can be chosen. To ask how long it would take an entire network to recognize an image and how much energy it would expend, they take an extra step and simulate the architecture. It is worth distinguishing them, because the first test shows whether the light reaches the chosen cell and the second shows how much that freedom would be used in a specific task.

Related reading. Spiking Neural Networks: Background, Recent Development and the NeuCube Architecture. Neural Processing Letters (2020).

Electronics

An interface that changes transport in an altermagnet

[2] P. Chen et al., “Tailoring topological altermagnetic spin texture via interfacial exchange coupling in quasi-2D CrSb/(Bi, Sb)2Te3 thin film,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5350, Jun. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72021-7.

Participating institutions
Massachusetts Institute of Technology (United States)

In a thick film the interface can be thought of as occupying only a small part of the sample. It's hard to make that argument when CrSb is just 2.4 nm thick. Here it is put in contact with a topological insulator and we look at what happens to the Hall voltage that appears from side to side when current flows.

An anomalous Hall signal appears whose magnitude varies with the insulator’s thickness and electronic level. The study links it to magnetic coupling at the interface, helping separate the film’s contribution from what changes when another material is placed on top.

How do you discriminate between a signal that already gave a layer and another that is born when they are put together? The team changes the thickness and electronic level of the insulator and also rotates the field. An interface-based explanation has to account for the evolution of the signal in all those measurements. A single curve with the field in the most favorable orientation would have left much more room for different explanations.

Related reading. Direct observation of altermagnetic band splitting in CrSb thin films. Nature Communications (2024).

Materials

Change the path of light without absorbing too much of it

[3] X. Shen, R. Chu, D. Xu, Y. Gao, W. Zhou, and W. Zhang, “Low-loss phase-change material-based programmable mode converter for photonic computing,” NPG Asia Materials, vol. 18, no. 1, Art. no. 22, Jun. 18, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41427-026-00645-8.

A phase-changing material allows you to write a path for light and leave it in place after removing the stimulus. But there is a small drawback if the phase that best deflects the beam also keeps a good part of it. At Xi’an Jiaotong University, in China, they are studying how to combine index difference and low absorption so that the next element in the circuit still receives sufficient signal.

The study combines atomic structure calculations with electromagnetic simulations to design a mode converter integrated into a waveguide. The proposal uses the index contrast between the amorphous and crystalline phases to redistribute light, and calculations suggest that different written patterns could provide up to 32 levels. By relying on the phase difference without requiring high absorption, the authors propose a possible route towards larger photonic arrays.

In their calculations, different writing patterns would give up to 32 levels. When you then see a 128 × 128 matrix, it is worth looking at where in the article it appears, because it corresponds to the model. This prediction allows us to ask how losses or errors would accumulate when adding many elements. To know how much they would vary when manufacturing each cell on a wafer, the physical matrix would be needed.

Related reading. Integrating phase-change materials into foundry-made photonic circuits (Nature Communications).
