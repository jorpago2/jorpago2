Week of September 2, 2026

A nonlinear metasurface, high-temperature vision and an adjustable Hall response

When light has only a very thin film to cross, every layer matters if you're trying to double its frequency. The other papers invite us to think about how we read a device, from a photodetector still responding at 450 °C to a Hall signal that reveals a previously written polarisation.

Photonics

A metasurface that gives access to nonlinearity

[1] P. U. Fathi et al., “Quantum-well metasurface for free-space-accessible enhanced nonlinear polarization,” Nature Nanotechnology, vol. 21, no. 11, pp. 1558–1564, Sep. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02268-0.

By doubling the frequency of light, it is usually good for the wave to travel a good distance through the material. If the device has to fit into a very thin film we will have to compensate for such a short distance. The idea of Pernille Undrum Fathi and colleagues consists of choosing both the electronic states with which the beam interacts and the way in which it reaches them.

Pernille Undrum Fathi and her collaborators, with participation from Harvard University in the United States, combine GaAs and AlGaAs quantum wells with a dielectric metasurface. The wells are designed to reinforce a transition between electronic states, while the upper structure modifies the optical fields and facilitates access to components of the nonlinear response that would be difficult to exploit with direct illumination.

At 1.57 µm, figures can be put to the two contributions. The wells contribute a second-order susceptibility component of 1.6 nm/V, and with the etched metasurface the effective response of the assembly reaches about 14 nm/V. To repeat the experiment in another colour, it would be necessary to look again at where the electronic transition and optical resonance fall. The two figures carry with them the wavelength at which they were measured.

Related reading. Coupled quantum well semiconductors with giant electric field tunable nonlinear optical properties in the infrared. IEEE Journal of Quantum Electronics (1994).

Electronics

A sensor with memory that still works at extreme temperatures

[2] Y. Zou et al., “Atomically thin heterojunction-based optoelectronic synaptic devices operable from −190 °C to 450 °C,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9430, Sep. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76364-z.

Participating institutions
Tsinghua University (China)

Imagine that the current of a photodetector increases while we heat the laboratory. Before attributing a flash to it, we would turn off the light and repeat the reading at the same temperature, because even in the dark current can circulate. This comparison is especially demanding in the hot end of the test we discuss here.

The team works with a two-dimensional MoS₂-based material junction and tunes its interface to study both sensing and memory. Test devices between −190 and 450°C and still distinguish visible light at 450°C. It is a direct measure of what the detector can withstand under mounting conditions.

Memory requires additional checking. Once the flash is removed, you have to wait and see how long its electrical footprint lasts at the temperature of interest. To choose a sensor that also remembers an event, we would want to know that time along with the thermal range in which it still sees light.

Related reading. High-temperature optoelectronic synaptic devices based on 4H-SiC. Science China Information Sciences (2025).

Materials

A Hall response that changes with ferroelectric polarization

[3] R. Wang et al., “Room-temperature ferroelectrically switchable quantum geometry in few-layer WTe2 for complementary in-memory computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9466, Sep. 4, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76369-8.

If we just wrote down an electrical state, it would be impractical to have to change it again to find out what it was. Wang and colleagues, involving Nanjing University, in China, are looking for a read on the nonlinear Hall response of a few layers of WTe₂. In addition to revealing polarisation, the signals here lend themselves to doing an operation on an image.

By changing the polarization at room temperature, the team observes second- and third-order responses that switch in a correlated manner and with opposite signs. The authors study how to use these complementary signals to extract edges in different orientations, and describe a nine-element configuration for a convolution operation. They also report retention of the order of 100,000 seconds and tests of more than 10,000 cycles under work conditions.

There is an insightful way to read texture recognition results. In the ideal simulation the network reaches 98%. When the measured response of the devices is incorporated, with their noise, it remains at 94%. These four points of difference give a scale to the effect that the real material has on this task, something difficult to appreciate if we only stick to the larger of the two percentages.

Related reading. Ferroelectric nonlinear anomalous Hall effect in few-layer WTe2. npj Computational Materials (2019).
