Week of September 2, 2026

A material that remembers light and vortices on silicon

From September 2 to 8, 2026

This week's three reads bring together quantum wells for nonlinear optics, a programmable luminescent crystal, and an integrated ferroelectric superlattice on silicon.

A metasurface that gives access to nonlinearity

[1] P. U. Fathi et al., “Quantum-well metasurface for free-space-accessible enhanced nonlinear polarization,” Nat. Nanotechnol., vol. 21, no. 11, pp. 1558–1564, Sep. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02268-0.

Converting light from one frequency to another usually requires materials with a strong nonlinear response or paths long enough to accumulate the effect. If we want to reduce the device to a thin film, one option is to design the material and the structure that illuminates it so that both work together.

Pernille Undrum Fathi and her collaborators, with participation from Harvard University in the United States, combine GaAs and AlGaAs quantum wells with a dielectric metasurface. The wells are designed to reinforce a transition between electronic states, while the upper structure modifies the optical fields and facilitates access to components of the nonlinear response that would be difficult to exploit with direct illumination.

The authors estimate an effective nonlinear susceptibility of about 14 nm/V near 1.57 µm for the combination of both elements. The figure describes an effective property of the device under the conditions considered, not a conversion efficiency expressed as a percentage. Furthermore, the response depends on the resonance, so its spectral width and its adjustment with respect to the light source are part of the design.

The experiment shows how a promising effect on the material can be much more useful when you also design how to couple light to it from free space. It remains to be seen how this strategy adapts to other wavelengths and the needs of devices that must work outside specific laboratory conditions.

A memory written electrically and read with light

[2] Y. Wen et al., “Ferroelectrically programmable lanthanide luminescent memristor,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10597, Sep. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77397-0.

For a photonic network to be able to use persistently stored values, it needs some mechanism that preserves the state without applying a maintenance electrical signal. Yiyang Wen and his collaborators explore that idea in a ferroelectric crystal that emits light and whose intensity can be programmed using electrical pulses.

Researchers from Nankai University and Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications in China, along with other institutions, participate in the study. The team uses a PMN-PT crystal doped with erbium and ytterbium, where the reorganization of the ferroelectric domains modifies the local environment of the emitters and, with it, their photoluminescence. In this way, the pulse history is reflected in light states that can be read later.

The authors report 16 analog levels, retention for more than 27 hours and endurance greater than 100,000 cycles in the indicated tests. They also study an eight-by-eight element matrix, whose emission is recorded with a CMOS sensor. In that demonstration they obtain 94.02% recognition of the pixel states using a decoding procedure that takes calibration into account.

The memory does not require maintenance power to maintain state, although that does not mean that lighting it, reading it and processing its signal is free in terms of energy. Furthermore, the described digit recognition combines optical and electrical stages. The proposal is interesting precisely because of this persistent light reading, which allows studying how to connect ferroelectric materials with optical calculation operations.

Ferroelectric vortices on a silicon base

[3] V. V. Hevelke et al., “Periodic polar vortices in BaTiO3/SrTiO3 superlattices monolithically grown on silicon,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9648, Sep. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77270-0.

In a ferroelectric film the polarization does not always point uniformly. Under certain conditions it can be organized into curved patterns, including nanometer-sized vortices that arouse interest due to their possible electrical properties and the possibility of integrating them into devices.

Valentin Hevelke and his collaborators have studied superlattices formed by alternating layers of barium titanate and strontium titanate, epitaxially grown on silicon using an intermediate layer of SrTiO₃. Researchers from the Pennsylvania State University in the United States, among other institutions, participate in the work. Using X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy they identify a periodic organization compatible with polarization vortex tubes within the layers.

The layers studied have approximate thicknesses of 4.8 nm for BaTiO₃ and 2.0 nm for SrTiO₃, repeated ten times. The order of these patterns disappears around 450 K, while phase field modeling indicates that mechanical stress and interlayer coupling strongly influence the shape the polarization takes.

The presence of these patterns on silicon expands the platforms on which complex ferroelectric structures can be studied, although the article does not yet demonstrate a commercial memory based on its individual writing and reading. The next challenge will be to understand how to manipulate them in a controlled way and preserve their behavior by integrating them with contacts and device processes.
