Week of January 28, 2026

Lasers, vision and fluctuating memories

Week of 28 January–3 February 2026

Usually, when setting up a laser, you take care to keep reflections from getting back in. This team deliberately sends more and more light back until the source falters. Elsewhere this week, a phototransistor picks up details an ordinary photograph would miss, and the noise from a memory turns out to be worth listening to.

Photonics

How much returning light can a laser tolerate

[1] Y. Shi et al., “Exploring the feedback limits of quantum dot lasers for isolator-free photonic integrated circuits,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 96, Jan. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02185-w.

How much light can you throw back into a laser before it starts to lose coherence? In an integrated circuit it is not an idle question. A reflection on a waveguide is enough for part of the signal to find its way back. The Shi and colleagues group, with the participation of KAUST in Saudi Arabia and University of California, Santa Barbara in the United States, uses a setup in which this return can be increased considerably more than in previous trials.

The beginning of the collapse appears around −6.7 dB, which in your setup is equivalent to returning 21.4%. It is an appreciable fraction of the light that just came out. Near that limit they also test a 10 Gb/s link and monitor the stability for more than one hundred hours.

To design another link I would take both measurements. One marks the point at which the laser begins to change regime in that setup. The other asks if, as you approach it, the data can still be read at the other end.

Related reading. Isolator-free quantum dot comb lasers with optical feedback enhanced DWDM transmission. APL Photonics (2024).

Electronics

A photosynapse that distinguishes the polarization of light

[2] S. Zhang et al., “Polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision sensing enabled by pristine black arsenic-phosphorus,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 100, Feb. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-025-02125-0.

Two garments can look equally bright in a photo and yet return light with different polarisations. Zhang and colleagues take advantage of this difference with a sheet of black arsenic-phosphorus that responds according to the orientation of the optical field. The interesting thing for a sensor is that the same phototransistor retains part of its previous response, so the illumination history also influences the next reading.

The group, with the participation of University of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, both in China, walks through the exhibition point by point to reconstruct images of polarisation. At 1550 nm it measures a polarisation ratio of 4.66 and estimates about 25 pJ per pulse under the test conditions.

Then change the question. Do these answers help to distinguish images of clothing? A hybrid network that incorporates them exceeds 90% success rates in Fashion-MNIST. Scanning shows what the detector distinguishes, and classification shows what can be done with its response once incorporated into a network.

Related reading. Multidimensional detection enabled by twisted black arsenic–phosphorus homojunctions. Nature Nanotechnology (2024).

Materials

Noise as a window into phase-change memory

[3] S. Walfort, X. T. Vu, J. Ballmaier, N. Holle, N. Vollmar, and M. Salinga, “A free energy landscape analysis of resistance fluctuations in a memristive device,” Nature Materials, vol. 25, no. 4, pp. 643–650, Jan. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02487-9.

If you read the resistance of a GeTe memory several times, you would expect to see the same value until someone writes it again. In a very small amorphous area the signal can jump even while you try to leave it alone. Normally you would try to average them to better read the bit, but then you would lose just the information that interests the authors.

In a study from the University of Münster and RWTH Aachen University, in Germany, Walfort and colleagues analyse those fluctuations in a small amorphous region of germanium telluride. Using a hidden Markov model, they identify jumps between resistance states and track how their rates change with temperature. The observed variation suggests that assigning a single energy barrier is not enough to describe the transitions, because entropic contributions also matter.

The advantage of listening to such a small area is that an individual reorganization can still be distinguished in the stream. By adding many zones you would see more of a continuous noise or a slow drift of resistance. The analysis of these jumps allows us to test a microscopic explanation of the drift, although it does not yet indicate what manufacturing change would eliminate it from a memory in common use.

Related reading. Inherent electron and hole trapping in amorphous phase-change memory materials: Ge 2 Sb 2 Te 5. Journal of Materials Chemistry C (2022).
