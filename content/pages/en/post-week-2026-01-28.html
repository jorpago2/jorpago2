Week of January 28, 2026

Lasers, vision and fluctuating memories

Week of 28 January–3 February 2026

How much returning light can a laser tolerate

[1] Y. Shi et al., “Exploring the feedback limits of quantum dot lasers for isolator-free photonic integrated circuits,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 96, Jan. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02185-w. Date record Open access.

In a photonic circuit, a small fraction of the light can be reflected at interfaces and return to the laser that emitted it. If this feedback is strong enough, the source can become unstable. Optical isolators are therefore used to protect it, although they also add size and complexity to the system.

The team of Shi and colleagues, including researchers from KAUST in Saudi Arabia and the University of California, Santa Barbara in the United States, has examined the tolerance of a quantum-dot laser to returning light. They built a setup capable of sending a much larger fraction of reflected light back to the device than had been used in earlier measurements, and observed the onset of coherence collapse at around −6.7 dB, equivalent to a 21.4% return under the conditions defined in the paper.

Besides measuring the laser’s behaviour, they tested transmission at 10 Gb/s close to the observed limit and studied stability for more than 100 hours. The results encourage the idea that some isolators might eventually be unnecessary in certain circuits. The threshold, however, depends on the source and on how reflection occurs, so it cannot simply be transferred to every integrated design.

A photosynapse sensitive to the direction of light

[2] S. Zhang et al., “Polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision sensing enabled by pristine black arsenic-phosphorus,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 100, Feb. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-025-02125-0. Date in Crossref Open access.

Intensity is not the only property of light we can measure. Polarization also carries information about a scene, although incorporating it into a sensor usually requires additional components. Zhang and colleagues exploit the directional response of a sheet of black arsenic phosphorus to combine polarization detection and synaptic behaviour in a phototransistor.

The device responds differently according to the orientation of the polarization, and its response can also be changed through the gate voltage and light pulses. This combination makes it possible to emulate forms of plasticity, or changes in response that depend on previous stimulation. In measurements with 1550 nm light, the authors obtain a polarization ratio of 4.66 and estimate consumption of around 25 pJ per pulse under the conditions specified in the paper.

The participating institutions include the University of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, both in China. The team combines the measured responses with a hybrid optoelectronic network to test classification and reconstruction tasks. This device demonstration should be distinguished from a complete neuromorphic camera, since the polarization image was obtained by scanning and integrated arrays remain a task for future work.

Noise as a window into phase-change memory

[3] S. Walfort, X. T. Vu, J. Ballmaier, N. Holle, N. Vollmar, and M. Salinga, “A free energy landscape analysis of resistance fluctuations in a memristive device,” Nat. Mater., vol. 25, no. 4, pp. 643–650, Jan. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02487-9. Date in Crossref Open access.

Phase-change memory stores information by exploiting the different resistances a material can have depending on its structure. At nanometre scales, however, that resistance does not always remain still. Small internal rearrangements produce fluctuations that can make reading harder, but they also contain clues about what atoms are doing in a disordered material.

In a study from the University of Münster and RWTH Aachen University, in Germany, Walfort and colleagues analyse those fluctuations in a small amorphous region of germanium telluride. Using a hidden Markov model, they identify jumps between resistance states and track how their rates change with temperature. The observed variation suggests that assigning a single energy barrier is not enough to describe the transitions, because entropic contributions also matter.

The distinction matters because the amorphous state is out of equilibrium and evolves over time, contributing to resistance drift and noise in memories and in-memory computing systems. The paper does not offer a direct solution to these problems, but it does provide a way to study individual processes that are often hidden when only an averaged signal is observed.
