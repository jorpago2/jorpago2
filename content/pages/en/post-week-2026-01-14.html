Week of January 14, 2026

On-demand photons and two ways to test materials with heat

One way to judge a quantum source is to ask how closely two successive photons match. Heat is the test in the other two papers. It can stabilise a memory during fabrication and degrade a solar cell that heats and cools in operation.

Photonics

Photons that arrive on cue and match one another

[1] N. Hauser et al., “Deterministic and highly indistinguishable single photons in the telecom C-band,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 537, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68336-0. Open access.

Two photons from the same source can fail to interfere if their frequencies or pulse shapes differ. To generate photons on demand in the telecommunications C band, researchers at the University of Stuttgart and the University of Würzburg in Germany place an indium arsenide quantum dot inside a circular Bragg resonator.

The team compares several ways of exciting the dot and obtains a two-photon interference visibility of 91.7% with phonon-assisted excitation. The measurement indicates how closely successive photons match, a requirement for combining them in a quantum circuit. A network built from separate emitters would still need to establish that photons from different sources are indistinguishable too.

Electronics

A more stable memory after annealing

[2] R. Ramesh et al., “Atomic layer deposited HfZrO4-based memristive devices: annealing effect and multilevel storage capability,” Frontiers in Nanotechnology, vol. 8, Art. no. 1729291, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.3389/fnano.2026.1729291. Open access.

Participating institutions University of Edinburgh (United Kingdom) and Indian Institute of Technology Patna (India).

In a resistive memory, the state is stored in the resistance of a very thin layer, the value of which can change when voltage is applied. Ramesh and their collaborators compare devices with a layer of hafnium zirconium oxide of about 10 nanometers, placed between titanium nitride electrodes, to find out what happens when that layer is heated after being manufactured. The treatment, carried out at 400 °C for one minute, modifies the structure of the material and, according to the authors' measurements, also the chemical composition of the area close to the electrodes.

The difference appears when repeating the switching. After one hundred sweeps, the area enclosed by the hysteresis curve decreased by about 73.5% in the untreated devices, while the reduction was about 21–23% in the treated ones. However, this comparison has a trade-off, as the heated devices required a higher voltage for initial electroforming and appreciable switching also occurred at higher voltages. Additionally, the cycling tests were done with different amplitudes, so the percentages describe those specific conditions and should not simply be extrapolated to the useful life of the device.

The authors also observed various resistance states after applying pulses of different amplitudes, a property that could be useful for representing more than two values ​​in a memory. For now, the work demonstrates characteristics of individual devices and retention measured over minutes, so it remains to be seen how they would behave in larger arrays, over more cycles, and when integrated into a computing circuit.

Materials

What happens to a perovskite cell as it heats and cools

[3] K. Sun et al., “Insights into the operational stability of wide-bandgap perovskite and tandem solar cells under rapid thermal cycling,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 596, Jan. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68219-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

A solar cell changes temperature during operation, and the resulting strain in the film can affect the current it delivers. The team puts wide-bandgap perovskite cells through rapid cycles, using X-rays and photoluminescence to follow the material as the cells operate.

Under these conditions, a sharp initial drop in performance gives way to slower degradation. The measurements link the decline to strain, phase changes and recombination losses. The cycles are deliberately accelerated to bring those degradation mechanisms into view.
