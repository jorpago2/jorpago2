Week of January 14, 2026

On-demand photons and two ways to test materials with heat

Two photons from the same source can still be told apart by when they arrive. How would you test that if their colours look identical to your detectors? We'll also turn up the heat on two devices. A minute in the oven helps a memory, while repeated trips from cold to hot take their toll on a solar cell.

Photonics

Photons that arrive on cue and match one another

[1] N. Hauser et al., “Deterministic and highly indistinguishable single photons in the telecom C-band,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 537, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68336-0.

Suppose you ask for two identical photons and they give you two that have come, one after the other, from the same quantum dot. How do you check the order? The universities of University of Stuttgart and University of Würzburg, in Germany, use an indium arsenide dot inside a circular Bragg resonator to broadcast in the telecommunications C-band. They share the emitter, but between one shot and the next the frequency or arrival time may vary slightly.

The trick is to delay the first photon until the two meet in a beam splitter. The more similar they are in everything that could distinguish them, the more they will tend to leave together through the same port. This is seen in the detectors as a drop in coincidences.

With phonon-assisted excitation, the visibility of that interference reaches 91.7%. It is a measurement between neighbours over time. If tomorrow we want to connect two nodes of a network, we will have to repeat the question with a photon from each emitter.

Related reading. Bright Source of Purcell‐Enhanced, Triggered, Single Photons in the Telecom C‐Band. Advanced Quantum Technologies (2023).

Electronics

A more stable memory after annealing

[2] R. Ramesh et al., “Atomic layer deposited HfZrO4-based memristive devices: annealing effect and multilevel storage capability,” Frontiers in Nanotechnology, vol. 8, Art. no. 1729291, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.3389/fnano.2026.1729291.

A modest request for a memory would be to tell the same two states apart on Tuesday that it could distinguish on Monday. To see where this separation begins to be lost, Ramesh and colleagues, from University of Edinburgh in the United Kingdom and Indian Institute of Technology Patna in India, compare two batches. Both have about 10 nm of hafnium zirconium oxide between titanium nitride electrodes, but one also spends a minute at 400 °C.

Heating changes the structure and chemistry near the contacts. After one hundred voltage sweeps, the area of the hysteresis loop has been reduced by 73.5% in the untreated batch. In the one that went through the oven, the reduction is between 21 and 23%.

You can also type in various resistance levels with pulses of different widths and follow them for minutes. The beauty of comparing the batches is that you can now see the effect of the treatment without confusing those minutes of monitoring with a years-long retention test.

Related reading. Electrical Analysis of Atomic Layer Deposited Thin HfO2 and HfO2/Ta2O5-Based Memristive Devices. IEEE Transactions on Electron Devices (2025).

Materials

What happens to a perovskite cell as it heats and cools

[3] K. Sun et al., “Insights into the operational stability of wide-bandgap perovskite and tandem solar cells under rapid thermal cycling,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 596, Jan. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68219-w.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

A solar cell does not spend the day at the temperature at which its efficiency was measured in the laboratory. It heats up, cools down and, as it has several layers attached, each one tries to expand in its own way. At what point does this begin to be noticed in the current? The team accelerates the cycling of a broadband perovskite cell and follows the structure with X-rays and the emission with photoluminescence as the cell operates.

The fall has two rhythms. At first, about 60% of the initial performance is lost, even in surface-treated samples. Afterwards the degradation continues more slowly. Deformation, phase changes, and charge recombination help explain evolution.

If they had only taken one measure at the beginning and another at the end, we would have a loss percentage and few clues about when to intervene. Following the cell during the cycles serves just that. Translating accelerated testing to years of outdoor use is another question.

Related reading. Morphological Insights into the Degradation of Perovskite Solar Cells under Light and Humidity. ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces (2023).
