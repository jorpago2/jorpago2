Week of January 14, 2026

On-demand photons and two ways to test materials with heat

A quantum dot emits photons one at a time, but making two of them interfere also depends on how closely they match. Repetition has a different purpose in the other experiments. One team heats a memory before writing it many times, while another cycles the temperature of a solar cell to follow the onset of degradation.

Photonics

Photons that arrive on cue and match one another

[1] N. Hauser et al., “Deterministic and highly indistinguishable single photons in the telecom C-band,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 537, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68336-0.

Successive photons from a single quantum dot need not be indistinguishable, since their frequency or temporal profile can vary between emissions. To generate photons on demand in the telecommunications C band, researchers at the University of Stuttgart and the University of Würzburg in Germany place an indium arsenide quantum dot inside a circular Bragg resonator.

The team compares several ways of exciting the dot and obtains a two-photon interference visibility of 91.7% with phonon-assisted excitation. The measurement indicates how closely successive photons match, a requirement for combining them in a quantum circuit. A network built from separate emitters would still need to establish that photons from different sources are indistinguishable too.

The comparison sends successive photons into the two inputs of a beam splitter. If their arrival times and other properties match, they tend to leave together through the same port. Varying the delay and counting coincidences between the detectors measures that resemblance even when neither detector can distinguish the photons by colour.

Related reading. Bright Source of Purcell‐Enhanced, Triggered, Single Photons in the Telecom C‐Band. Advanced Quantum Technologies (2023).

Electronics

A more stable memory after annealing

[2] R. Ramesh et al., “Atomic layer deposited HfZrO4-based memristive devices: annealing effect and multilevel storage capability,” Frontiers in Nanotechnology, vol. 8, Art. no. 1729291, Jan. 14, 2026, doi 10.3389/fnano.2026.1729291.

Ramesh and colleagues heat a roughly 10 nm hafnium zirconium oxide layer between titanium nitride electrodes at 400 °C for one minute. Both its structure and the chemistry near the contacts change. The question is whether its memory states then remain easier to distinguish over repeated voltage sweeps.

The difference appears when repeating the switching. After one hundred sweeps, the area enclosed by the hysteresis curve decreased by about 73.5% in the untreated devices, while the reduction was about 21–23% in the treated ones. However, this comparison has a trade-off, as the heated devices required a higher voltage for initial electroforming and appreciable switching also occurred at higher voltages. Additionally, the cycling tests were done with different amplitudes, so the percentages describe those specific conditions and should not simply be extrapolated to the useful life of the device.

Pulses of different amplitudes also write several resistance levels into a single device, which the authors follow for minutes. This answers a different question from the hundred sweeps. One test asks how long the written values remain distinguishable, while the other measures how the device changes with repeated writing.

Participating institutions University of Edinburgh (United Kingdom) and Indian Institute of Technology Patna (India).

Related reading. Electrical Analysis of Atomic Layer Deposited Thin HfO2 and HfO2/Ta2O5-Based Memristive Devices. IEEE Transactions on Electron Devices (2025).

Materials

What happens to a perovskite cell as it heats and cools

[3] K. Sun et al., “Insights into the operational stability of wide-bandgap perovskite and tandem solar cells under rapid thermal cycling,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 596, Jan. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68219-w.

Participating institutions
Technical University of Munich (Germany)

As a perovskite solar cell heats and cools, its layers expand and contract, placing the film under strain even as it continues to deliver current. The team puts wide-bandgap perovskite cells through rapid cycles, using X-rays and photoluminescence to follow the material as the cells operate.

Under these conditions, a sharp initial drop in performance gives way to slower degradation. The measurements link the decline to strain, phase changes and recombination losses. The cycles are deliberately accelerated to bring those degradation mechanisms into view.

About 60% of the initial performance is lost during the first stage, even in samples whose surfaces had been treated to reduce losses. Measuring only before and after all the cycles would have combined that early drop with the subsequent gradual decline, obscuring the fact that the two periods call for different diagnoses.

Related reading. Morphological Insights into the Degradation of Perovskite Solar Cells under Light and Humidity. ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces (2023).
