Week of August 19, 2026

A shortcut for light, event-based vision and colour from infrared

A path that carries part of a signal past an operation can matter as much to an optical network as the operation itself. A sensor that registers changes in a scene also selects what to transmit, while a set of perovskites offers a choice of visible colours under infrared excitation.

Photonics

A shortcut connection inside a photonic chip

[1] Z. Quan et al., “On-chip non-volatile all-optical residual neural network accelerator,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 348, Aug. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02325-2.

The output of a neural network stage can be combined with a copy of its input, which means keeping that copy available while the calculation takes place. Residual connections allow part of the signal to take a more direct path and then be combined with the processed result. Quan et al, from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in China, have taken that idea to an integrated photonic circuit that performs convolutions and preserves its weights without continuous power.

The device divides the information between different wavelengths. Four channels perform the convolution and a fifth carries the shortcut signal, so the contributions can be added to the output. To adjust the weights, the researchers turn to a small region of phase-change material, whose transmission changes when its state is changed by optical pulses. The team tests the operation with images and demonstrates fast residual convolution, as well as keeping two programming states separate after 300 cycles.

The demonstration also allows you to see how much work goes into the chip itself. Images are prepared and encoded with external electronics and modulators, while detection and other layers of the network are performed electronically. Therefore, although photonic operation is the center of the experiment, the result is not yet equivalent to a complete neural network functioning autonomously with light.

Electronics

A transistor array that detects when a scene changes

[2] Y. Zhai et al., “Amphibian-inspired neuromorphic dynamic vision systems based on ferroelectric field-effect transistor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8641, Aug. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74769-4.

Participating institutions
Shenzhen University (China)

A conventional camera keeps sending frames of a stationary scene even when almost every pixel repeats its previous reading. The team combines ferroelectric transistors sensitive to several wavelengths and arranges their signals to strengthen local contrast before processing the image.

The transistors respond with pulses on a microsecond timescale. Recognition tasks test the sensor and algorithm together. The device contribution is that some of the signal preparation takes place in the array that collects it.

When an object crosses the field of view, the stationary background occupies many pixels but offers few clues about the trajectory. Encoding changes as spikes concentrates activity at locations the object has just passed. Recognition then depends on how the spikes are combined over time, even if each individual transistor responds quickly.

Related reading. Integrated In‐Memory Sensor and Computing of Artificial Vision Based on Full‐vdW Optoelectronic Ferroelectric Field‐Effect Transistor. Advanced Science (2023).

Materials

Perovskites that convert infrared to color

[3] Y. Xu et al., “Giant anomalous exciton-multiphoton nonlinearities in layered hybrid perovskites,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 360, Aug. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02286-6.

A material illuminated in the infrared can emit a visible photon by drawing the necessary energy from several incident photons absorbed together. This conversion is attractive for working at infrared wavelengths, although high-order responses are often weak. Xu et al have studied three layered hybrid perovskites in which the confinement of the excitons favors a particularly strong multiphoton response.

The crystals combine inorganic and organic layers, and each composition produces a different emission in red, green or blue. By exciting materials in the infrared, researchers identify absorption processes that reach up to six photons and observe that the emitted intensity depends on the orientation of the polarization. To explain this behavior, they propose a mechanism based on the dynamics of excitonic dipoles and contrast it with simulations.

The study brings together the universities of Fuzhou, Nanjing and Nanjing University of Science and Technology, in China. The excitation spans two infrared bands, 1550–1870 nm and 2100–2300 nm. Under laser illumination the materials emit visible light by absorbing several photons. A biological imaging application would raise two further questions about how much irradiance the specimen tolerates and how to isolate it from the lead in all three compositions.
