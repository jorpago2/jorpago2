Week of August 19, 2026

A shortcut for light, event-based vision and colour from infrared

One chip keeps a copy of its optical input to add back after a calculation, while an array watching a moving object singles out what has just changed. Three layered perovskites do something quite different with the light that reaches them, pooling the energy of several infrared photons to emit a visible one.

Photonics

A shortcut connection inside a photonic chip

[1] Z. Quan et al., “On-chip non-volatile all-optical residual neural network accelerator,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 348, Aug. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02325-2.

Sometimes it is interesting to process an image and at the same time preserve the information that was at the beginning. In software, it is enough to save a copy before applying the filter. In the chip presented by Quan et al, from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in China, that copy needs a physical path along which it can arrive in time to be added to the result of the convolution.

The device divides the information between different wavelengths. Four channels perform the convolution and a fifth carries the shortcut signal, so the contributions can be added to the output. To adjust the weights, the researchers turn to a small region of phase-change material, whose transmission changes when its state is changed by optical pulses. The team tests the operation with images and demonstrates fast residual convolution, as well as keeping two programming states separate after 300 cycles.

If we followed an image throughout the assembly, we would go through conventional electronics several times. Off-chip input is prepared and modulated, and sensing and other layers of the network are also electronic. What is gathered in the photonic part is a concrete operation, the convolution with that path back to the input, and the possibility of conserving the weights without continuously supplying power.

Electronics

A transistor array that detects when a scene changes

[2] Y. Zhai et al., “Amphibian-inspired neuromorphic dynamic vision systems based on ferroelectric field-effect transistor,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 8641, Aug. 19, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-74769-4.

Participating institutions
Shenzhen University (China)

Think of a camera looking at a wall while someone fleetingly crosses the scene. Sending the entire wall dozens of times does little to reconstruct where that person went. In this matrix, the pixels that see a change respond with pulses, so both their position and the instant at which they appear are of interest.

The team uses ferroelectric transistors sensitive to various wavelengths and combines their signals to enhance local contrast. The response of the transistors comes on the scale of microseconds, so part of the preparation begins where the light is collected.

Recognizing a trajectory, on the other hand, requires combining the pulse here with the one that appeared a little later further away. Therefore, when reading the recognition results, it is advisable to attribute them to the sensor and the algorithm that recomposes that sequence. The speed of a transistor tells us in what temporal detail we can begin to tell the story.

Related reading. Integrated In‐Memory Sensor and Computing of Artificial Vision Based on Full‐vdW Optoelectronic Ferroelectric Field‐Effect Transistor. Advanced Science (2023).

Materials

Perovskites that convert infrared to color

[3] Y. Xu et al., “Giant anomalous exciton-multiphoton nonlinearities in layered hybrid perovskites,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 360, Aug. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02286-6.

If we illuminate a sample in the infrared and see that green light comes out, the extra energy has to come from somewhere. In the perovskites studied Xu et al, several excitation photons occur in the same transition. What is striking, given the low probability that these processes usually have, is how far they go when confining the excitons between layers.

The crystals combine inorganic and organic layers, and each composition produces a different emission in red, green or blue. By exciting materials in the infrared, researchers identify absorption processes that reach up to six photons and observe that the emitted intensity depends on the orientation of the polarization. To explain this behavior, they propose a mechanism based on the dynamics of excitonic dipoles and contrast it with simulations.

At the universities of Fuzhou, Nanjing and Nanjing University of Science and Technology, in China, they work between 1550 and 1870 nm and between 2100 and 2300 nm. These are intervals that invite one to think about obtaining images with infrared excitation, although a biological sample would add a couple of quite earthly conditions. It would be necessary to check how much irradiance it supports and how it would be kept separate from the lead contained in the three materials.

Related reading. 2D Perovskites with Giant Excitonic Optical Nonlinearities for High‐Performance Sub‐Bandgap Photodetection. Advanced Materials (2019).
