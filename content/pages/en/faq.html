Prospective PhD students

PhD in Spain: practical questions

A PhD is a research training period and a professional commitment. The right decision depends on your interests, expectations, supervisor, funding and working environment.

This page reflects my personal experience, not legal advice. Funding and employment conditions change, so always check the current call, your contract and the applicable collective agreement.

Is a PhD useful outside academia?

Yes, particularly for roles involving advanced R&D, specialist technical work and complex problem-solving. However, a PhD does not guarantee a particular job or salary.

A strong doctoral experience should give you opportunities to:

  • Work on an emerging scientific or technological problem.
  • Develop rigorous analytical and experimental skills.
  • Use advanced tools and methods.
  • Manage uncertainty, projects and technical communication.
Evidence, not a promise

Employment outcomes vary with discipline, sector, experience and economic context. OECD indicators are useful context, but they should not be interpreted as a guarantee that a doctorate will produce a better position.

An academic career is also possible, but it is long and highly competitive. Investigate the career structure, mobility requirements and available positions before treating it as the only acceptable outcome.

How should I choose a topic and supervisor?

Choose a topic that can sustain your curiosity for several years while helping you develop methods and skills that remain useful beyond one project.

Before committing, discuss:

  • How often you will meet and how feedback will be provided.
  • The project scope, available resources, funding and expected timeline.
  • Publication, authorship, data management and conference expectations.
  • The group culture, working practices and career paths of former students.

Previous experience with the supervisor through a BSc or MSc project can help, but it is not essential. Speak with current or former group members and arrange a substantive meeting. Supervision is a mutual decision, and no single meeting schedule is appropriate for every project.

How is a PhD funded in Spain?

Many public predoctoral schemes fund a full-time employment contract with the host university or research organisation. Under Article 21 of the Spanish Science Act, the researcher is an employee and the host institution is the employer.

  1. Identify a suitable supervisor and host research group.
  2. Check current national, regional, university and project-linked calls.
  3. Prepare the academic record, research proposal and other documents required by the specific call.
  4. If selected, the host institution formalises the employment contract.

Eligibility, duration, salary, selection criteria, deadlines and application limits vary between calls. The process can take several months, so it is sensible to begin discussions during your master’s studies.

Pay already received normally corresponds to work already performed. Leaving the doctoral programme ends the employment relationship, but notice periods and administrative consequences must be checked in the contract and funding call.

What working conditions should I expect?

A predoctoral contract is an employment relationship, not an expectation of working continuously or giving up holidays.

Working hours, rest periods, holidays and leave are determined by the collective agreement applicable to the employer. If no collective agreement applies, the Spanish Workers’ Statute governs these conditions. There is therefore no universal weekly schedule that can be stated for every PhD contract.

Occasional deadline pressure can occur in research, but workload expectations should be explicit, sustainable and discussed with the supervisor. Regular unpaid overtime should not be treated as a normal requirement of doctoral training.

Which non-technical skills matter?

These abilities support good research, but they are skills to develop rather than fixed requirements you must already master.

  • CommunicationSynthesise decisions, problems and results clearly.
  • TeamworkCollaborate reliably and contribute to shared goals.
  • AdaptabilityRevise plans when evidence or experiments challenge expectations.
  • Self-managementWork independently while remaining organised, responsible and open to feedback.
