Flat bands, artificial vision and crystals that remember

From February 4 to 10, 2026

Photonics

When a flat band helps mix light and magnetism

[1] Q. Hong, J. Qian, F. Chen, Y. Yang, and Y.-P. Wang, “Strong magnon–photon coupling enhanced by photonic lattice flat-bands,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2438, Feb. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69326-y. Open access.

Getting an electromagnetic wave to exchange energy with a magnetic material is relatively simple. Much more delicate is to make this exchange intense enough so that both stop behaving as independent systems and form hybrid states. The difficulty increases when the photonic structure grows, since the field tends to spread out and the local interaction can weaken.

A team from Zhejiang University and East China Normal University, both in China, has studied a way to address this problem using a network of resonators with a flat band. Many modes with similar frequencies appear in it that, when combined, can concentrate their interaction with a small sphere of yttrium iron garnet. The researchers tune the magnetic response of the sphere with an external field and observe the coupling with the electromagnetic modes of the lattice.

In the experiment, carried out with microwaves, the separation between the hybrid modes reaches 180 MHz for a network of twelve cells, compared to 92 MHz in the comparison structure. Furthermore, the authors observe that the coupling associated with the flat band is maintained as the network size increases. It is a demonstration of wave physics, still far from an optical integrated circuit, but it helps to understand how the collective design of a structure can sustain a local interaction that would otherwise be diluted.

Electronics

A neuron that sees and a synapse that stores

[2] J. Wang et al., “Homogeneous integration of two-dimensional material-based optoelectronic neurons and ferroelectric synapses for neuromorphic vision,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2538, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68905-3. Open access.

In conventional vision systems, information typically travels from the sensor to a separate processor and memory. Bringing some of that work to the device itself could reduce data movements, although integrating sensing and calculation poses a practical problem, as each function often requires different materials and processes.

Researchers from Peking University and Peking Jiaotong University, China, have explored a shared platform based on molybdenum disulfide. Its phototransistor converts light pulses into signals that feed an electronic neuron, while ferroelectric transistors made from the same semiconductor act as synapses whose weights can be modified. In this way, the system links detection with calculation operations that resemble some of those used in spiking neural networks.

The work presents an experimental array and also uses simulations to study broader recognition tasks. That distinction matters, because the percentages obtained in the tasks do not alone describe a complete commercial sensor. Still, combining devices on a common platform offers an interesting way to investigate where processing should be placed when information arrives in the form of light.

Materials

Tune a ferroelectric to save more states

[3] T. Yang et al., “Phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der Waals crystal,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2546, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69272-9. Open access.

A ferroelectric material retains a polarization that can be changed with an electric field, a property that is attractive for storing information. However, when we want to represent many intermediate values, as happens in some neuromorphic devices, it is also important that this change can be controlled gradually.

A team from the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, in Saudi Arabia, together with researchers from the Southwest Jiaotong University and the Sichuan University, in China, has studied CuInP₂(S₁₋ₓSeₓ)₆ crystals whose composition allows modifying the coexistence of structural phases. By adjusting the proportion of sulfur and selenium, the authors find a region with relaxor ferroelectric behavior, associated with small polarized regions and a more gradual response.

The comparison of devices shows that the relaxor crystal admits more analog resistance states and operates at lower voltages than the conventional ferroelectric materials used as reference in the study. Although there is still a way to go to evaluate its integration and behavior on a large scale, the result illustrates why the internal structure of the material can be as important for a memory as the geometry of the device that contains it.
