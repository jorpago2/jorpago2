Week of February 4, 2026

A two-cycle pulse, a light-sensitive synapse and multilevel memory

This week’s infrared pulse barely lasts two oscillations of the electric field. For the photosynapse and the ferroelectric memory, the interesting measurements come after excitation. One device retains information about the polarization of the light, while the other needs distinguishable intermediate resistance levels that can be written again.

Photonics

A light pulse just two cycles long

[1] R. M. Gray et al., “Two-optical-cycle pulses from nanophotonic two-color soliton compression,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 107, Feb. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02187-8.

Participating institutions
California Institute of Technology (United States)

Inside the lithium niobate guide, the infrared pulse generates light at twice its frequency and continues to exchange energy with it as the two travel. Adjusting the guide’s dispersion lets the two components evolve together so the pulse shortens.

The experiment yields pulses of about 13 fs at 2 µm from about 3 pJ of input energy. At that wavelength, 13 fs amounts to roughly two cycles of the field. Reaching a single cycle is a possibility the paper explores separately.

Electronics

A transistor that remembers the polarization of light

[2] J. Huo et al., “Coupled ferroelectric-anisotropic optoelectronic synapse for polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1468, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68206-1.

A pixel could register a property of the incoming light and retain the result for the next stage of computation. Huo and his collaborators focus on a property that is often left out of a conventional image, polarization, and build a device that can respond differently depending on the orientation of the light it receives.

To do this, they combine a rhenium disulfide channel, ReS₂, whose optical response depends on the direction within the crystal, with a hafnium and zirconium oxide ferroelectric gate. The structure also incorporates a floating gate, which helps control the interaction between the layers. Thus, both the illumination and the electrical signal allow the device's conductance to be modified and preserve states that can be used as weights of an artificial synapse.

The authors estimate an energy of about 2 femtojoules per synaptic operation for the specific conditions of optical programming and electrical erasure that they studied. Additionally, they used a three-by-three array of devices to extract polarization-sensitive image features and combined their results with a neural network. It is convenient to separate this demonstration from a complete neuromorphic camera, because the recognition system and its energy needs are not reduced to the figure of the individual device.

The interest lies in the fact that the same structure brings together detection, state and a first processing operation, while the anisotropy of the material adds information that an intensity measurement alone does not provide.\n\nThe work brings together researchers from the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the Tsinghua University, in China.

Materials

Tune a ferroelectric to save more states

[3] T. Yang et al., “Phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der Waals crystal,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2546, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69272-9.

A ferroelectric memory relies on the polarization persisting after the field used to write it is removed. However, when we want to represent many intermediate values, as happens in some neuromorphic devices, it is also important that this change can be controlled gradually.

A team from the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, in Saudi Arabia, together with researchers from the Southwest Jiaotong University and the Sichuan University, in China, has studied CuInP₂(S₁₋ₓSeₓ)₆ crystals whose composition allows modifying the coexistence of structural phases. By adjusting the proportion of sulfur and selenium, the authors find a region with relaxor ferroelectric behavior, associated with small polarized regions and a more gradual response.

With the relaxor crystal in a memristor, the team can distinguish more resistance levels and write them at lower voltages than in the reference devices. Those intermediate levels are the values an analogue memory needs to store, which gives a reason to favour a material whose polarization can change by degrees.
