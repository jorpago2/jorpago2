Week of February 4, 2026

A two-cycle pulse, a light-sensitive synapse and multilevel memory

Sometimes the aim is to make a response as short as possible, as with this week’s pulse compressed to two cycles. For processing light or storing information, the question is which states a synapse can distinguish and which ones a ferroelectric can retain.

Photonics

A light pulse just two cycles long

[1] R. M. Gray et al., “Two-optical-cycle pulses from nanophotonic two-color soliton compression,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 107, Feb. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02187-8. Open access.

Participating institutions
California Institute of Technology (United States)

To compress a pulse, the researchers design a lithium niobate waveguide in which the input light and its second harmonic interact as they travel. Adjusting the guide’s dispersion lets the two components evolve together so the pulse shortens.

The experiment yields pulses of about 13 fs at 2 µm from about 3 pJ of input energy. At that wavelength, 13 fs amounts to roughly two cycles of the field. Reaching a single cycle is a possibility the paper explores separately.

Electronics

A synapse that distinguishes the orientation of light

[2] J. Huo et al., “Coupled ferroelectric-anisotropic optoelectronic synapse for polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1468, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68206-1. Open access.

The work brings together researchers from the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the Tsinghua University, in China.

A camera records light and then sends the data elsewhere for processing, but there are alternatives that attempt to do some of that work on the sensor itself. Huo and his collaborators focus on a property that is often left out of a conventional image, polarization, and build a device that can respond differently depending on the orientation of the light it receives.

To do this, they combine a rhenium disulfide channel, ReS₂, whose optical response depends on the direction within the crystal, with a hafnium and zirconium oxide ferroelectric gate. The structure also incorporates a floating gate, which helps control the interaction between the layers. Thus, both the illumination and the electrical signal allow the device's conductance to be modified and preserve states that can be used as weights of an artificial synapse.

The authors estimate an energy of about 2 femtojoules per synaptic operation for the specific conditions of optical programming and electrical erasure that they studied. Additionally, they used a three-by-three array of devices to extract polarization-sensitive image features and combined their results with a neural network. It is convenient to separate this demonstration from a complete neuromorphic camera, because the recognition system and its energy needs are not reduced to the figure of the individual device.

The interest lies in the fact that the same structure brings together detection, state and a first processing operation, while the anisotropy of the material adds information that an intensity measurement alone does not provide. The next question is how to translate this behavior, observed in a small matrix, to larger and more uniform sets.

Materials

Tune a ferroelectric to save more states

[3] T. Yang et al., “Phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der Waals crystal,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2546, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69272-9. Open access.

A ferroelectric material retains a polarization that can be changed with an electric field, a property that is attractive for storing information. However, when we want to represent many intermediate values, as happens in some neuromorphic devices, it is also important that this change can be controlled gradually.

A team from the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, in Saudi Arabia, together with researchers from the Southwest Jiaotong University and the Sichuan University, in China, has studied CuInP₂(S₁₋ₓSeₓ)₆ crystals whose composition allows modifying the coexistence of structural phases. By adjusting the proportion of sulfur and selenium, the authors find a region with relaxor ferroelectric behavior, associated with small polarized regions and a more gradual response.

The comparison of devices shows that the relaxor crystal admits more analog resistance states and operates at lower voltages than the conventional ferroelectric materials used as reference in the study. Although there is still a way to go to evaluate its integration and behavior on a large scale, the result illustrates why the internal structure of the material can be as important for a memory as the geometry of the device that contains it.
