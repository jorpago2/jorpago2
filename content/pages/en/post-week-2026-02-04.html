Week of February 4, 2026

A two-cycle pulse, a light-sensitive synapse and multilevel memory

At a wavelength of two micrometres, thirteen femtoseconds is barely enough time for the field to oscillate twice. We'll see how to make a pulse that short in a waveguide, then visit two devices built to hold on to something after the stimulus has gone, one to the polarisation of light and the other to several resistance levels.

Photonics

A light pulse just two cycles long

[1] R. M. Gray et al., “Two-optical-cycle pulses from nanophotonic two-color soliton compression,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 107, Feb. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02187-8.

Participating institutions
California Institute of Technology (United States)

In thirteen femtoseconds, how many times does light have time to oscillate? It depends on the colour. At 2 µm the field manages roughly two cycles. That is the full duration of the pulse leaving this lithium niobate waveguide. To get there, the team takes advantage of a wave that the pulse itself generates along the way, at a frequency twice as high as its own.

Second-harmonic conversion also explains why the waveguide width matters. If the two components drift apart in time, they stop exchanging energy over much of their path. Keeping them together lets that length contribute to pulse compression at an input energy of only a few picojoules, without resorting to a more energetic laser.

With about 3 pJ input they already obtain 13 fs in the experiment. It is tempting to round off the story and say that the single cycle has been reached, but there we would change ground. Two oscillations is the result that was measured. Going down to one is the possibility they explore separately, and that last swing counts for a lot when you only had two left.

Related reading. Soliton-effect compression of picosecond pulses on a photonic chip. Optics Letters (2021).

Electronics

A transistor that remembers the polarization of light

[2] J. Huo et al., “Coupled ferroelectric-anisotropic optoelectronic synapse for polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1468, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68206-1.

Imagine a pixel that, after being illuminated, could tell you something about the orientation of the electric field of light. For the answer to depend on that orientation, you would have to give the material some way to distinguish directions. Huo and colleagues at National University of Singapore in Singapore and Tsinghua University in China choose rhenium disulfide, a crystal that already has that preference.

To do this, they combine a rhenium disulfide channel, ReS₂, whose optical response depends on the direction within the crystal, with a hafnium and zirconium oxide ferroelectric gate. The structure also incorporates a floating gate, which helps control the interaction between the layers. Thus, both the illumination and the electrical signal allow the device's conductance to be modified and preserve states that can be used as weights of an artificial synapse.

In a three-by-three element matrix they extract features from polarisation images and combine them with a neural network. When reading the figure of about 2 fJ per operation, it is worth looking at what has been counted. The estimate corresponds to a synaptic operation under the conditions of optical writing and electrical erasure of the trial. Reading the nine elements and recognizing an entire image also involves the rest of the system.

Related reading. Ferroelectric Polarization Enhanced Optoelectronic Synaptic Response of a CuInP 2 S 6 Transistor Structure. ACS Nano (2024).

Materials

Tune a ferroelectric to save more states

[3] T. Yang et al., “Phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der Waals crystal,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2546, Feb. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69272-9.

When writing a bit in a ferroelectric it is good that the polarisation changes decisively and stays where you left it. When you want to save an analog weight, however, a jump from one end to the other eats up all the values that were in between. Could we get different parts of the crystal to respond little by little?

A team from the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, in Saudi Arabia, together with researchers from the Southwest Jiaotong University and the Sichuan University, in China, has studied CuInP₂(S₁₋ₓSeₓ)₆ crystals whose composition allows modifying the coexistence of structural phases. By adjusting the proportion of sulfur and selenium, the authors find a region with relaxor ferroelectric behavior, associated with small polarized regions and a more gradual response.

By mounting these crystals in a memristor, the team distinguishes more levels of resistance and can write them at lower voltage than in the reference samples. I like that the important decision is made before connecting the device. Changing how much sulfur is replaced by selenium changes the size and behaviour of the polar regions and, in the end, what values you have available when you go to program the memory.

Related reading. Towards two-dimensional van der Waals ferroelectrics. Nature Materials (2023).
