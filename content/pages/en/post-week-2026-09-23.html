Week of September 23, 2026

Cavities on a fiber, graphene electrodes and ion channels

Week of September 23 to 29, 2026.

A fibre supports a chain of resonators, and two graphene edges define an electrode crossing so small that it matters which area is being quoted. In the third paper the selectivity is chemical, since a transistor in contact with water must admit ions without becoming waterlogged.

Photonics

Building a network of resonators on the surface of a fiber

[1] N. Fried, D. L. P. Vitullo, and A. Dutt, “Multiband topological heterojunctions on the surface nanoscale axial photonics platform,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10071, Sep. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76041-1.

Light can pass from each resonator to its neighbour along a chain, so varying the coupling strength changes the available optical states. Fried and his collaborators build these networks using the surface of an optical fiber, on a platform where minute variations in its effective radius create cavities along the axis.

The team couples neighboring resonators to form photonic networks inspired by the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model and studies junctions between regions with different topological properties. The platform allows the cavities to be locally adjusted after characterizing them, an advantage when small manufacturing differences shift their frequencies and make the desired coupling difficult. Furthermore, each axial mode offers another band with which to explore the response of the structure.

The demonstration serves to investigate topological photonics phenomena in a low-loss system, although a network of resonators on fiber should not be confused with a planar integrated circuit.\n\nThe work includes researchers from the University of Maryland, in the United States.

Electronics

A memristor where two graphene edges intersect

[2] Y. Liu et al., “Stress-engineered 0.34 nm graphene memristors for low-power probabilistic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11054, Sep. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77789-2.

The area where two electrodes cross can be tiny even when the complete cell, including the space needed for contacts, is considerably larger. Liu and his collaborators work with crossed graphene edges and describe a transfer procedure that controls mechanical stresses to place the sheet on vertical walls.

The two-edge geometry of a layer defines a nominal crossing region of 0.34 by 0.34 nanometers. In the devices studied, the authors observed volatile switching and a ratio between states greater than one thousand, and used their variability in a demonstration of probabilistic bits with a reported power of 370 nanowatts under the conditions tested.

The 0.34 nm on each side describes the crossing of the graphene edges. Estimating how many cells would fit on a chip would also require counting the contacts and the area needed to address each cell. In the probabilistic-bit experiment, what matters about the crossing is that its state fluctuates.\n\nThe team brings together researchers from Tsinghua University and Fudan University, in China.

Materials

Allow ions to pass without water doing the same

[3] Y. Wang et al., “Rapid-responsive and water-stable organic electrochemical transistors enabled by supramolecular artificial ion channels,” Nature Materials, Sep. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02745-w.

To record electrical activity in an aqueous medium, an electrochemical transistor can use ions in the liquid to modulate its channel current. However, water that uncontrollably penetrates the active materials can modify their structure and limit both the speed and stability of the device. Wang and his collaborators propose a layer that facilitates the passage of ions while reducing water permeation.

The material combines supramolecular stacks of crown ethers with a fluorinated polymer matrix. In their measurements, the authors obtained a response time constant of 1.3 microseconds for a specific configuration and studied the stability of the device in aqueous solutions. The work also includes an experimental demonstration of detection in an animal model, with which it explores a possible bioelectronic application.

The layer has to meet two demands that would seem to pull in opposite directions. Ions need to reach the channel to modulate its current, while water entering the semiconductor can disrupt it. The crown ethers provide a path for the ions and the fluorinated matrix limits the passage of water.\n\nAmong the participating institutions is the University of Pennsylvania, in the United States.
