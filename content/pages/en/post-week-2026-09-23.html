Week of September 23, 2026

Cavities on a fiber, graphene electrodes and ion channels

Week of September 23 to 29, 2026.

A fibre that looks smooth can hide a whole chain of cavities along its surface. We'll see how to tune them after fabrication, which part of a memory cell really does measure a fraction of a nanometre, and how to give ions a path to a submerged transistor while keeping much of the water out.

Photonics

Building a network of resonators on the surface of a fiber

[1] N. Fried, D. L. P. Vitullo, and A. Dutt, “Multiband topological heterojunctions on the surface nanoscale axial photonics platform,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10071, Sep. 29, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76041-1.

An optical fibre is usually represented to us as a thread of uniform diameter. However, a tiny variation in its effective radius is enough for light to find a cavity along that thread. Fried and colleagues, with the participation of University of Maryland, in the United States, arrange several neighbouring cavities so that light can pass from one to another.

The team couples neighboring resonators to form photonic networks inspired by the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model and studies junctions between regions with different topological properties. The platform allows the cavities to be locally adjusted after characterizing them, an advantage when small manufacturing differences shift their frequencies and make the desired coupling difficult. Furthermore, each axial mode offers another band with which to explore the response of the structure.

What anyone who does the experiment would most appreciate is being able to measure each cavity and touch it up later. When the frequencies of two neighbours are not where expected, this adjustment helps to recover the network that was intended to be studied. Thus, optical states can be searched at the border between two regions with different topological properties, taking advantage of the low losses of a structure that is still on a fibre.

Related reading. Theory of nonlinear whispering-gallery-mode dynamics in surface nanoscale axial photonics microresonators. Physical Review A (2023).

Electronics

A memristor where two graphene edges intersect

[2] Y. Liu et al., “Stress-engineered 0.34 nm graphene memristors for low-power probabilistic computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11054, Sep. 23, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77789-2.

How much of a memory fits into a fraction of a nanometre? In Liu and colleagues' samples, from Tsinghua University and Fudan University, in China, that scale corresponds to the intersection between two graphene edges. To cross them they have to transfer the sheet to vertical walls, controlling the mechanical tension that appears when placing it.

The two-edge geometry of a layer defines a nominal crossing region of 0.34 by 0.34 nanometers. In the devices studied, the authors observed volatile switching and a ratio between states greater than one thousand, and used their variability in a demonstration of probabilistic bits with a reported power of 370 nanowatts under the conditions tested.

It is worth imagining the cell seen from a little further away. The contacts and the space to access it would appear there, which also count to know how many would fit on a chip. On the other hand, for the probabilistic test the attention returns to the intersection. What in a conventional memory would take effort to avoid, the state fluctuating, is precisely what the bit they want to build needs.

Related reading. Aberration-Corrected Electron Beam Lithography at the One Nanometer Length Scale. Nano Letters (2017).

Materials

Allow ions to pass without water doing the same

[3] Y. Wang et al., “Rapid-responsive and water-stable organic electrochemical transistors enabled by supramolecular artificial ion channels,” Nature Materials, Sep. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41563-026-02745-w.

An immersed transistor that records an electrical signal has to allow ions in the liquid to approach the channel. The complication is that these ions are accompanied by water, which can alter the semiconductor and end up ruining the measurement. Wang and colleagues, with the participation of University of Pennsylvania, in the United States, are trying to give a path for ions while making it difficult for water.

The material combines supramolecular stacks of crown ethers with a fluorinated polymer matrix. In their measurements, the authors obtained a response time constant of 1.3 microseconds for a specific configuration and studied the stability of the device in aqueous solutions. The work also includes an experimental demonstration of detection in an animal model, with which it explores a possible bioelectronic application.

The crown ethers provide sites for ions to move through, while the fluorinated matrix limits water entry. This way you can understand why it is interesting to combine both parts in the same layer. The measured time constant belongs to a specific configuration, and the detection assay in an animal model addresses another question, whether this interface allows a signal to be collected in a medium of biological interest.

Related reading. Influence of Water on the Performance of Organic Electrochemical Transistors. Chemistry of Materials (2019).
