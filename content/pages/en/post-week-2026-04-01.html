Week of April 1, 2026

Polarisation in a film, avalanche in a transistor and a transition in VO₂

An optical pulse changes how a film responds to two polarizations, while an electrical pulse opens a conducting path in VO₂. Between these experiments, a ReSe₂ transistor shows why even the direction in which charges cross a crystal matters.

Photonics

A film whose response to polarisation can change

[1] W. Jaffray et al., “All-optical polarization control in time-varying low-index films via plasma symmetry breaking,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 628–636, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01886-3.

An ordinary polarizer responds the same way each time the same beam passes through, because its optical axes are fixed during fabrication. But if we want to change that response very quickly, the question shifts to the material itself and what happens in it during the first moments after receiving a pulse of light.

Wallace Jaffray and colleagues, from Heriot-Watt University in the United Kingdom, Purdue University in the United States, and the universities of Brescia and L'Aquila in Italy, have studied a film of aluminum-doped zinc oxide, a transparent conductive oxide. Near a wavelength where its permittivity approaches zero, the response of the material is especially sensitive to optical excitation.

The team illuminates a 900-nanometer film with 100 femtosecond pulses and observes how the polarization of a second pulse, used as a probe, changes. Depending on the polarization of the excitation, the film temporally acquires different responses, including birefringence and polarization rotation. The changes occur on sub-picosecond scales and the authors' model relates them to the dynamics of excited electrons.

The demonstration uses strong pulses and a laboratory setup, so the response time should not be confused with an operating speed already available in a practical circuit.

Electronics

A transistor that multiplies carriers in a ReSe₂ channel

[2] J. Zhang et al., “Low breakdown field and high ionization index in ReSe2 avalanche field-effect transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3207, Apr. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69994-w.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

When a carrier accelerated by an electric field frees another on impact, both can repeat the process and trigger an avalanche. Transport in a ReSe₂ channel depends on the crystal direction, and the team uses that difference to start multiplication at a comparatively low field.

A hafnium–zirconium oxide gate insulator improves control of the channel. The authors build a phototransistor with this structure and measure a gain of 173. Avalanche amplifies the electrical signal produced by light, which makes achieving it at a lower field useful.

Materials

VO₂ speed seen from the inside

[3] A. Pofelski et al., “Switching speed limits in electrically driven VO2 structural Mott–Peierls transition,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3139, Apr. 1, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69904-0.

In VO₂, an electrical pulse can open a conducting path through a region that was initially insulating. This transition is of interest for both electronic and photonic devices, but knowing how long it takes for the resistance to change does not tell the whole story, since the crystal structure also transforms and needs to recover before the next cycle.

Researchers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, the University of California at San Diego and other institutions in the United States, Canada and France have observed this evolution in a VO₂ film integrated into a device. To do so, they combined electrical excitation with pulsed electron microscopy and diffraction, two tools that allow us to examine how the structure responds while the switching is repeated.

At 1 MHz the first metallic nuclei appear beneath the electrodes and the front advances at about 4.54 nm per nanosecond. Shortening the interval between pulses exposes a different limit. For the switching to remain reversible, the lattice must recover its structure before the next pulse arrives.
