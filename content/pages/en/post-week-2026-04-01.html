Polarisation in a film, avalanche in a transistor and a transition in VO₂

A polariser usually arrives with its axes already set. In this film, one pulse changes what the next one sees. We'll then follow two rather different currents, an avalanche that starts more readily along one crystal direction and a conducting path that takes shape inside a film of VO₂.

Photonics

A film whose response to polarisation can change

[1] W. Jaffray et al., “All-optical polarization control in time-varying low-index films via plasma symmetry breaking,” Nature Photonics, vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 628–636, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01886-3.

Hold up a polariser, run the same beam through it twice, and you'll usually get the same light at the exit. Now, what if between the two measurements you could change the optical properties of the film with a flash? To do it very quickly, it is convenient for the material to respond during the pulse itself, without waiting to rotate a part or move the assembly axes.

Wallace Jaffray and colleagues, from Heriot-Watt University in the United Kingdom, Purdue University in the United States, and the universities of Brescia and L'Aquila in Italy, have studied a film of aluminum-doped zinc oxide, a transparent conductive oxide. Near a wavelength where its permittivity approaches zero, the response of the material is especially sensitive to optical excitation.

On a 900 nm film, the team uses 100 fs pulses and follows the polarisation of another pulse that serves as a probe. That second measurement is what gives meaning to the experiment. The variation in intensity alone could tell us that more light has been absorbed, but when reading the polarisation we can also ask which components have acquired a different phase or have been attenuated differently.

Related reading. Ultrafast All‐Optical Polarization Switching Based on Composite Metasurfaces with Gratings and an Epsilon‐Near‐Zero Film. Advanced Photonics Research (2021).

Electronics

A transistor that multiplies carriers in a ReSe₂ channel

[2] J. Zhang et al., “Low breakdown field and high ionization index in ReSe2 avalanche field-effect transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3207, Apr. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69994-w.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

An accelerated electron can release another when colliding and then there are two capable of repeating the game. For this avalanche to start, it matters how much energy each person gains before the next crash. In a ReSe₂ sheet, the path that charges find changes with the direction of the crystal. Choosing where the contacts are placed, therefore, is also choosing in which field the multiplication will begin.

A hafnium–zirconium oxide gate insulator improves control of the channel. The authors build a phototransistor with this structure and measure a gain of 173. Avalanche amplifies the electrical signal produced by light, which makes achieving it at a lower field useful.

In a phototransistor this is quite practical. The light sets off the first current and the rush amplifies it, so the device can return a considerable signal from a few initial carriers. What would have to be decided for another application is in which direction to orient the channel to have that gain at a voltage that the circuit can supply.

Related reading. Two-Dimensional Thickness-Dependent Avalanche Breakdown Phenomena in MoS2 Field-Effect Transistors under High Electric Fields. ACS Nano (2018).

Materials

VO₂ speed seen from the inside

[3] A. Pofelski et al., “Switching speed limits in electrically driven VO2 structural Mott–Peierls transition,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3139, Apr. 1, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69904-0.

Put two probes on VO₂ and you can suddenly see how its resistance drops. Now imagine that at that same moment you could look under the electrodes. Does the entire film become conductive at once or is there a small region that arrives first and then grows? The electrical measurement alone would give a jump in both cases.

Researchers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, the University of California at San Diego and other institutions in the United States, Canada and France have observed this evolution in a VO₂ film integrated into a device. To do so, they combined electrical excitation with pulsed electron microscopy and diffraction, two tools that allow us to examine how the structure responds while the switching is repeated.

At 1 MHz the first metal nuclei appear under the electrodes and the front advances about 4.54 nm per nanosecond. This local data allows us to make an important distinction for a pulse device. One thing is how long you have to wait for the first conductive region to appear and another is how long it takes for it to grow enough to join two contacts. The size of the device may change the second wait even if the material is the same.

Related reading. Propagation of insulator-to-metal transition driven by photoinduced strain waves in a Mott material. Nature Physics (2024).
