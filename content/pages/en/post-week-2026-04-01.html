Week of April 1, 2026

Light-spinning films, responsive nanowires and gold in a resonator

From April 1 to 7, 2026

A film whose response to polarisation can change

[1] W. Jaffray et al., “All-optical polarization control in time-varying low-index films via plasma symmetry breaking,” Nat. Photon., vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 628–636, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01886-3. Open Access.

The polarization of light can be manipulated with materials and structures designed to treat its components differently. But if we want to change that response very quickly, the question shifts to the material itself and what happens in it during the first moments after receiving a pulse of light.

Wallace Jaffray and colleagues, from Heriot-Watt University in the United Kingdom, Purdue University in the United States, and the universities of Brescia and L'Aquila in Italy, have studied a film of aluminum-doped zinc oxide, a transparent conductive oxide. Near a wavelength where its permittivity approaches zero, the response of the material is especially sensitive to optical excitation.

The team illuminates a 900-nanometer film with 100 femtosecond pulses and observes how the polarization of a second pulse, used as a probe, changes. Depending on the polarization of the excitation, the film temporally acquires different responses, including birefringence and polarization rotation. The changes occur on sub-picosecond scales and the authors' model relates them to the dynamics of excited electrons.

The demonstration uses strong pulses and a laboratory setup, so the response time should not be confused with an operating speed already available in a practical circuit. Even so, the study is attractive for thinking about modulators and optical surfaces whose function can be modified by light, taking advantage of a flat material without the need to record a different geometry for each response.

An artificial neuron built with nanowires

[2] J. E. Sestoft et al., “Nanoscale photonic artificial neuron with biological signal processing,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4798, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71446-4. Open Access.

When designing brain-inspired circuits, one question is how to bring together signals that can be reinforced or inhibited and then produce a nonlinear response. If these signals arrive as light, it is tempting for detection to already occur in the elements participating in the calculation, although this requires connecting optical and electrical functions on a very small scale.

Researchers at University of Copenhagen, in Denmark, and Lund University, in Sweden, have built an optoelectronic neuron from semiconductor nanowires. The structure combines two indium phosphide photodiodes connected with opposite polarities and a transistor based on indium arsenide, so that illumination modifies the signal that governs the transistor.

By illuminating the photodiodes separately, the team studies responses of opposite sign and demonstrates how optical signals combine before the nonlinear response. The test serves to explore an elementary cell and its connection possibilities, but is not yet equivalent to a complete network manufactured on a large scale. This is precisely why it is useful to look at the work on its own scale, where the relationship between geometry, illumination and electrical response can be examined in detail.

What happens when gold is implanted in a silicon ring

[3] Q.-S. Liu et al., “Shallow Au implantation into silicon-on-insulator slot ring resonator waveguide devices,” Sci Rep, vol. 16, no. 1, Art. no. 15959, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41598-026-46478-x. Open Access.

Bringing metal nanoparticles closer to an optical waveguide could strengthen the electromagnetic field in small areas and open possibilities for detection. However, the process necessary to introduce them can also damage the waveguide, so before talking about an improvement it is worth measuring what has happened to the entire resonator.

Quan-Shan Liu and colleagues have implanted gold ions in silicon-on-insulator microresonators and then applied thermal treatments to form nanoparticles. The study compares the spectra before and after processing, together with a reference device that received heat treatment without implantation, which allows us to partially distinguish the effect of the gold from that of heating alone. Among the participants is University of Manchester, in the United Kingdom.

Nanoparticles form near the surface, but the resonator response degrades considerably after some treatments, before recovering under other conditions. Furthermore, the authors do not observe evidence of ring-coupled plasmonic enhancement in their measurements. The result is useful precisely because it shows the experimental cost of a strategy that might seem attractive on paper and points out what will have to be controlled before evaluating its possible advantages for integrated sensors.
