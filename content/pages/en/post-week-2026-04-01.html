Week of April 1, 2026

Polarisation in a film, avalanche in a transistor and a transition in VO₂

A film changes its response to polarisation, and a ReSe₂ crystal multiplies charge carriers at a comparatively low field. The third paper asks how quickly VO₂ changes when the transition is measured inside the material.

Photonics

A film whose response to polarisation can change

[1] W. Jaffray et al., “All-optical polarization control in time-varying low-index films via plasma symmetry breaking,” Nat. Photon., vol. 20, no. 6, pp. 628–636, Apr. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-01886-3. Open access.

The polarization of light can be manipulated with materials and structures designed to treat its components differently. But if we want to change that response very quickly, the question shifts to the material itself and what happens in it during the first moments after receiving a pulse of light.

Wallace Jaffray and colleagues, from Heriot-Watt University in the United Kingdom, Purdue University in the United States, and the universities of Brescia and L'Aquila in Italy, have studied a film of aluminum-doped zinc oxide, a transparent conductive oxide. Near a wavelength where its permittivity approaches zero, the response of the material is especially sensitive to optical excitation.

The team illuminates a 900-nanometer film with 100 femtosecond pulses and observes how the polarization of a second pulse, used as a probe, changes. Depending on the polarization of the excitation, the film temporally acquires different responses, including birefringence and polarization rotation. The changes occur on sub-picosecond scales and the authors' model relates them to the dynamics of excited electrons.

The demonstration uses strong pulses and a laboratory setup, so the response time should not be confused with an operating speed already available in a practical circuit. Even so, the study is attractive for thinking about modulators and optical surfaces whose function can be modified by light, taking advantage of a flat material without the need to record a different geometry for each response.

Electronics

A transistor that multiplies carriers in a ReSe₂ channel

[2] J. Zhang et al., “Low breakdown field and high ionization index in ReSe2 avalanche field-effect transistors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3207, Apr. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69994-w. Open access.

Participating institutions
National University of Singapore (Singapore)

Electrical avalanche requires charges to gain enough energy between collisions to free new carriers. Transport in a ReSe₂ channel depends on the crystal direction, and the team uses that difference to start multiplication at a comparatively low field.

A hafnium–zirconium oxide gate insulator improves control of the channel. The authors build a phototransistor with this structure and measure a gain of 173. Avalanche amplifies the electrical signal produced by light, which makes achieving it at a lower field useful.

Materials

VO₂ speed seen from the inside

[3] A. Pofelski et al., “Switching speed limits in electrically driven VO2 structural Mott–Peierls transition,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3139, Apr. 1, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-69904-0. Open access.

Vanadium dioxide can change from an insulating state to a conductive state when the temperature changes or an electrical stimulus is applied. This transition is of interest for both electronic and photonic devices, but knowing how long it takes for the resistance to change does not tell the whole story, since the crystal structure also transforms and needs to recover before the next cycle.

Researchers at Brookhaven National Laboratory, the University of California at San Diego and other institutions in the United States, Canada and France have observed this evolution in a VO₂ film integrated into a device. To do so, they combined electrical excitation with pulsed electron microscopy and diffraction, two tools that allow us to examine how the structure responds while the switching is repeated.

In images obtained at 1 MHz, metallic regions appear near the electrodes and the transformation front advances at an estimated speed of 4.54 nanometers per nanosecond. On the other hand, measurements at higher frequencies make it possible to study up to several gigahertz how the recovery of the crystal lattice limits reversible switching. The speed of the transformation front and the maximum frequency are not interchangeable quantities, but together they help relate what occurs inside the material to the electrical conditions of the device. For those working with VO₂, that connection is especially valuable when comparing promises of ultra-fast switching with the repeatability that a real-world application demands.
