Week of July 1, 2026

A dispersive lens, a light-sensitive fingerprint and a magnetoelectric oxide

A red fringe in a photo is usually something to correct. Why would anyone deliberately make it worse? In one instrument it provides a way to measure distance. In the other papers, coloured lights call up a chip's electronic fingerprint and a magnetic field leaves an electrical signature in hafnium oxide.

Photonics

A metalens that makes use of dispersion

[1] Y. Wu et al., “Ultra-dispersive metasurfaces enabled by convergence-phase design using simplified nanopillar arrays,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5766, Jul. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72332-9.

If you adjust the focus until you see a red line clearly, a blue line at the same distance may be somewhat blurry. It would be good for a photographer if both colours coincided, but someone who wants to measure the height of a surface is interested in separating them. If you know the distance at which each colour focuses, just find out which one comes back from the sample.

Yunquan Wu and colleagues propose a metallens formed by simple nanopillars, whose design uses the phase of the different wavelengths to produce very high dispersion. The work involves researchers from the Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China and the Singapore University of Technology and Design, among other institutions.

There are two figures worth reading together. The surface can move along 13 mm without leaving the measurement path and, within that path, the team can distinguish an axial displacement of 50 nm. To understand the instrument, both are important, just as to choose a ruler we would want to know how far it goes and what distance there is between its marks.

Related reading. All-optical geometric image transformations enabled by ultrathin metasurfaces. Nature Communications (2023).

Electronics

An electronic fingerprint that changes with light

[2] T. Li et al., “Optically reconfigurable physical unclonable functions based on 2D MoS2 ring-oscillator arrays for attack-resistant hardware authentication,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5803, Jul. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72557-8.

Participating institutions
Hunan University (China)

In a circuit factory it is difficult to get two transistors to turn out exactly the same. Tong Li and colleagues have good reason to be happy about these small differences, because they want to distinguish one chip from another even when both are made with the same mask. To read your fingerprint, in addition to connecting the chip, they change the light.

Tong Li and colleagues investigate this possibility with an array of 64 oscillators built with monolayer molybdenum disulfide transistors. The oscillators respond to lighting and their frequencies change by different amounts under red, green and blue light, so differences between devices can combine with optical conditions to obtain different responses.

Let's imagine that we already know the frequencies that a matrix returns under red light. The interesting thing would be to know to what extent this helps us anticipate the key that will be given under different lighting. Between the measured frequency and the key there is a processing stage, so security tests concern the whole. If one day we want to recognize the same chip after years of use, we will also have to find out how much that fingerprint ages.

Related reading. Crypto primitive of MOCVD MoS2 transistors for highly secured physical unclonable functions. Nano Research (2020).

Materials

A magnetic field strengthens polarisation in hafnium oxide

[3] H. Zhang et al., “Observation of room-temperature multiferroicity with strong magnetoelectric coupling in hafnia thin films,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5776, Jul. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72456-y.

Participating institutions
University of Science and Technology of China (China)

Suppose that, among several films of an oxide, the most magnetic are also those with the greatest electrical polarisation. It's tempting to think that one property controls the other, even though they might share a cause. To clear up any doubts, it would be a good idea to stick with a single film, hold a magnet to it and measure the polarisation again. That is, broadly speaking, the logic of the experiment.

The team works with chromium-doped hafnium oxide, which in these films exhibits ferromagnetism and ferroelectricity at room temperature. When applying a magnetic field, it finds changes in both the electrical and piezoelectric response. There appears the link that the comparison between samples only suggested.

Hafnium is familiar to those who make circuits because its oxide is already used in them. What this experiment adds is a physical basis for an electrical readout of a magnetic change in that same type of material.

Related reading. Colossal Room-Temperature Magnetoelectric Coupling in Anion-Deficient Layered Perovskite Films with Ordered Cation Distribution. Journal of the American Chemical Society (2025).
