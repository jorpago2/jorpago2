Week of July 1, 2026

Infrared pixels, silicon antennas and ultrathin silver

From July 1 to 7, 2026

A metasurface that changes with electrical pulses, an antenna integrated into silicon, and silver films that are just a few atoms thick show this week how the design of materials and devices makes it possible to control very different signals.

An infrared image written pixel by pixel

Metasurfaces can modify light using much smaller structures than conventional lenses. The difficulty increases when you want each area of ​​the surface to change independently, since the optical material, the heaters and the electrical connections have to coexist in a reduced space without the writing of one pixel accidentally altering the others.

Cosmin-Constantin Popescu and his collaborators, with the participation of Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the United States and KAIST in South Korea, have developed a transmissive array for the mid-infrared based on GSST, a phase change material. When passing between the amorphous and crystalline states, the GSST changes its refractive index and shifts a resonance of the structure, allowing the intensity of light transmitted by each pixel to be modified.

The prototype contains six rows and six columns of electrically addressable pixels, with a selector diode in each position to reduce unwanted currents through the array. The authors measure a transmission contrast of 5.1 dB at a wavelength of 2.585 µm and use the array to program simple patterns. In an endurance test, switching was stable for about 16,700 cycles before delamination failure occurred.

These figures put the progress in context. The device mainly modifies the transmitted amplitude, does not offer arbitrary control of all light properties, and its fill factor is still low. On the other hand, optical characterization provided relative estimates from images, a limitation that the authors acknowledge. The combination of a two-dimensional array with integrated selectors still provides a concrete route to larger reconfigurable optical surfaces.

C.-C. Popescu et al., “Two-dimensional pixel-level addressable mid-infrared metasurface spatial light modulator,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 8393, 2026. Full article. Epub July 7, 2026.

Antennas and switches on the same wafer

A surface that directs radio waves can change the coverage of a link without physically moving an antenna. To do this you need many elements whose response can be adjusted, but connecting and assembling discrete components one by one becomes more laborious as the surface size grows.

Jinhyun Kim and his collaborators, from Seoul National University in South Korea, propose integrating the active elements into a high-resistivity silicon wafer. The architecture, called STARE, combines a diode designed to control the electrical response with an antenna structure that couples the signal between the two sides of the wafer, without requiring paths that pass through the silicon.

The team fabricates and characterizes a prototype that shifts the transmitted phase between two states 180 degrees apart. In tests at 15.5 GHz, this capability allows the beam to be steered between approximately −45 and +45 degrees. The work also combines electromagnetic simulations with parameters obtained from manufactured devices, so that the antenna design takes into account the real behavior of the switch.

Although the authors relate this type of surfaces to future mobile networks, the result is an experimental demonstration at a specific frequency and in a specific setup, not an operational 6G network. Additionally, the prototype employs one-bit phase control, so increasing resolution will require more switching states or paths. Its contribution is to bring radiofrequency design closer to semiconductor manufacturing processes.

J. Kim et al., “STARE, a semiconductor-integrated transmit-array architecture for 6G beamforming,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 8199, 2026. Full article. Epub July 1, 2026.

When thinning silver changes its non-linear response

By reducing the thickness of a metal film to a few atomic layers, electrons no longer behave exactly as they do in a macroscopic piece. Their motion is confined in one direction and discrete energy levels appear, a modification that can change the way the material responds to light.

Philipp Jenke and his collaborators, including researchers from the University of Vienna in Austria and the ICFO in Spain, have studied ultrathin crystalline films of silver grown on silicon. By illuminating them, they measure second harmonic generation, a nonlinear response that produces light at twice the excitation frequency, and compare how it changes with the number of layers.

The authors observe an improvement of almost two orders of magnitude in the thickness-normalized conversion efficiency when reducing the films to the regime of few atomic layers. Their calculations relate this evolution to the confined electronic states, which allows us to discuss the optical response based on the electronic structure of the film itself and not only the field concentrated by a plasmonic geometry.

Normalization matters when interpreting the result, since it is not equivalent to stating that a thinner film automatically delivers a hundred times greater absolute power. In fact, the authors indicate that the efficiencies obtained are still lower than those of other two-dimensional materials. The work offers, however, a way to think about silver as an optical material whose atomic thickness can also be part of the design of a nonlinear function.

P. K. Jenke et al., “Few-atom-thick silver films for enhanced nanoscale nonlinear optics,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 8214, 2026. Full article. Epub July 1, 2026.
