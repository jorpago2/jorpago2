Week of July 1, 2026

A dispersive lens, a light-sensitive fingerprint and a magnetoelectric oxide

A metalens makes use of the fact that different colours behave differently. The response also depends on the stimulus in the other papers, from light changing an electronic fingerprint to a magnetic field strengthening polarisation in a hafnium oxide film.

Photonics

A metalens that makes use of dispersion

[1] Y. Wu et al., “Ultra-dispersive metasurfaces enabled by convergence-phase design using simplified nanopillar arrays,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5766, Jul. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72332-9. Open access.

In a lens, having each color focus at a different distance is usually a problem in forming sharp images. In a chromatic confocal sensor, however, this difference becomes information, because it allows the detected wavelength to be related to the position of a surface. Achieving sufficient separation within a small component is, however, a challenging task for optical design.

Yunquan Wu and colleagues propose a metallens formed by simple nanopillars, whose design uses the phase of the different wavelengths to produce very high dispersion. The work involves researchers from the Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China and the Singapore University of Technology and Design, among other institutions.

The researchers fabricated several metalenses and use one of them in a miniaturized confocal sensor, with which they report a measurement range of 13 millimeters and an axial resolution of 50 nanometers. The device makes deliberate use of a property that other systems try to correct, although the resolution of a specific test does not in itself describe all the errors that would appear in an industrial environment. By combining a strong chromatic effect with a relatively simple geometry to manufacture, the approach provides a way to explore compact sensors with less dependence on bulky optical components.

Electronics

An electronic fingerprint that changes with light

[2] T. Li et al., “Optically reconfigurable physical unclonable functions based on 2D MoS2 ring-oscillator arrays for attack-resistant hardware authentication,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5803, Jul. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72557-8. Open access.

Participating institutions
Hunan University (China)

Two circuits made with the same design are never completely identical. Their small differences can be used to generate a fingerprint associated with each device, an idea that is being studied to identify hardware without simply storing a fixed key in memory. The challenge is to obtain stable responses that are, at the same time, difficult to predict from what has already been measured.

Tong Li and colleagues investigate this possibility with an array of 64 oscillators built with monolayer molybdenum disulfide transistors. The oscillators respond to lighting and their frequencies change by different amounts under red, green and blue light, so differences between devices can combine with optical conditions to obtain different responses.

The measurements are post-processed to generate and evaluate keys, so the security properties reported apply to the system and tests as a whole, not just to the material. Before practical use, the system would need to be assessed for stability over extended operation and against other attack scenarios. The proposal adds an interesting possibility to two-dimensional photosensitive devices, by using their variability as part of an identification function.

Materials

A magnetic field strengthens polarisation in hafnium oxide

[3] Z. Haiyi et al., “Observation of room-temperature multiferroicity with strong magnetoelectric coupling in hafnia thin films,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 5776, Jul. 2, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-72456-y. Open access.

Participating institutions
University of Science and Technology of China (China)

Adding chromium to a HfO₂ film produces magnetic order and electric polarisation at room temperature. Comparing films, the authors find that samples with stronger ferromagnetism also have larger polarisation.

They test the relationship by applying a magnetic field and measuring the changes in electrical and piezoelectric response. Finding both kinds of order in a film of this oxide is of interest because HfO₂ is already used in circuit fabrication.
