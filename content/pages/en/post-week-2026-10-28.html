Week of October 28, 2026

Separate what light only half tells

Photonics — Separate what light only half tells

[1] P.-A. Mor et al., “Automatic on-chip separation of partially coherent light into its mutually incoherent components,” Nat. Photon., Oct. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-02035-6.

When two beams overlap, their intensity is not always enough to know their relationship to each other. Coherence, which describes how the phases of the field at different points are related, adds information that is often recovered by calculations from many measurements. But can this information be physically separated and made available for further processing with light?

A team involving Stanford University, in the United States, and the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, in Austria, has addressed that question with a silicon photonic circuit. Its network of interferometers is adjusted by means of electronic feedback until the coherence modes of the input field are directed to different outputs. In one test, the researchers mixed two independent beams and sampled the result with nine chip inputs, after which they separated the two dominant modes. They also tested the operation with three modes.

It is convenient to distinguish these modes from the original sources, because, when the starting fields are not orthogonal, each separate mode can contain contributions from both. Even with this nuance, the work is suggestive for image formation and optical processing, since it transforms a decomposition that we usually calculate into a physical operation. Practical issues remain to be resolved, including the control electronics, the consumption of the thermal adjusters and the integration of the input and detection system.

Electronics — A ferroelectric memory with more than two states

[2] K. Wang et al., “High-performance annealing-free wurtzite ferroelectric field-effect transistors with ultrathin oxide semiconductors for neuromorphic computing,” Nat Commun, Nov. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-79253-7.

Storing information on a transistor does not require limiting it to on and off. If the device allows its conductance to be adjusted in a controlled manner, each intermediate state can represent a value and, with this, open the door to both multilevel storage and components for neural networks. The difficulty is in keeping those levels distinguishable while the transistor is programmed over and over again.

Researchers from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China, and Hong Kong Polytechnic University, China, have compared three ferroelectric transistor structures that combine scandium aluminum nitride with an ultrathin indium gallium oxide channel. The architecture that sandwiches a metallic layer and an insulator between the ferroelectric and the semiconductor achieved a memory window of up to 20.6 V and withstood one million cycles. The authors also distinguish 64 states in a multilevel storage test.

The article also explores digit recognition, although that result comes from a neural network simulation that uses measured characteristics of the device, not from a complete circuit that has recognized the images on its own. That simulation uses 128 states and reports an accuracy of 93.02%. It is a useful indication of what could be done with this class of devices, while the operating voltage and the behavior of large assemblies remain decisive aspects in assessing their application.

Materials — Track electrons as light oscillates

[3] V. Eggers et al., “Subcycle videography of Landau–Zener–Majorana transitions in graphene,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8139, pp. 63–68, Oct. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11214-y.

In graphene, electrons respond to an electric field in a very particular band landscape, where the separation between states can become extraordinarily small. If the field is sufficiently intense, the evolution is no longer easy to describe as a movement within a single band, since transitions between bands appear that occur during a fraction of the cycle of the light wave itself.

A team from the University of Regensburg, the University of Marburg and the Jülich Research Center, in Germany, has observed these dynamics in graphene using infrared pulses and photoelectron momentum microscopy. Instead of following an individual electron as if it were a particle filmed by a camera, the technique reconstructs how the electronic distribution changes in momentum space by varying the delay between the pulse that excites the sample and the one that probes it.

The results show that the distribution shifts and deforms during the most intense half-cycles, a sign of the combination between the acceleration within the bands and the Landau–Zener–Majorana transitions between them. Collisions then cause the observed coherence to be lost. Rather than presenting a ready-made device, the experiment offers a way to examine ultrafast processes that will be valuable in understanding how to manipulate materials with light fields.
