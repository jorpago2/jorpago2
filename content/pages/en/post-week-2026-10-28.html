Week of October 28, 2026

Separating light modes, stabilizing transistors and tracking electrons in graphene

Mixing two beams is easy. Sorting out their coherence afterwards is a different matter, especially if you'd like to carry on working with light at the output. We'll also look at what an additive's counterion does for a tin perovskite, and how to follow electrons in graphene within a single cycle of light.

Photonics

Separate what light only half tells

[1] P.-A. Mor et al., “Automatic on-chip separation of partially coherent light into its mutually incoherent components,” Nature Photonics, Oct. 30, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41566-026-02035-6.

A photograph can tell us that two beams have the same brightness without clarifying which fringes will appear if we match them. For this, the stability of their relative phases counts. The usual thing is to reconstruct coherence from measurements and stay with the calculation. The proposal of this work goes one step further, separating the modes on a chip so that each one follows its path like a beam of light.

A team involving Stanford University, in the United States, and the Institute of Science and Technology Austria, in Austria, has addressed that question with a silicon photonic circuit. Its network of interferometers is adjusted by means of electronic feedback until the coherence modes of the input field are directed to different outputs. In one test, the researchers mixed two independent beams and sampled the result with nine chip inputs, after which they separated the two dominant modes. They also tested the operation with three modes.

Will the two original beams then come out through different ports? Only under certain conditions. If the starting fields were not orthogonal, an output mode can carry some of both. That subtlety explains what physical operation the circuit has actually done. To bring it to an imaging system, it will also be necessary to integrate input and detection, and count how much it costs to keep the interferometers adjusted.

Related reading. Ghost imaging with incoherent and partially coherent light radiation. Physical Review E (2005).

Electronics

An additive that reshapes the channel of a perovskite transistor

[2] B. H. Jeong et al., “A-site cation–pseudohalide co-design for tin halide perovskite transistors,” Science Advances, vol. 12, no. 44, Art. no. eaeh2062, Oct. 30, 2026, doi 10.1126/sciadv.aeh2062.

If two additives share half of their formula, comparing them allows a fairly precise question to be asked about the other half. At Hanyang University, in South Korea, they keep the thiocyanate anion and change the cation to study tin perovskite transistors. Replacing lead with tin is attractive, although tin oxidizes and mobile ions complicate reading the current.

With guanidinium thiocyanate, larger grains and less oxidized tin appear. In p-channel transistors, the field effect mobility exceeds 88 cm²/(V·s), and the ratio between the on and off currents exceeds 4 × 10⁹. Stability monitoring is done with encapsulated transistors.

The interesting thing about keeping the same anion is that the differences in growth between the films force us to also look at its companion. Its role begins before we can connect some electrodes and measure the transistor.

Related reading. High‐Performance P‐Channel Tin Halide Perovskite Thin Film Transistor Utilizing a 2D–3D Core–Shell Structure. Advanced Science (2021).

Materials

Track electrons as light oscillates

[3] V. Eggers et al., “Subcycle videography of Landau–Zener–Majorana transitions in graphene,” Nature, vol. 659, no. 8139, pp. 63–68, Oct. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11214-y.

What can be seen in graphene during half a cycle of a light wave? Near the points where their bands touch, a strong field can accelerate the electrons and also move them to another band. If we waited until the end of the pulse to watch, it would be difficult to reconstruct how much each movement contributed.

A team from the University of Regensburg, the University of Marburg and the Jülich Research Center, in Germany, has observed these dynamics in graphene using infrared pulses and photoelectron momentum microscopy. Instead of following an individual electron as if it were a particle filmed by a camera, the technique reconstructs how the electronic distribution changes in momentum space by varying the delay between the pulse that excites the sample and the one that probes it.

During the half cycles of greater field the distribution shifts and is also deformed. These two traces allow us to interpret both the acceleration within the bands and the Landau–Zener–Majorana transitions between them. Then collisions erase the coherence. Hence the interest in precisely varying the delay between pulses, because the information sought only remains visible for a very brief part of the experiment.

Related reading. Nonadiabatic Landau–Zener–Stückelberg–Majorana transitions, dynamics, and interference. Physics Reports (2023).
