Week of October 14, 2026

Infrared in a suspended guide, a reconfigurable transistor and the first steps of crystal growth

Sometimes a waveguide's biggest problem is what's underneath it. Elsewhere this week, the same transistor can track the light or remember an exposure depending on its electrical settings, and the timing of a small scratch changes how a semiconductor film grows as it dries.

Photonics

A suspended modulator for the mid-infrared

[1] C.-H. Lee et al., “Suspended thin-film lithium niobate modulator for broadband mid-infrared light modulation and frequency comb generation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10936, Oct. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77674-y.

Participating institutions
University of California, Berkeley (United States)

Lithium niobate can still be transparent in a mid-infrared area where its support begins to absorb. If the light hits that support, it doesn't matter how good the waveguide is. Hence the decision to suspend it and to simultaneously design the electrodes, so that the microwave wave accompanies the light along the active section.

The prototype modulates between 2.4 and 3.6 µm and generates a frequency comb with a 10 dB span above 0.8 THz. The study also tests a free-space optical link, illustrating two uses of the same platform in frequency synthesis and signal transmission.

Reading the results reveals two bandwidths that may seem difficult to reconcile. The 50 GHz describes how far the modulator can follow the electrical excitation. The optical comb, on the other hand, brings together many lines separated by the modulation frequency. It is enough to accumulate several of these intervals so that the set occupies a strip much larger than the electrical band with which it was generated.

Electronics

A transistor that changes tasks

[2] J. Si et al., “Multifunctional Vertical Bulk‐Heterojunction Transistors With Amorphous GaO x for Neuromorphic Perception‐to‐Decision,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78415, Oct. 20, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78415.

A detector that continued to remember yesterday's light would be a somewhat uncomfortable instrument for measuring today's light. In an artificial synapse, however, that same persistence may be the property we are looking for. The beauty of this transistor is that we can ask it for both functions by changing the electrical conditions of the test.

A team involving Jimei University and Fuzhou University in China presents a vertical bulk-heterojunction transistor with amorphous gallium oxide that can be configured to act as a deep ultraviolet detector, artificial synapse, threshold neuron or logic element. Instead of manufacturing a different part for each operation, researchers modify the electrical conditions to obtain different responses, which opens an interesting avenue to study circuits that combine perception and processing.

The work includes a 97.4% success rate in recognizing footprints. To locate this result, it is enough to follow the route from the measurement bank. From there comes the response of the devices and, from it, a hybrid network simulation is built. The recognition is evaluated in that network, while the physical demonstration allows us to verify how a transistor responds under each configuration.

Related reading. Toward Switching and Fusing Neuromorphic Computing: Vertical Bulk Heterojunction Transistors with Multi‐Neuromorphic Functions for Efficient Deep Learning. Advanced Materials (2025).

Materials

The moment to start crystallizing

[3] Z. Ling et al., “Universal crystallization kinetics of solution-processed semiconductors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10785, Oct. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78354-7.

What can a small scratch on a drying film change? More than it seems, if it arrives just when the first crystal nuclei begin to form. The nucleus that starts early finds a different concentration and other neighbours to compete with than the one that appears a few moments later.

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research, in Germany, and the Utrecht University, in the Netherlands, study this transition by causing nucleation with a small scratch at different times during processing. When the moment of the perturbation changes, the concentration of the solution and the competition between the induced nuclei and those that appear spontaneously also change. Thus they can obtain large domains, regions of oriented growth or films with small domains.

Once growth has started, the nuclei that were born from scratching and those that appeared spontaneously follow the same kinetics. The mechanical gesture intervenes, therefore, in the start of the race. Advancing or retarding it changes the distribution of domains that ends up in the transistor, and in the oriented films the team measures better charge transport. It is enough to choose the moment to modify what a charge encounters when crossing the dry film.
