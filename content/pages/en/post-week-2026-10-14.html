Week of October 14, 2026

Twisted waveguides, a versatile transistor and the pace of crystallization

Turning the polarization to steer light

[1] J. Gao et al., “Polarization-controlled Floquet engineering in twisted photonic lattices,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11655, Oct. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78660-0. Open access.

In a row of closely spaced waveguides, light can pass from one guide to the next. Researchers at Peking University and Nanjing University in China build lattices whose waveguides twist, changing the relative polarization angle. This lets them tune the coupling between adjacent guides from positive to negative values through zero.

By repeating that modulation along the lattice, they explore how the propagation changes. In one dimension they observe oscillations associated with flat bands, and in two dimensions they reverse the direction of edge-state propagation. Both effects can be tuned using the same coupling mechanism.

A transistor that changes tasks

[2] J. Si et al., “Multifunctional Vertical Bulk‐Heterojunction Transistors With Amorphous GaO x for Neuromorphic Perception‐to‐Decision,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78415, Oct. 20, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78415.

In a conventional system, detecting a signal, keeping a trace of it and making a decision are usually functions distributed between different components. Neuromorphic electronics seeks to explore other possibilities, although bringing together several functions in one device does not imply that a complete system capable of solving a real problem is already available.

A team involving Jimei University and Fuzhou University in China presents a vertical bulk-heterojunction transistor with amorphous gallium oxide that can be configured to act as a deep ultraviolet detector, artificial synapse, threshold neuron or logic element. Instead of manufacturing a different part for each operation, researchers modify the electrical conditions to obtain different responses, which opens an interesting avenue to study circuits that combine perception and processing.

The fingerprint recognition figure that accompanies the work, 97.4%, requires careful reading, since it corresponds to a simulation of a hybrid network based on the behavior of the devices and not to an autonomous recognizer manufactured entirely with them. The result shows what could be done with those responses, while integration, variability between devices and the cost of the entire system remain issues to be resolved.

The moment to start crystallizing

[3] Z. Ling et al., “Universal crystallization kinetics of solution-processed semiconductors,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10785, Oct. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78354-7.

Making a semiconductor from a solution seems simple until you wonder how to control the crystals that appear as the solvent disappears. The boundaries that the charges encounter when passing through the film and, therefore, the behavior of the finished device may depend on its size and orientation.

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research, in Germany, and the Utrecht University, in the Netherlands, study this transition by causing nucleation with a small scratch at different times during processing. When the moment of the perturbation changes, the concentration of the solution and the competition between the induced nuclei and those that appear spontaneously also change. Thus they can obtain large domains, regions of oriented growth or films with small domains.

The work combines this observation with growth modeling and testing on several families of semiconductors, including perovskites and organic materials. In the transistors studied, the oriented films offered better results than the other morphologies compared. It is a suggestive way of connecting a process that occurs during manufacturing to subsequent electronic properties, although the breadth of the model must be understood within the materials and conditions examined, and its incorporation into production processes would require new testing.

Also worth a look

A team has studied near-infrared detectors that combine microcones of silicon with gold, taking advantage of a Schottky junction to detect wavelengths that pose difficulties for silicon. The publisher describes a responsivity of 73 mA/W at 1.31 µm without external voltage applied, although I have not been able to verify access to the full text and therefore prefer to leave it as a brief mention. “Plasmonic Au/Si Microcone Diode Arrays for Superior Photodetection of Near-Infrared Light,” Advanced Optical Materials, October 14, 2026, doi 10.1002/adom.71928.
