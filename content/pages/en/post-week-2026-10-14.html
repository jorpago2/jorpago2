Week of October 14, 2026

Infrared in a suspended guide, a reconfigurable transistor and the first steps of crystal growth

Removing material beneath a waveguide keeps the support from absorbing the infrared light to be modulated. In another device, the operating conditions determine whether a transistor detects light or retains a trace of it. The last paper examines a decision that precedes any electrical measurement, choosing when crystals begin to grow.

Photonics

A suspended modulator for the mid-infrared

[1] C.-H. Lee et al., “Suspended thin-film lithium niobate modulator for broadband mid-infrared light modulation and frequency comb generation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10936, Oct. 20, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77674-y.

Participating institutions
University of California, Berkeley (United States)

A lithium niobate waveguide can be transparent at a mid-infrared wavelength its support already absorbs, which makes suspending the guide an attractive option. The electrodes and waveguide are designed together so the microwave signal and light travel along the active section together.

The prototype modulates between 2.4 and 3.6 µm and generates a frequency comb with a 10 dB span above 0.8 THz. The study also tests a free-space optical link, illustrating two uses of the same platform in frequency synthesis and signal transmission.

Another use of lithium niobate modulators, this time with individual photons (Light: Science & Applications).

Electronics

A transistor that changes tasks

[2] J. Si et al., “Multifunctional Vertical Bulk‐Heterojunction Transistors With Amorphous GaO x for Neuromorphic Perception‐to‐Decision,” Advanced Science, Art. no. e78415, Oct. 20, 2026, doi 10.1002/advs.78415.

A detector reporting the light present now and a memory retaining the previous exposure demand very different time responses from a material. Neuromorphic electronics seeks to explore other possibilities, although bringing together several functions in one device does not imply that a complete system capable of solving a real problem is already available.

A team involving Jimei University and Fuzhou University in China presents a vertical bulk-heterojunction transistor with amorphous gallium oxide that can be configured to act as a deep ultraviolet detector, artificial synapse, threshold neuron or logic element. Instead of manufacturing a different part for each operation, researchers modify the electrical conditions to obtain different responses, which opens an interesting avenue to study circuits that combine perception and processing.

The fingerprint recognition figure that accompanies the work, 97.4%, requires careful reading, since it corresponds to a simulation of a hybrid network based on the behavior of the devices and not to an autonomous recognizer manufactured entirely with them.

Materials

The moment to start crystallizing

[3] Z. Ling et al., “Universal crystallization kinetics of solution-processed semiconductors,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 10785, Oct. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-78354-7.

As a solution-deposited semiconductor film dries, the first nuclei to appear compete for the material still available around them. The boundaries that the charges encounter when passing through the film and, therefore, the behavior of the finished device may depend on its size and orientation.

Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research, in Germany, and the Utrecht University, in the Netherlands, study this transition by causing nucleation with a small scratch at different times during processing. When the moment of the perturbation changes, the concentration of the solution and the competition between the induced nuclei and those that appear spontaneously also change. Thus they can obtain large domains, regions of oriented growth or films with small domains.

Once a nucleus has formed, measurements during growth give the same kinetics for induced and spontaneous nuclei. The mechanical intervention therefore acts on the starting time. Moving that time changes the film morphology, and in the transistors tested the oriented films carry charge more effectively.
