Week of September 16, 2026

Computing with light, retaining polarisation and turning a mask into an emitter

An optical processor repeating a calculation has a good reason to keep its settings between inputs. Making use of what's already there takes a different form in the other papers, where a ferroelectric film retains its polarisation loops after transfer and a lithographic resist stays behind to emit light from the pixel it helped define.

Photonics

Keeping a photonic processor’s settings in its modulators

[1] W. Zhou et al., “Ultrafast reconfigurable topological-modulators-based photonic processing accelerator,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9920, Sep. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-75993-8.

Participating institutions
Shanghai Jiao Tong University (China)

There are circuits whose consumption deserves to be measured even when they seem to be waiting. If an optical processor requires continuous power to maintain the weights of a network, it pays to retain the configuration from one image to the next. Here the lead titanate zirconate modulators keep the adjustment through their ferroelectric polarisation. The design concentrates the light in that region so that the stored change intervenes in the calculation.

A 16-channel prototype is tested on tasks including digit classification. Keeping the configuration inside the modulators reduces the power needed to hold it. This applies to static operation, while loading data and reading results still have a cost.

To appreciate how much that savings can matter, let's think about two series of images. If in the entire first we apply the same filter, we write the weights once and use them many times. If in the second the filter changes in each image, they must be programmed again. The same chip will then have two different energy balances, although it will never need to expend energy just to remember the last setting.

Related reading. Parallel convolutional processing using an integrated photonic tensor core. Nature (2021).

Electronics

A memory that preserves polarization loops

[2] F.-H. Gong et al., “Creation of ferroelectric flux-closure transistor array for non-volatile field-effect memory,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 11009, Sep. 21, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77913-2.

Participating institutions
Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Hong Kong, China), Zhejiang University (China), Hunan University of Science and Technology (China), Lanzhou University of Technology (China)

A ferroelectric film can collect its polarisation lines into loops that reduce the field outside the material. To convert these loops into memory you have to do something quite delicate, remove the film from the substrate where it grew and preserve its behaviour when it is part of a transistor. That's the step being studied by Gong and colleagues at institutions in the Chinese Academy of Sciences and other Chinese universities.

To do this, they prepare ferroelectric films independent of the growth substrate and integrate them into transistor matrices. According to the authors, the closure structures preserve a response with a low coercive field and allow reversible write and erase operations.

There are two big figures in the article and they answer different questions. Ferroelectric structures reach ten billion cycles in fatigue tests. Once the transistor is assembled, however, we would want to know if what we have just written is clearly distinguished. There the data is the quotient between the currents of the two states of the channel, which reaches one hundred million in the devices tested.

Related reading. Laterally gated ferroelectric field effect transistor (LG-FeFET) using α-In2Se3 for stacked in-memory computing array. Nature Communications (2023).

Materials

A material that serves as a mask and also emits light

[3] S.-W. Lo et al., “Electroluminescent photoresists extending lithographic scaling to OLEDs,” Nature, vol. 658, no. 8134, pp. 78–85, Sep. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-026-11042-0.

In photolithography there usually comes a time when we remove the resin, finish its work and continue manufacturing the device. Lo and colleagues, with the participation of ETH Zürich, in Switzerland, wonder what would happen if that same resin could emit light. Once the pixel is drawn, we could leave it precisely where it is to incorporate it into the diode.

The polymers have a core-shell architecture that protects the emitter while the outer regions participate in the cross-linking reactions necessary to form the pattern. The team demonstrated sequential multilayer processing, fluorescent structures with dimensions down to 110 nanometers, and multicolor OLEDs with external quantum efficiencies greater than 13% in the characterized devices.

The two figures in the article invite you to do a check before imagining a screen. The 110 nm is measured in the finest detail of a fluorescent pattern, which is illuminated from the outside. Efficiency greater than 13% corresponds to electrically powered OLEDs. To put them together in a display specification we would still want to measure how efficient the diode retains when we reduce its own pixel to that scale.

Related reading. Fine metal mask material and manufacturing process for high‐resolution active‐matrix organic light‐emitting diode displays. Journal of the Society for Information Display (2020).
