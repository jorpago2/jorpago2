Week of September 16, 2026

A laser for cold atoms, ferroelectric memories and light-drawn OLEDs

Week of September 16 to 22, 2026.

Photonics

Adjusting a laser with a built-in resonator

A. Isichenko et al., “Sub-Doppler rubidium atom cooling using a programmable agile integrated PZT-on-SiN resonator”, Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 10275, 2026. Epub September 16. Open Access Article.

The study includes researchers from the University of California, Santa Barbara, in the United States.

To cool atoms with a laser, its frequency must be carefully controlled and modified during the experimental sequence. It is a task that usually uses external components, so integrating part of that control can help build more compact instruments. Isichenko and his collaborators explore this possibility with a silicon nitride resonator on which they incorporate a piezoelectric actuator made of lead zirconate titanate, or PZT.

The 780-nanometer laser is stabilized against the resonator, whose frequency can be electrically shifted by deforming the device. The team alternates referencing a rubidium transition with rapid tuning sequences and uses that source to cool a cloud of rubidium-87 atoms. In one of the configurations, it measured a minimum temperature of about 16 microkelvin in one of the cloud axes.

The result shows that an electrically controlled resonator can take on part of the functions necessary for a sequence of cold atoms. However, the assembly still includes a semiconductor laser, electronics and the rest of the atomic experiment, so the advance should be understood as a step towards more integrated sources and not as a complete clock or sensor on a single chip.

Electronics

A memory that preserves polarization loops

F.-H. Gong et al., “Creation of ferroelectric flux-closure transistor array for non-volatile field-effect memory”, Nature Communications, 2026. E-publishing September 21. Open Access Article.

The work brings together researchers from institutions of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and other universities in China.

The polarization of a ferroelectric material does not always point uniformly, as it can be organized into domains that form closed configurations to reduce its energy. Gong and his collaborators study how to harness these structures in a field-effect memory, a problem that requires preserving them when the film is incorporated into a device.

To do this, they prepare ferroelectric films independent of the growth substrate and integrate them into transistor matrices. According to the authors, the closure structures preserve a response with a low coercive field and allow reversible write and erase operations. The study reports endurance up to 10 billion cycles for the structures investigated, in addition to characterizing the memory window and the relationship between states of the transistors.

The possibility of transferring a domain configuration from a film to a transistor is interesting because it connects a local property of the material with the memory function. The editorial page currently offers an early version of the article, so it will be advisable to review the reference and experimental details again when the final version appears, without extrapolating the number of cycles to any matrix made with the same material.

Materials

A material that serves as a mask and also emits light

S.-W. Lo et al., “Electroluminescent photoresists extending lithographic scaling to OLEDs”, Nature, vol. 658, pp. 78–85, 2026. Epub September 16. Open Access Article.

Among the participating institutions is ETH Zürich, in Switzerland.

In lithographic manufacturing, a light-sensitive layer is used to draw structures, but that layer usually serves a temporary function and is then removed. Lo and his collaborators propose a material that retains a second function after the pattern, since it can also emit light when incorporated into an organic diode.

The polymers have a core-shell architecture that protects the emitter while the outer regions participate in the cross-linking reactions necessary to form the pattern. The team demonstrated sequential multilayer processing, fluorescent structures with dimensions down to 110 nanometers, and multicolor OLEDs with external quantum efficiencies greater than 13% in the characterized devices.

The idea is especially attractive for bringing microfabrication methods closer to small organic emitters, although there is still a distance between experimental patterns and a cost-effectively manufactured complete display. Likewise, the pattern resolution and efficiency of an OLED are related results, but each describes a different measurement and should not be confused with the resolution or final performance of a product.
