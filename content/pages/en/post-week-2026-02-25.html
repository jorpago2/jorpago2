Week of February 25, 2026

Light between two metals, power on a thin film and a blue diode

The distance between two metals can change how strongly an optical signal is enhanced. On other scales, a thin film can deliver power across a large area, while a molecule at a nanocrystal surface helps keep an LED blue.

Photonics

One volt to change light in an atomic-scale gap

[1] S. Takahashi, A. Sakurai, T. Mochizuki, and T. Sugimoto, “Giant near-field nonlinear electrophotonic effects in an angstrom-scale plasmonic junction,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2012, Feb. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68823-4. Open access.

Participating institutions
Institute for Molecular Science (Japan)

The gap between a scanning tunnelling microscope’s metal tip and a metal surface concentrates the optical field. The researchers apply a voltage across it and measure the change in light emitted at twice the input frequency.

In this geometry, a voltage change of at most one volt boosts the second-harmonic signal by around 2,000%. The figure comes from a microscopic junction where both light confinement and the short electrode spacing help produce the effect.

Electronics

Powering a sensor array with thin-film transistors

[2] M. Velazquez Lopez, N. Papadopoulos, P. Coulson, B. Vandecasteele, and K. Myny, “High output power low temperature polysilicon thin-film transistor boost converters for large-area sensor and actuator applications,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 32, Feb. 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00536-6. Open access.

Participating institutions
KU Leuven (Belgium)

A sensor array built over a large area needs power delivered to the circuits spread across it. The team designs boost converters using low-temperature polysilicon transistors, a technology compatible with such surfaces.

The prototypes deliver between 0.62 and 2.17 W at efficiencies ranging from 47 to 69.5%. Both figures matter. Higher output power helps drive larger arrays, while losses still matter if the system is to run for long periods.

Materials

A surface molecule that helps a perovskite stay blue

[3] H. Maimaitizi, H. Ågren, and G. Chen, “Multifunctional ligand engineering enables high-performance CsPb(Br/Cl)3 nanocrystals toward efficient and stable pure-blue perovskite LEDs,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 135, Feb. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02214-8. Open access.

Participating institutions
Harbin Institute of Technology (China)

Nanocrystals containing bromine and chlorine can emit blue light, but their colour becomes unstable if the halides redistribute under bias. The researchers attach a molecule with several groups that can interact with the crystal, near both undercoordinated lead and the halides.

The treated diodes emit at 467 nm and reach an external quantum efficiency of 14.8%. They also last longer than the reference device. The comparison shows why surface chemistry matters for both maintaining the colour and getting more photons out of the diode.
