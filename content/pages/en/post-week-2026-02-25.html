Week of February 25, 2026

Light between two metals, power on a thin film and a blue diode

Across the tiny gap beneath a microscope tip, a single volt can make a striking difference to the light that emerges. At the other end of the scale, a sheet of sensors needs whole watts of power. We'll finish at the surface of a blue pixel, where a change in chemistry helps it last longer.

Photonics

One volt to change light in an atomic-scale gap

[1] S. Takahashi, A. Sakurai, T. Mochizuki, and T. Sugimoto, “Giant near-field nonlinear electrophotonic effects in an angstrom-scale plasmonic junction,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 2012, Feb. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68823-4.

Participating institutions
Institute for Molecular Science (Japan)

One volt seems like a small thing until you ask how far apart the two electrodes are. Here one is the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope and the other a metal surface at an atomic scale distance. The light is also concentrated in that gap. This allows applying a voltage right in the place where the metal generates light at twice the frequency it receives.

By varying the voltage by one volt or less, the team finds an increase of about 2000% in the second harmonic signal. Before taking that percentage as an efficiency figure, I would do a very simple check. What has the output signal been divided with? Here it is compared with the signal that existed before changing the voltage.

To know what fraction of the incident light is converted to the second harmonic, the power entering would also have to be measured. A spectacular relative change can come from a small signal, and the very particular geometry of this junction matters in that comparison.

Related reading. Quantum Plasmonics: Nonlinear Effects in the Field Enhancement of a Plasmonic Nanoparticle Dimer. Nano Letters (2012).

Electronics

Powering a sensor array with thin-film transistors

[2] M. Velazquez Lopez, N. Papadopoulos, P. Coulson, B. Vandecasteele, and K. Myny, “High output power low temperature polysilicon thin-film transistor boost converters for large-area sensor and actuator applications,” npj Flexible Electronics, vol. 10, no. 1, Art. no. 32, Feb. 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41528-026-00536-6.

Participating institutions
KU Leuven (Belgium)

It is easy to draw a sheet covered with sensors and forget about a line in the diagram, the one that supplies their power. If the available voltage does not reach what the matrix needs, the converter has to raise it and deliver enough current without occupying the entire surface. The team is trying to build it with low-temperature polysilicon transistors, a technology that already fits into this type of sheet.

The prototypes deliver between 0.62 and 2.17 W, with efficiencies of 47 to 69.5%. This is where I would do two accounts. The power output indicates how many sensors will be able to be powered, while the efficiency tells how much the source will actually have to supply.

The difference ends up largely as heat near the devices that must measure. That's why raising the voltage on the sheet itself is only half the problem if the matrix is going to work for hours.

Related reading. Thin-film transistors for large-area electronics. Nature Electronics (2023).

Materials

A surface molecule that helps a perovskite stay blue

[3] H. Maimaitizi, H. Ågren, and G. Chen, “Multifunctional ligand engineering enables high-performance CsPb(Br/Cl)3 nanocrystals toward efficient and stable pure-blue perovskite LEDs,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 135, Feb. 28, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02214-8.

Participating institutions
Harbin Institute of Technology (China)

To achieve a specific blue, the distribution of bromine and chlorine in a perovskite nanocrystal can be adjusted. The difficult part comes when turning on the diode, because the halides can move and take the colour with them. The team places a molecule on the surface capable of interacting both with the lead that is poorly coordinated and with the halides that surround it.

The treated LEDs emit at 467 nm and achieve 14.8% external quantum efficiency. They also last longer than the reference ones, and here it is worth translating the improvement into clock time. Under the test conditions, it takes 342 s for the brightness to drop by half, about thirteen times longer.

To compare surface treatments, the difference is considerable. If, on the other hand, you were choosing the pixel on a screen that must remain on for hours, those same 342 seconds would lead you to a much more cautious conclusion.

Related reading. Bifunctional Molecule Enables High-Quality CsPb(Br/Cl)3 Nanocrystals for Efficient and Stable Pure-Blue Perovskite Light-Emitting Diodes. ACS Energy Letters (2022).
