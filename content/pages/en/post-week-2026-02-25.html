Week of February 25, 2026

Apparent gain, slow memories and oxides that see in infrared

A cavity without gain can show a signal that evolves much like a laser during startup. The resemblance becomes clear when we follow the signal, a useful precaution when studying a memory sensitive to the timing of pulses or a detector whose reading depends on where the sample sits.

Photonics

A passive cavity that imitates the start of a laser

[1] B. Xue et al., “Lasing-like dynamics with virtual gain driven by complex-frequency excitations,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3359, Mar. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70123-w.

The work brings together researchers from the Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in China, and the City University of New York, in the United States.

When we think of a laser we usually imagine an active material that compensates for losses and allows the light to be amplified. Xue and his collaborators study a more subtle situation, in which a passive microcavity shows a temporal evolution similar to that of the start of a laser when illuminated with a carefully prepared signal. The key is in the temporal shape of the excitation, which provides a way to explore what the authors call virtual gain.

In their experiments they used a whispering-gallery-mode microcavity and pulses whose amplitude varies exponentially in time. By changing that excitation they observed different transmission regimes and a threshold-like transition. The instantaneous response may exceed the input intensity at certain times, but this must be understood from the previously stored energy and the temporal shape of the pulse, without attributing to the cavity an amplifying medium that it does not contain.

This distinction is precisely what is interesting about the work, because it allows the dynamics of light in a cavity to be separated from the physics of the active material of a conventional laser. For now this is a controlled demonstration of optical dynamics, so proposed applications such as sensors or communications will still require specific devices and a complete evaluation of their operation.

Electronics

Memory that changes at its own pace

[2] J. Ming et al., “Intrinsic gradient oxygen-driven second-order memristors for continual reinforcement learning,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 3367, Mar. 3, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70014-0.

Participating institutions include Nanjing University of Posts and Telecommunications, in China, and Northwestern Polytechnical University, also in China.

In a resistive memory, the history of the applied signals can be reflected in the device's conductance. Ming and his collaborators take advantage of this time dependence to build a memristor whose response evolves slowly, with the idea of ​​studying how a physical dynamic of this type could help adjust the learning rate of an algorithm.

The device incorporates a porphyrin molecular layer and an aluminum oxide layer deposited by atomic layer deposition. According to the authors' interpretation, the interaction between the two generates an oxygen gradient that conditions the evolution of an internal barrier and allows correlated conductance states to be obtained for more than one hundred seconds. When applying pulse sequences they observed a broad modulation and more than forty distinguishable states under their measurement conditions.

They then translated those states into the learning rate of a route-finding task using reinforcement learning. Compared to the chosen comparison strategies, the number of iterations decreased in the environments studied, although the step between the device measurement and the algorithm includes data processing and is not equivalent to demonstrating that the entire network has been physically executed in an array of memristors. Even with this limit, the work poses a suggestive connection between the characteristic timescale of a material and the adaptation of a calculation system.

Materials

When the sample position changes what the IGZO sees

[3] J. Choe et al., “Sputtering-driven formation of interstitial oxygen for intrinsic NIR detection in IGZO phototransistor,” Sci Rep, vol. 16, no. 1, Art. no. 11065, Feb. 25, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41598-026-40769-z.

Researchers from the Korea Institute of Ceramic Engineering and Technology, Korea University and Yonsei University, in South Korea, along with the Argonne National Laboratory, in the United States, participate in the study.

Indium gallium zinc oxide, known as IGZO, is used in thin film transistors, although its intrinsic near-infrared response is limited. Choe and his collaborators examine a way to extend that response without adding another absorbent layer, for which they modify the deposition geometry and compare films obtained with the sample located on or off-axis of the sputtering target.

In their phototransistors, the off-axis deposited film responded to 850 nanometer light, while the reference device showed barely any detectable signal at that wavelength. Composition measurements and calculations suggest that interstitial oxygen introduces electronic states that facilitate a response below the usual absorption energy of the material. The study reports a responsivity of 42.5 amperes per watt under the conditions tested.

Sensitivity has a counterpart, since trapped carriers also prolong the response. At 850 nanometers, the authors obtained approximate rise and fall times of 4.1 and 10 seconds, respectively, figures that outline the applications for which this version of the device could be useful. It is a good example of how an apparently geometric manufacturing decision ends up modifying the physics of the material and, with it, the type of sensor that can be built.
