Week of March 25, 2026

Signals in the air, molecular memory and collective light

From March 25 to 31, 2026

A wireless communication assisted by photonics, a molecule whose conductance changes with its environment and a network of quantum dots that collectively emit light star in this week's readings. All three articles are open access and first appeared online within these dates.

From photonic chip to wireless link

[1] Y. Guo et al., “Minimalist terahertz wireless transceiver in integrated photonics,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4429, Mar. 26, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71081-z.

To send large amounts of information through the air, it is tempting to use increasingly higher frequencies, where very wide channels are available. However, generating and receiving signals in the subterahertz region often requires complex equipment, especially if the system has to correct for frequency and phase fluctuations from its sources. Photonics can help produce those signals, although the entire setup must also be simple enough for the solution to make sense outside the lab.

Yijun Guo and his collaborators, from Peking University and ShanghaiTech University, in China, propose an architecture that takes advantage of the relationship between an optical carrier and the signal it carries. Through residual carrier modulation and injection locking, they manage to recover frequency and phase information without resorting to the digital stages that would normally be dedicated to that task. The receiver uses a single photodetector, while the assembly incorporates a thin film lithium niobate modulator and a photodiode capable of generating the radio frequency signal.

In the experimental test they transmit up to 144 gigabits per second with a 200 GHz carrier, using commercial distributed feedback lasers. The result is interesting because it reduces some of the demands placed on optical sources and carrier recovery, although it does not eliminate all the digital processing necessary to demodulate the signal. In addition, the antennas were about 20 centimeters apart and the assembly included amplifiers and other external equipment, so we are not yet looking at a complete transceiver integrated on a single chip.

The question that the work leaves open is how to preserve this simplicity when the distance and number of users increase, or when the components must operate in less controlled conditions. Still, it shows how a decision about signal architecture can be as important as the speed of each individual device.

A molecule with several memory states

[2] H. Zhang et al., “Single-molecule neuromorphic device with aJ-level power consumption per switching,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4655, Mar. 31, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-71127-2.

Connections between neurons change their response with previous activity, a property that has inspired devices capable of storing and processing information through gradual changes in conductance. If this function could be carried out in an object as small as a molecule, an interesting possibility would appear to explore the physical limits of neuromorphic electronics, although it would also be necessary to learn to control a system that is especially sensitive to its environment.

Hua Zhang and his collaborators, from the Xiamen University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences, in China, have studied a junction in which a molecule connects two gold electrodes within an electrolyte solution. By applying a gate voltage, the ions in the solution modify their interaction with the molecule and, thereby, change its conductance. The combination of measurements and calculations suggests that molecular reorganization and the movement of ions participate in the different observed states.

The team finds more than ten levels of conductance and reproduces responses that resemble the plasticity of a synapse, from transient changes to more persistent states. For specific measurement conditions, with a bias of 1 mV and pulses of 1 ms, they estimate an average energy of about 6.34 attojoules per event, including the contributions of the gate and the channel. The authors also explore simple association and pattern recognition tasks, which serve to illustrate the possibilities of this adjustable response.

The scale of the active element is impressive, but the experiment employs a molecular junction formed and characterized with laboratory electrochemical instrumentation. Therefore, the energy attributed to a device event does not represent the consumption of an entire processor, nor does the demonstration yet resolve how to fabricate and connect large stable arrays. The advance is in showing that a single molecule, together with its neighboring ions, can sustain memory dynamics that are worth studying in more detail.

When a network of quantum dots flashes in unison

[3] Q. Tan et al., “Room temperature collective blinking and photon bunching from CsPbBr3 quantum dot superlattice,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 4536, Mar. 27, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-70931-0.

A semiconductor quantum dot can emit light intermittently, alternating bright periods with much weaker ones. By arranging many of these nanocrystals into a common structure, the question stops being just what each one does separately and becomes how their excitations move and accumulate within the group.

Qiwen Tan and his collaborators, from the Institute of Science Tokyo in Japan and the KU Leuven University in Belgium, together with researchers from other institutions, have studied CsPbBr₃ perovskite quantum dot superlattices. At room temperature they observed an emission that alternates between intensity levels and a tendency for photons to arrive grouped over time, whose correlation intensity reaches a value of up to 3.9 in the measurements described.

Image and emission time observations suggest that the light comes mainly from an area much smaller than the entire superlattice. The authors propose that excitations created at different points in the structure migrate toward a low-energy region, where they can accumulate and give rise to a cascade emission that contributes to photon bunching. It's an interpretation supported by several measurements, although the observation of bunched photons alone does not prove that those photons are entangled.

The study is attractive because it examines collective phenomena at room temperature in a material prepared from nanocrystals, without having to assume that the entire superlattice behaves as an identical individual emitter. It remains to be understood how to best control the position of the emitting region and the variation between samples, important questions if we want to move from fundamental observation to optical sources designed for a specific task.

It also caught my attention

Polaritons in a perovskite at room temperature. Rui Cai and collaborators study how excitations that combine light and matter propagate in layered perovskite crystals. According to the publisher's abstract, published March 26 in Nature Photonics, the results include coherent propagation and slow light control. The page provides a preview of the subscription content.

An electro-optical comb to transmit over many channels. A team with participation from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, in China, presents a frequency comb generated by integrated lithium niobate modulators. The ACS Photonics summary, published on March 26, describes an aggregate transmission of 16.85 terabits per second with two combs and 86 channels. The publisher presents the text as paid content.
