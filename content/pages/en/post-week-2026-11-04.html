Week of November 4, 2026

Moiré light, memory on two timescales and emission from a defect

If you've turned one mesh over another and seen broad fringes appear, you're already partway into the first paper. Here it's patterns of polarisation that rotate. The other papers use two kinds of memory to recognise a gesture and a microscope to identify which atomic defects make a monolayer glow.

Photonics

Two light patterns that form a moiré in free space

[1] V. Dev and Y. Shen, “Freespace “twistronics”: moiré structured light for optical supertopologies,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 396, Nov. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02533-w.

Overlap two fine meshes and rotate just one of them. The stripes that appear are much wider than the holes and change with minimal movement. Vasu Dev and Yijie Shen, from Nanyang Technological University, in Singapore, do something similar without any material mesh. They prepare two light fields with periodic polarisation patterns and adjust the angle between them.

Changing the angle produces new symmetries and groupings of polarization structures. Some of the resulting patterns preserve their topology as they propagate, even with the obstacles tested in the study. The work shows how to prepare fields that tolerate certain disturbances and leaves open how well they would survive turbulence in a real link.

There's a good reason the experiment doesn't end with a pretty photo. The structure that is interesting is in how the polarisation rotates when moving from one point to the next. Two images can match in brightness and hide different twists. Therefore, to follow the pattern as it moves and find out what it retained when it encountered an obstacle, it is also necessary to measure the polarisation in different planes.

Related reading. Localization and delocalization of light in photonic moiré lattices. Nature (2019).

Electronics

A transistor to remember for a moment and for longer

[2] Q. Dai et al., “Orthogonally regulated multi-timescale reservoir and linear nonvolatile readout in a vertical transistor for dynamic machine vision,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 398, Nov. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02420-4.

A hand can end up in the same place after two different gestures. To distinguish them we need to remember where it happened a moment ago, while what we learned about how to recognize a gesture should still be there tomorrow. Asking a transistor to bring together both memories implies working with two very different time scales.

A team from Shaoxing University, Nanjing University and Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, proposes a vertical organic transistor that works in two modes. Under illumination, the transient dynamics of the carriers depend on the wavelength and provide different time scales. Instead, a non-volatile conductance that can represent reading weights is adjusted through the ferroelectric gate.

By recognizing human actions, the researchers record the device's response and distribute its states into four time windows. Variable reduction and classification come later, in software. The 91.95% correct rate tells how these stages worked together. From the point of view of the transistor, the most interesting contribution is that its fleeting response already transforms the sequence and its gate allows a lasting setting to be saved for reading it.

Related reading. Hyperpolarizing photoreceptor inspired biomimetic energy-saving sensor for dynamic machine vision. Nano Research (2025).

Materials

The luminous trace of an atomic defect

[3] L. Huberich et al., “Atomically resolved exciton emission from single defects in MoS2,” Nature Nanotechnology, Nov. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02314-x.

A photon of a certain energy can warn us that there is a defect in a semiconductor, although it will hardly give us the address and names of its atoms on its own. In a MoS₂ monolayer where several defects coexist, one way to narrow the gap is to simultaneously record where the current was injected and what light came out from there.

Researchers from Empa, ETH Zurich, both in Switzerland, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in China, among other institutions, have studied molybdenum disulfide monolayers with a scanning tunneling microscope capable of recording the light produced by the tunneling current. By combining spatial and spectral information, they assign a defect-associated emission band to carbon-hydrogen complexes at sulfur positions. Other defects studied, however, attenuate excitonic emission without producing the same characteristic signal.

There is a detail of the microscope itself that deserves to accompany that identification. At cryogenic temperatures, the tip with which the sample is excited also creates an electric field that can displace the emission energy. Taking this into account allows us to relate the signal to the local chemistry and raises the following question, whether the same centre could be prepared reproducibly in a device.

Related reading. Tunable and high-purity room temperature single-photon emission from atomic defects in hexagonal boron nitride. Nature Communications (2017).
