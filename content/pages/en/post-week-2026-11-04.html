Week of November 4, 2026

The same platform for very different distances

Photonics — The same platform for very different distances

[1] Z. Zhou et al., “Silicon photonics provides a unified solution for all-distance optical communications,” Nat Commun, Nov. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-79026-2.

An optical connection within a data center and a link between cities have quite different problems. In the first case it is very important to reduce the cost and complexity of thousands of short connections, while in the second the signal must survive great distances without losing the information it carries. That is why it is interesting to ask to what extent they can share components.

A team from Peking University, together with SiFotonics and the Semiconductor Institute of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, has combined a tunable laser with silicon photonics transceivers to explore both scenarios. In the short-range trials, the researchers tested 256 wavelengths without optical amplification, with an equivalent aggregate capacity of 81.92 Tbit/s. In another setup, they demonstrated transmission over a distance of more than 4,400 km, this time on a long-distance link with amplification.

There is an important detail behind the capacity figure, since the channels were tested successively and not as a simultaneous transmission of 256 wavelengths. Therefore, the result shows the potential capacity of the set, but does not yet demonstrate a commercial link that brings together all these channels at the same time. Still, the possibility of using such a coherent platform for different ranges, together with fast wavelength switching, points to attractive flexibility for future networks.

Electronics — A transistor to remember for a moment and for longer

[2] Q. Dai et al., “Orthogonally regulated multi-timescale reservoir and linear nonvolatile readout in a vertical transistor for dynamic machine vision,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 398, Nov. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02420-4.

Recognizing a movement requires relating what is happening now with what just happened, while preserving what has been learned requires a more stable memory. These two needs, one fleeting and the other persistent, are not usually easy to bring together in the same device. In reservoir computing, the temporal response of a physical system can be exploited to transform an input sequence before classifying it.

A team from Shaoxing University, Nanjing University and Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, proposes a vertical organic transistor that works in two modes. Under illumination, the transient dynamics of the carriers depend on the wavelength and provide different time scales. Instead, a non-volatile conductance that can represent reading weights is adjusted through the ferroelectric gate.

To study human action recognition, the authors used measured transistor responses as reservoir states and then processed those signals with principal component analysis and a neural network run in software. With four temporal groups they report a 91.95% success rate in the data set used. The distinction matters, because the work demonstrates the physical functions of the device and a hybrid assessment of the task, not a stand-alone camera that performs all the recognition on the hardware itself.

Materials — The luminous trace of an atomic defect

[3] L. Huberich et al., “Atomically resolved exciton emission from single defects in MoS2,” Nat. Nanotechnol., Nov. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02314-x.

A defect in a monolayer is not necessarily an imperfection that needs to be removed, as it can change how charges move or how light is emitted. The problem is to relate an optical signal to the specific atomic structure that produces it, something especially difficult when several types of defect can coexist in the same sample.

Researchers from Empa, ETH Zurich, both in Switzerland, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in China, among other institutions, have studied molybdenum disulfide monolayers with a scanning tunneling microscope capable of recording the light produced by the tunneling current. By combining spatial and spectral information, they assign a defect-associated emission band to carbon-hydrogen complexes at sulfur positions. Other defects studied, however, attenuate excitonic emission without producing the same characteristic signal.

This identification helps connect local chemistry to optical response and provides a basis for investigating emitters in two-dimensional materials. However, the experiment was performed under laboratory conditions, at cryogenic temperatures and with a tip that also modifies the electrical environment of the sample, so work remains before turning that knowledge into reproducible devices. The authors themselves discuss how the tip field influences the emission energy and its interpretation.
