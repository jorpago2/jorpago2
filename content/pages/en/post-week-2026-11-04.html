Week of November 4, 2026

Moiré light, memory on two timescales and emission from a defect

Rotating two patterns of light relative to each other produces a moiré structure that propagates through air. We then change scale to see how a transistor retains both fleeting and lasting information and how an emission band can be traced to a particular atomic defect.

Photonics

Two light patterns that form a moiré in free space

[1] V. Dev and Y. Shen, “Freespace “twistronics”: moiré structured light for optical supertopologies,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 396, Nov. 6, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02533-w.

Looking through two nearly aligned meshes reveals fringes much wider than the openings, with a spacing that changes as one mesh rotates relative to the other. At Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, Vasu Dev and Yijie Shen take the idea into free space. Their lattices are structured light fields with periodic polarization patterns, and the angle between them can be adjusted.

Changing the angle produces new symmetries and groupings of polarization structures. Some of the resulting patterns preserve their topology as they propagate, even with the obstacles tested in the study. The work shows how to prepare fields that tolerate certain disturbances and leaves open how well they would survive turbulence in a real link.

Photographing intensity in two planes is not enough to establish that the pattern persists. The topology in question lies in the way polarization varies from point to point, so two similar brightness maps can conceal different distributions. Following the polarization as the field propagates is what makes the structure distinguishable.

Related reading. Localization and delocalization of light in photonic moiré lattices. Nature (2019).

Electronics

A transistor to remember for a moment and for longer

[2] Q. Dai et al., “Orthogonally regulated multi-timescale reservoir and linear nonvolatile readout in a vertical transistor for dynamic machine vision,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 398, Nov. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02420-4.

Telling apart two movements that end in the same position requires a record of the recent sequence, while learned parameters have to last much longer. These two needs, one fleeting and the other persistent, are not usually easy to bring together in the same device. In reservoir computing, the temporal response of a physical system can be exploited to transform an input sequence before classifying it.

A team from Shaoxing University, Nanjing University and Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, proposes a vertical organic transistor that works in two modes. Under illumination, the transient dynamics of the carriers depend on the wavelength and provide different time scales. Instead, a non-volatile conductance that can represent reading weights is adjusted through the ferroelectric gate.

To recognise human actions, the authors record the transistor responses and group the states into four time windows. Software then reduces the number of variables and classifies the sequences. The 91.95% accuracy measures the transistor’s temporal memory in combination with that software readout.

Related reading. Hyperpolarizing photoreceptor inspired biomimetic energy-saving sensor for dynamic machine vision. Nano Research (2025).

Materials

The luminous trace of an atomic defect

[3] L. Huberich et al., “Atomically resolved exciton emission from single defects in MoS2,” Nature Nanotechnology, Nov. 5, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41565-026-02314-x.

An emission band can betray the presence of defects in a semiconductor without revealing which atoms make up the centre that emitted the photon. The problem is to relate an optical signal to the specific atomic structure that produces it, something especially difficult when several types of defect can coexist in the same sample.

Researchers from Empa, ETH Zurich, both in Switzerland, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in China, among other institutions, have studied molybdenum disulfide monolayers with a scanning tunneling microscope capable of recording the light produced by the tunneling current. By combining spatial and spectral information, they assign a defect-associated emission band to carbon-hydrogen complexes at sulfur positions. Other defects studied, however, attenuate excitonic emission without producing the same characteristic signal.

This identification helps connect local chemistry to optical response and provides a basis for investigating emitters in two-dimensional materials. However, the experiment was performed under laboratory conditions, at cryogenic temperatures and with a tip that also modifies the electrical environment of the sample, so work remains before turning that knowledge into reproducible devices. The authors themselves discuss how the tip field influences the emission energy and its interpretation.

Related reading. Tunable and high-purity room temperature single-photon emission from atomic defects in hexagonal boron nitride. Nature Communications (2017).
