Week of February 11, 2026

Sensing heat with light and adjusting current with charge or a field

A waveguide can reveal the heat left behind when a few molecules absorb light. A device’s response can also be set from within, by distributing charge between two floating gates or by using the dependence of interface resistance on the field direction.

Photonics

A waveguide that detects the heat of a few molecules

[1] K. Zheng et al., “Suspended waveguide-enhanced near-infrared photothermal spectroscopy for ppb-level molecular gas sensing on a chalcogenide chip,” Light Sci Appl, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 116, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02196-7. Open access.

Detecting a gas with light usually consists of searching for the wavelengths that its molecules absorb. The idea works well when the beam has enough distance to traverse the sample, although it becomes more difficult to bring to a chip, where the path is short and only part of the light traveling through the waveguide reaches the gas that surrounds it.

Kaiyuan Zheng and his collaborators, among whom are researchers from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, have tested an alternative that pays attention to the heat released after absorption. To do this, they manufacture a chalcogenide glass waveguide suspended over an air cavity. An optical signal excites the gas and the heating produced modifies the refractive index of the waveguide, so that a second signal can register this change as a phase variation.

Suspension has two consequences that are useful at the same time. On the one hand, part of the optical field is more exposed to the gas and the interaction with its molecules increases. On the other hand, the structure dissipates less heat towards the substrate, allowing a small absorption to produce a temperature change that is easier to measure. The team compares the design with a waveguide supported on silicon dioxide and calculates a 45-fold improvement in photothermal modulation efficiency.

In their demonstration with acetylene, the authors achieve a detection limit of 330 parts per billion and a response time of less than one second. However, this result corresponds to the gas and the conditions of the experiment, so extending it to other compounds or real mixtures will require studying both the selectivity and the prolonged operation of the sensor. Even with that caution, the combination of an integrated waveguide and photothermal measurement offers an ingenious way to increase sensitivity without resorting to a long optical cell.

Electronics

A memory that also controls how its sensor responds to light

[2] Z.-C. Zhang et al., “A reconfigurable photosensitive split-floating-gate memory for neuromorphic computing and nonlinear activation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1697, Feb. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68402-7. Open access.

Participating institutions
Nankai University (China)

The memory’s two floating gates can retain different amounts of charge. Programming them separately lets the team tune how much current changes under illumination and how current depends on applied voltage.

That second control lets the same device implement nonlinear responses used in neural networks. The researchers build an array to test learning tasks, with several operations taking place in the structure that receives the signal.

Materials

Resistance changes as the field turns across two stacked layers

[3] Q. Chen et al., “Giant unusual anisotropic magnetoresistance enabled by hole-electron resonance in van der Waals heterostructures,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1736, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68438-9. Open access.

Participating institutions
Southeast University (China)

When the team stacks WTe₂ on a magnetic material, the resistance depends strongly on the field direction. The change reaches 289%, and its angular dependence departs from what simple scattering models predict.

The authors suggest that interactions between electrons and holes in WTe₂ alter spin transfer across the interface. The result puts that explanation to the test and shows why it helps to track both field and magnetisation directions.
