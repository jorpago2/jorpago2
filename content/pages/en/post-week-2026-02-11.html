Week of February 11, 2026

Light between gases, shadows and crystals

From February 11 to 17, 2026

This week we look at three ways to take advantage of the interaction between light and matter, from a suspended waveguide that detects small amounts of gas to a transistor sensitive to dim light and crystals whose emission changes when crushed with salts.

A waveguide that detects the heat of a few molecules

Detecting a gas with light usually consists of searching for the wavelengths that its molecules absorb. The idea works well when the beam has enough distance to traverse the sample, although it becomes more difficult to bring to a chip, where the path is short and only part of the light traveling through the waveguide reaches the gas that surrounds it.

Kaiyuan Zheng and his collaborators, among whom are researchers from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, have tested an alternative that pays attention to the heat released after absorption. To do this, they manufacture a chalcogenide glass waveguide suspended over an air cavity. An optical signal excites the gas and the heating produced modifies the refractive index of the waveguide, so that a second signal can register this change as a phase variation.

Suspension has two consequences that are useful at the same time. On the one hand, part of the optical field is more exposed to the gas and the interaction with its molecules increases. On the other hand, the structure dissipates less heat towards the substrate, allowing a small absorption to produce a temperature change that is easier to measure. The team compares the design with a waveguide supported on silicon dioxide and calculates a 45-fold improvement in photothermal modulation efficiency.

In their demonstration with acetylene, the authors achieve a detection limit of 330 parts per billion and a response time of less than one second. However, this result corresponds to the gas and the conditions of the experiment, so extending it to other compounds or real mixtures will require studying both the selectivity and the prolonged operation of the sensor. Even with that caution, the combination of an integrated waveguide and photothermal measurement offers an ingenious way to increase sensitivity without resorting to a long optical cell.

K. Zheng et al., “Suspended waveguide-enhanced near-infrared photothermal spectroscopy for ppb-level molecular gas sensing on a chalcogenide chip,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, art. 116, 2026. Full article. Published electronically on February 17, 2026.

A dark-adaptive transistor

When there is very little light, a sensor has to distinguish a weak signal from its own noise and also deliver an image that allows us to recognize what appears in it. Owls solve that problem with a visual system adapted to the night, an inspiration that Zishen Zhao and his collaborators transfer to an electronic device that combines optical sensitivity and an adjustable electrical response.

Researchers from the Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University in China, the University of Liverpool in the United Kingdom and the National University of Singapore in Singapore, among other institutions, participate in the work. Its transistor incorporates lead sulfide nanocrystals in a photosensitive layer and an organic semiconductor channel, while an insulating layer can trap charges. Thus, the illumination and pulses applied to the gate modify the current so that the device retains part of its state after the stimulus.

The authors fabricate a 19 by 17 element matrix and test the response to light patterns, as well as the modification of the conductance using electrical pulses. Under their measurement conditions, they observe signal with intensities as low as 0.146 nW/cm². In addition, they explore how to use different conductance states as weights for neural networks and how to improve the contrast of images taken in low light.

It is important to distinguish the manufacturing of the matrix from the computational tests. Evaluations of multi-network classification and contrast enhancement for target recognition include simulations based on device characteristics, so they are not yet equivalent to a complete system installed on a camera. Still, the study shows why the layers that generate, transport and retain charge can be designed together to move some processing closer to sensing.

Z. Zhao et al., “Owl-vision-inspired near sensor computing,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 2676, 2026. Full article. Published electronically on February 12, 2026.

Crush a crystal to change the light it emits

In the synthesis of a luminescent material we usually think that its properties are decided when the crystal finishes growing. However, subsequent treatment can also change its response, as shown in a study by Junhong Wu and collaborators from Tianjin University, in China, which was based on an observation during the preparation of samples for optical characterization.

The team works with chiral indium halides, that is, materials that can present two related structures such as a hand and its image in a mirror. This chirality is reflected in the light they emit, which can favor one circular polarization over the other. By incorporating antimony they obtain materials with orange emission and, by grinding them with certain bromide salts, they find additional changes in both color and response to polarization.

The composition of the salt matters. Grinding some samples with formamidinium bromide, for example, shifts the circularly polarized emission into the near-infrared. With other salts the team observes a change in the sign of the signal and, in one of the cases studied, a greater generation of second harmonics. They also prepared a light-emitting diode demonstration to explore how this response could be exploited in a device.

Grinding is attractive because it allows the material to be modified after it is synthesized, although the study does not show that any salt or any crystal leads to the same effect. In fact, the authors observed different responses with chlorides and a quenching of fluorescence with the iodides tested. The interest is precisely in understanding which combinations work and why, before converting a laboratory observation into a reproducible manufacturing technique.

J. Wu et al., “Mechanochemical engineering of chiroptical properties in indium-based chiral metal halides by grinding,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, art. 2619, 2026. Full article. Published electronically on February 11, 2026.

It also caught my attention

More efficient light from a perovskite. Jingyu Peng and collaborators present an ordered heterojunction of three-dimensional and two-dimensional perovskites to fabricate green emitting diodes. In the publisher's abstract, the team reports a maximum external quantum efficiency of 42.9%, with a certified value of 42.3%. The article was published on February 11 in Nature and the editorial page does not identify it as open access.

A synapse that responds differently depending on color. Jiaxin Wu and collaborators study a graphene and chromium oxychloride optoelectronic transistor whose temporal response changes between visible and infrared illumination. The editorial summary describes its application to motion extraction and a speed estimation task. The work appeared on February 17 in Advanced Optical Materials and open access to the full text has not been confirmed.
