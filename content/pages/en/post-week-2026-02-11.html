Week of February 11, 2026

Sensing heat with light and adjusting current with charge or a field

An acetylene molecule absorbs light and gives off heat. Could you find it by measuring the tiny rise in temperature next to a waveguide? We'll also look at a transistor whose response depends on how it was programmed, and a magnet whose resistance changes as you rotate the field.

Photonics

Detecting acetylene by the heat it leaves beside a waveguide

[1] K. Zheng et al., “Suspended waveguide-enhanced near-infrared photothermal spectroscopy for ppb-level molecular gas sensing on a chalcogenide chip,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 116, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02196-7.

If there is very little acetylene, the dip in transmitted light may be barely visible. Kaiyuan Zheng and colleagues at Hong Kong Polytechnic University in China are trying to find the absorbed energy elsewhere. The molecule ends up giving it off as heat, and near a suspended waveguide that heat changes the phase of a second optical signal.

The waveguide is made of chalcogenide glass and has air underneath. Thus there is more optical field in contact with the gas and the heat takes longer to escape to the substrate. In their calculations, suspending it multiplies the photothermal modulation efficiency by about 45 compared to supporting it on silica.

With acetylene they measure a detection limit of 330 parts per billion and a response of less than one second. These are two different questions that one would ask when mounting the sensor. How much gas is needed to see it and how long will we have to wait to know that it has arrived.

Related reading. Waveguide‐Based On‐Chip Photothermal Spectroscopy for Gas Sensing. Laser & Photonics Reviews (2024).

Electronics

A memory that also controls how its sensor responds to light

[2] Z.-C. Zhang et al., “A reconfigurable photosensitive split-floating-gate memory for neuromorphic computing and nonlinear activation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1697, Feb. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68402-7.

Participating institutions
Nankai University (China)

When you raise the sensitivity of a sensor, you expect a larger signal for the same flash. Now imagine that you could also choose the shape of the response, so that doubling the input sometimes doubled the output and sometimes barely moved it. For a neural network, this second possibility saves having to manufacture part of the operation in another circuit.

Zhang and colleagues divide the floating gate of the transistor into two regions that can be charged separately. With the same structure they thus adjust the sensitivity to light and the dependence of the current on the voltage. Once regions are programmed, the sensor retains the setting for subsequent readings.

They test it by setting up a matrix and trying out learning tasks. If you wanted to repeat the comparison in the laboratory, it would be enough to start with something simple. Hold the light steady, scan the voltage and see if only the size of the current or also the pattern of the curve has changed.

Related reading. Ferroelectric-defined reconfigurable homojunctions for in-memory sensing and computing. Nature Materials (2023).

Materials

Resistance changes as the field turns across two stacked layers

[3] Q. Chen et al., “Giant unusual anisotropic magnetoresistance enabled by hole-electron resonance in van der Waals heterostructures,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1736, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68438-9.

Participating institutions
Southeast University (China)

There is a nice experiment to see if an interface has a preferred direction. Keep the field strength fixed, rotate the field relative to the sample and record the resistance. In the junction between WTe₂ and Fe₃GaTe₂, the change reaches 289%. What is more difficult to explain is the shape of the curve, which deviates from the usual angular dependence.

The authors suggest that interactions between electrons and holes in WTe₂ alter spin transfer across the interface. The result puts that explanation to the test and shows why it helps to track both field and magnetisation directions.

When circling that curve you have to distinguish two angles that appear to be the same in the assembly drawing. One is the field that you control from outside. The other is that of magnetization, which also senses the preferred directions of the crystal. Separating them allows us to ask what remains to be explained by the exchange of angular momentum across the interface.

Related reading. Unusual Van der Waals Magnetoresistance in Stacked Ferromagnetic Fe 3 GeTe 2 : The Role of Atomically Sharp Interfaces. Advanced Science (2025).
