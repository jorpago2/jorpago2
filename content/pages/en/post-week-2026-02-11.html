Week of February 11, 2026

Sensing heat with light and adjusting current with charge or a field

Acetylene absorbs light even when there is too little gas for the attenuation of the beam to stand out. A suspended waveguide lets the researchers look for the heat left by that absorption. The other two devices have electrical readouts whose interpretation depends on where charge is stored in a sensor and which way the field points at a magnetic interface.

Photonics

Detecting acetylene by the heat it leaves beside a waveguide

[1] K. Zheng et al., “Suspended waveguide-enhanced near-infrared photothermal spectroscopy for ppb-level molecular gas sensing on a chalcogenide chip,” Light: Science & Applications, vol. 15, no. 1, Art. no. 116, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41377-026-02196-7.

One way to identify a gas is to tune a laser to an absorption line and measure how much light goes missing along the path. The idea works well when the beam has enough distance to traverse the sample, although it becomes more difficult to bring to a chip, where the path is short and only part of the light traveling through the waveguide reaches the gas that surrounds it.

Kaiyuan Zheng and his collaborators, among whom are researchers from the Hong Kong Polytechnic University, in China, have tested an alternative that pays attention to the heat released after absorption. To do this, they manufacture a chalcogenide glass waveguide suspended over an air cavity. An optical signal excites the gas and the heating produced modifies the refractive index of the waveguide, so that a second signal can register this change as a phase variation.

Suspension has two consequences that are useful at the same time. On the one hand, part of the optical field is more exposed to the gas and the interaction with its molecules increases. On the other hand, the structure dissipates less heat towards the substrate, allowing a small absorption to produce a temperature change that is easier to measure. The team compares the design with a waveguide supported on silicon dioxide and calculates a 45-fold improvement in photothermal modulation efficiency.

For acetylene the detection limit is 330 parts per billion and the response takes less than a second. What the sensor measures here is a gas concentration. Detecting another compound would mean choosing a wavelength it absorbs and checking how much the other molecules in the mixture contribute to the signal.

Electronics

A memory that also controls how its sensor responds to light

[2] Z.-C. Zhang et al., “A reconfigurable photosensitive split-floating-gate memory for neuromorphic computing and nonlinear activation,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1697, Feb. 16, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68402-7.

Participating institutions
Nankai University (China)

Zhang and colleagues split the floating gate into two regions that can store charge independently. Programming them separately lets the team tune how much current changes under illumination and how current depends on applied voltage.

That second control lets the same device implement nonlinear responses used in neural networks. The researchers build an array to test learning tasks, with several operations taking place in the structure that receives the signal.

Materials

Resistance changes as the field turns across two stacked layers

[3] Q. Chen et al., “Giant unusual anisotropic magnetoresistance enabled by hole-electron resonance in van der Waals heterostructures,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1736, Feb. 17, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-68438-9.

Participating institutions
Southeast University (China)

Rotating the magnetic field relative to a WTe₂/Fe₃GaTe₂ junction changes its resistance even though the composition of the sample stays fixed. The change reaches 289%, and its angular dependence departs from what simple scattering models predict.

The authors suggest that interactions between electrons and holes in WTe₂ alter spin transfer across the interface. The result puts that explanation to the test and shows why it helps to track both field and magnetisation directions.
