Week of January 7, 2026

Light on a chip, ions in a pore and spins in a crystal

Would you heat a chip to stop it losing light? The answer depends on the colour you plan to use. We'll also meet a pore with identical openings that passes ions more readily one way, and a crystal whose spins give away which nuclei are nearby. In each experiment it pays to ask what changed between readings.

Photonics

Optical waveguide loss depends on the colour of the light

[1] H.-J. Chen et al., “Towards fibre-like loss for photonic integration from violet to near-infrared,” Nature, vol. 649, no. 8096, pp. 338–344, Jan. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-025-09889-w.

Silica has been transporting light through fibre for decades. The temptation to draw a circuit on silicon with it is quite understandable, although on a chip there are etched walls and roughness scatters more light at visible wavelengths. Chen and colleagues use ultraviolet lithography and add germanium to the core to make its index higher than that of the surrounding silica. With this glass they make resonators whose quality factor exceeds 180 million at various points between violet and infrared.

There is a comparison I would make before deciding how to make my circuit. At 532 nm, dispensing with annealing increases the losses from 0.32 to 1.02 dB per meter. At 1550 nm, however, they go from 0.09 to 0.15 dB per meter. If I already have a component on the chip that handles heat poorly, the optical price of saving that step depends a lot on the colour.

The team also generates Brillouin emission and frequency combs. The California Institute of Technology and University of California, Santa Barbara, in the United States, the Leiden University, in the Netherlands, and the University of Southampton, in the United Kingdom, participate.

Related reading. Low‐Temperature Sputtered Ultralow‐Loss Silicon Nitride for Hybrid Photonic Integration. Laser & Photonics Reviews (2023).

Electronics

A symmetric pore that lets more ions through one way

[2] C.-H. Chiang et al., “Single symmetric nanopores in ultrathin crystalline ferroelectric BiFeO3 as polarization-switchable ionic diodes,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1542, Jan. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68262-7.

Participating institutions
National Taiwan University (Taiwan)

Imagine that you turn a funnel upside down. It is easy to tell which way you have placed it because one mouth is wider than the other. With this pore it is much more difficult to understand the preference. Its two openings are similar, and yet, when the voltage is reversed, the magnitude of the ion current that passes through it changes.

The clue is in the bismuth ferrite membrane, which is only 30 nm thick. It is ferroelectric, so that ions that approach its two faces find different electrical environments even though the geometry of the passage is symmetrical. By varying the pH, researchers are able to reverse the polarisation and with it the favored direction of passage.

If you wanted to use that pore as a switch, there would be something to note in addition to the voltage. You would also need to know how the membrane was treated before, because that treatment leaves a preference that can be read later in the current.

Related reading. Ultrathin Ionic Diodes with Electrostatically Heterogeneous Hybrid Interfaces of Nanoporous SiO 2 Nanofilms and Polymer Layer‐by‐Layer Multilayers. Small (2024).

Materials

Chromium and iron spins inside a perovskite crystal

[3] S. Khamkaeo et al., “Spin Qubits Candidate in Transition-Metal-Ion doped Halide Double Perovskites,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 415, Jan. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-67980-2.

Participating institutions
Linköping University (Sweden)

How long does a freshly prepared spin keep time? In this crystal you can try two different guests, chromium and iron. The team incorporates them separately into a perovskite and cools it to 4 K. By following the phase of the spin ensemble, it obtains coherence times of about 30 µs for chromium and 21 µs for iron.

The spins are not alone in the crystal either. The chlorine and cesium nuclei around them leave their signature on the electronic resonance, so the measurement tells something about that neighbourhood without having to directly observe each nucleus. Chromium also has an advantage if you want to take the experiment to optics, since certain orientations of its spin are distinguished with light.

To talk about an individual qubit, there would be a fairly concrete test. One of these spins would have to be chosen, prepared and read separately, while in this work the signal comes from many at the same time.

Related reading. Spin Centers in Vanadium-Doped Cs2NaInCl6 Halide Double Perovskites. ACS Materials Letters (2024).
