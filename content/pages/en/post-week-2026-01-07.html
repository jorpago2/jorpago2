Week of January 7, 2026

Light on a chip, ions in a pore and spins in a crystal

The search for stable light on a chip has an echo in this week’s other papers. In one, a membrane determines which way ions flow. In the other, a crystal preserves the phase of a spin for tens of microseconds.

Photonics

Optical waveguide loss depends on the colour of the light

[1] H.-J. Chen et al., “Towards fibre-like loss for photonic integration from violet to near-infrared,” Nature, vol. 649, no. 8096, pp. 338–344, Jan. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-025-09889-w.

The California Institute of Technology and the University of California, Santa Barbara, in the United States, the Leiden University, in the Netherlands, and the University of Southampton, in the United Kingdom, participate in the work.

The light that travels through an integrated waveguide does not disappear suddenly, but is lost little by little through absorption and scattering. This loss becomes especially troublesome when the circuit needs long paths or resonators capable of conserving light for a long time, and is accentuated when working in the visible, where small manufacturing irregularities have a greater effect. Chen and his collaborators wonder if a material familiar in optical fibers could also help inside a chip.

The proposal uses silica doped with germanium, whose refractive index allows light to be confined in a waveguide surrounded by silica, manufactured through an ultraviolet lithography process on silicon wafers. The team built resonators and measured their losses from violet to telecommunications infrared, obtaining quality factors greater than 180 million at different points in that range. For example, in heat-treated waveguides it measured 0.32 dB per meter at 532 nanometers and 0.08 dB per meter at 1064 nanometers.

However, not all applications tolerate heating the chip once its components are integrated. Therefore, it is relevant that the authors also obtained low losses without this treatment, although higher than in the annealed samples. In addition to characterizing the waveguides, they demonstrated functions such as frequency comb generation and Brillouin emission, with which they illustrate some possibilities of the platform.

Leaving out the anneal costs more at some wavelengths than at others. At 532 nm the loss rises from 0.32 to 1.02 dB per metre, whereas at 1550 nm it rises only from 0.09 to 0.15. For a designer adding a heat-sensitive component, the operating wavelength makes a difference to that trade-off.

Electronics

A symmetric pore that lets more ions through one way

[2] C.-H. Chiang et al., “Single symmetric nanopores in ultrathin crystalline ferroelectric BiFeO3 as polarization-switchable ionic diodes,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1542, Jan. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68262-7.

Participating institutions
National Taiwan University (Taiwan)

A pore can act as a diode if it offers less resistance to ion flow in one direction. Here the opening itself is symmetric. The difference comes from the polarisation of the bismuth ferrite membrane, as thin as 30 nm in the thinnest samples.

Changing the pH lets the researchers reverse the polarisation and switch the direction in which ions flow more easily. The current depends on the membrane’s treatment history, a point that matters if the pore is to work as a switch in an ionic circuit.

Materials

Chromium and iron spins inside a perovskite crystal

[3] S. Khamkaeo et al., “Spin Qubits Candidate in Transition-Metal-Ion doped Halide Double Perovskites,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 415, Jan. 12, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-67980-2.

Participating institutions
Linköping University (Sweden)

By introducing a small number of chromium or iron ions into a halide perovskite, the team obtains localised electron spins that it can manipulate with pulses. At 4 K the spins remain coherent for about 30 µs in chromium and 21 µs in iron.

In chromium, light can also distinguish between certain spin orientations. The study measures groups of ions, so using them as qubits would require a way to prepare and read individual spins.
