Light that remains, synapses that see and disordered tellurium

Photonics

Bringing fibre-like losses to a chip

[1] H.-J. Chen et al., “Towards fibre-like loss for photonic integration from violet to near-infrared,” Nature, vol. 649, no. 8096, pp. 338–344, Jan. 7, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41586-025-09889-w. date in Crossref

The California Institute of Technology and the University of California, Santa Barbara, in the United States, the Leiden University, in the Netherlands, and the University of Southampton, in the United Kingdom, participate in the work.

The light that travels through an integrated waveguide does not disappear suddenly, but is lost little by little through absorption and scattering. This loss becomes especially troublesome when the circuit needs long paths or resonators capable of conserving light for a long time, and is accentuated when working in the visible, where small manufacturing irregularities have a greater effect. Chen and his collaborators wonder if a material familiar in optical fibers could also help inside a chip.

The proposal uses silica doped with germanium, whose refractive index allows light to be confined in a waveguide surrounded by silica, manufactured through an ultraviolet lithography process on silicon wafers. The team built resonators and measured their losses from violet to telecommunications infrared, obtaining quality factors greater than 180 million at different points in that range. For example, in heat-treated waveguides it measured 0.32 dB per meter at 532 nanometers and 0.08 dB per meter at 1064 nanometers.

However, not all applications tolerate heating the chip once its components are integrated. Therefore, it is relevant that the authors also obtained low losses without this treatment, although higher than in the annealed samples. In addition to characterizing the waveguides, they demonstrated functions such as frequency comb generation and Brillouin emission, with which they illustrate some possibilities of the platform.

The result opens an interesting avenue for circuits that must work in the visible, from optical clocks to quantum sensors, although between demonstrating components and building a complete system there are still issues of integration with sources, modulators and detectors.

Electronics

A synapse that distinguishes the orientation of light

[2] J. Huo et al., “Coupled ferroelectric-anisotropic optoelectronic synapse for polarization-sensitive neuromorphic vision,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1468, Jan. 8, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68206-1. date in Crossref

The work brings together researchers from the National University of Singapore, in Singapore, and the Tsinghua University, in China.

A camera records light and then sends the data elsewhere for processing, but there are alternatives that attempt to do some of that work on the sensor itself. Huo and his collaborators focus on a property that is often left out of a conventional image, polarization, and build a device that can respond differently depending on the orientation of the light it receives.

To do this, they combine a rhenium disulfide channel, ReS₂, whose optical response depends on the direction within the crystal, with a hafnium and zirconium oxide ferroelectric gate. The structure also incorporates a floating gate, which helps control the interaction between the layers. Thus, both the illumination and the electrical signal allow the device's conductance to be modified and preserve states that can be used as weights of an artificial synapse.

The authors estimate an energy of about 2 femtojoules per synaptic operation for the specific conditions of optical programming and electrical erasure that they studied. Additionally, they used a three-by-three array of devices to extract polarization-sensitive image features and combined their results with a neural network. It is convenient to separate this demonstration from a complete neuromorphic camera, because the recognition system and its energy needs are not reduced to the figure of the individual device.

The interest lies in the fact that the same structure brings together detection, state and a first processing operation, while the anisotropy of the material adds information that an intensity measurement alone does not provide. The next question is how to translate this behavior, observed in a small matrix, to larger and more uniform sets.

Materials

Disordered tellurium seen from inside the device

[3] N. Hur et al., “On-device cryogenic quenching enables robust amorphous tellurium for threshold switching,” Nat Commun, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 1509, Jan. 13, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-025-68223-0. date in Crossref

Participants include Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology and Kyungpook National University, in South Korea.

In some materials the current increases sharply when exceeding a threshold voltage, a behavior useful for selecting cells in memory arrays. Interpreting what happens just before that jump is not always easy, because the current also heats the material and can alter its structure. Hur and his collaborators study this relationship in tellurium devices, a chalcogenide that can adopt ordered and disordered configurations.

The team uses electrical heating and controls cooling to produce different states of the material within the device itself. By also working in a cryogenic environment, the researchers manage to preserve during characterization an amorphous phase that is otherwise difficult to study, and thus compare its response with that of crystalline tellurium. In experiments at room temperature, the authors further show that the rate of pulse decay influences the final configuration, and that a rapid decay can favor the disordered state.

The comparison reveals threshold switching in both amorphous and crystalline tellurium. From the measurements and their calculations, the authors propose that transitions associated with defects contribute to the current jump before subsequent heating can melt the material. Therefore, the study helps to distinguish two phenomena that can occur in the same device and that, observed from the outside, lend themselves to confusion.

The cryogenic technique here is a tool to investigate the mechanism, so it should not be interpreted as a demonstration that a commercial memory needs to operate with cryogenic cooling. Validation in arrays as well as stability and consumption under operating conditions comparable to those of a practical circuit remain open.
