Week of September 9, 2026

Carrying high-frequency signals, filtering spikes and forming vortices

Two optical tones produce a radio carrier when they reach a sufficiently fast photodiode. The other papers examine the estimated savings from filtering an image before it reaches a spiking network and use X-ray diffraction to uncover an arrangement of vortices on silicon.

Photonics

Two materials to send information above one hundred gigahertz

[1] L. Wang et al., “Heterogeneously integrated photonic terahertz transmitter on thin-film lithium niobate,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9797, Sep. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76733-8.

Transmitting data by radio at 200 GHz requires a source that generates the carrier and can encode information on it without losing the necessary bandwidth. Photonics offers a way to produce these signals by mixing two optical tones, although integrating a good modulator and a sufficiently fast photodetector into the same platform remains complicated.

Researchers from ShanghaiTech University, Zhejiang University and City University of Hong Kong in China have coupled a lithium niobate thin film modulator with an indium phosphide-based photodiode. The combination attempts to take advantage of the electro-optic response of the former and the ability of the latter to transform fast optical signals into a high-frequency current.

The team reports a bandwidth of 240 GHz for the integrated photodiode and tests wireless transmission around 150 and 200 GHz with different modulation formats. These are tests of a transmitter and its components in an experimental setup, not of a complete network ready to be deployed.

Electronics

Filtering before activating a spiking network

[2] L. Li et al., “A spiking neural network-in-logic architecture based on reconfigurable molybdenum disulfide dual-gate transistors with ferroelectric gating,” Nature Electronics, vol. 9, no. 10, pp. 1110–1121, Sep. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41928-026-01706-0.

If the object to be recognised fills only part of an image, processing the entire background can activate neurons that contribute little to the decision. In spiking systems, that unnecessary activity also means activating more elements, so deciding what information passes to the network can be as important as the operation of the network itself.

Lingqi Li and his collaborators, with researchers from National University of Singapore and A*STAR, in Singapore, study double-gate transistors with a MoS₂ channel and a hafnium zirconium oxide ferroelectric layer. Depending on the signals applied, the devices can display logical functions and behaviors that the authors use as blocks of neurons or artificial synapses.

The proposed architecture uses logical operations to select part of the input before sending it to a spiking network. In a digit classification simulation, with parameters adjusted to the device measurements, the authors obtain a 95.9% success rate on MNIST after 50 epochs. They also explore more complex tasks, for which increasing network size and simulation time improves accuracy, but increases estimated consumption.

Each activation avoided saves energy in the model, which uses transistor measurements to estimate the cost of the operations. Moving from that estimate to chip power would mean including the peripheral circuits that receive images, address the cells and collect the output.

Materials

Ferroelectric vortices on a silicon base

[3] V. V. Hevelke et al., “Periodic polar vortices in BaTiO3/SrTiO3 superlattices monolithically grown on silicon,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9648, Sep. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77270-0.

Even when every region of a ferroelectric film is polarized, the direction of polarization can rotate from point to point. Under certain conditions it can be organized into curved patterns, including nanometer-sized vortices that arouse interest due to their possible electrical properties and the possibility of integrating them into devices.

Valentin Hevelke and his collaborators have studied superlattices formed by alternating layers of barium titanate and strontium titanate, epitaxially grown on silicon using an intermediate layer of SrTiO₃. Researchers from the Pennsylvania State University in the United States, among other institutions, participate in the work. Using X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy they identify a periodic organization compatible with polarization vortex tubes within the layers.

The layers studied have approximate thicknesses of 4.8 nm for BaTiO₃ and 2.0 nm for SrTiO₃, repeated ten times. The order of these patterns disappears around 450 K, while phase field modeling indicates that mechanical stress and interlayer coupling strongly influence the shape the polarization takes.

The vortex tubes form stripes that turn through 90 degrees in the plane. The team compares temperature-dependent X-ray diffraction with a phase-field model, where it can vary the strain in the layers and examine why this arrangement is stable on silicon.
