Week of September 9, 2026

Carrying high-frequency signals, filtering spikes and forming vortices

Two optical tones generate a radio carrier, a network reading handwritten digits decides which parts of the image deserve attention, and a film on silicon arranges its polarisation into tiny swirls. Look closely at how the parts fit together and all three results become easier to understand.

Photonics

Two materials to send information above one hundred gigahertz

[1] L. Wang et al., “Heterogeneously integrated photonic terahertz transmitter on thin-film lithium niobate,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9797, Sep. 15, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-76733-8.

If we were to beat two optical tones in a detector fast enough, we would obtain a current that oscillates at the difference between their frequencies. It's an elegant way to prepare a 200 GHz radio carrier, as long as someone also prints the light data and the detector keeps pace when that signal arrives.

Researchers from ShanghaiTech University, Zhejiang University and City University of Hong Kong in China have coupled a lithium niobate thin film modulator with an indium phosphide-based photodiode. The combination attempts to take advantage of the electro-optic response of the former and the ability of the latter to transform fast optical signals into a high-frequency current.

The integrated photodiode reaches a bandwidth of 240 GHz. The team then takes the transmitter to a wireless link and tests different modulation formats around 150 and 200 GHz. They are two different steps that make sense to count in that order. The first shows how fast a component supports and the second checks what happens when that component must collaborate with the modulator to carry data through the air.

Related reading. Ultra-wideband Waveguide-coupled Photodiodes Heterogeneously Integrated on a Thin-film Lithium Niobate Platform. Light: Advanced Manufacturing (2023).

Electronics

Filtering before activating a spiking network

[2] L. Li et al., “A spiking neural network-in-logic architecture based on reconfigurable molybdenum disulfide dual-gate transistors with ferroelectric gating,” Nature Electronics, vol. 9, no. 10, pp. 1110–1121, Sep. 14, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41928-026-01706-0.

Look at the blank space surrounding a handwritten digit. If the circuit devotes as many pulses to the background as to the stroke, a portion of its activity will barely help distinguish the number. Lingqi Li and colleagues explore what could be saved by making that decision near the entrance, before putting the rest of the network to work.

Lingqi Li and his collaborators, with researchers from National University of Singapore and A*STAR, in Singapore, study double-gate transistors with a MoS₂ channel and a hafnium zirconium oxide ferroelectric layer. Depending on the signals applied, the devices can display logical functions and behaviors that the authors use as blocks of neurons or artificial synapses.

With parameters obtained from the transistors, the simulation reaches a 95.9% success rate in MNIST after 50 epochs. For more complex tasks, expanding the network and giving it more time improves accuracy and increases estimated consumption. When taking the energy account to a physical chip, it will also be necessary to add what it costs to receive the image, select the cells and read the response. These expenses occur even when we have managed to skip a good part of the fund.

Related reading. A MoS2 Hafnium Oxide Based Ferroelectric Encoder for Temporal‐Efficient Spiking Neural Network. Advanced Materials (2022).

Materials

Ferroelectric vortices on a silicon base

[3] V. V. Hevelke et al., “Periodic polar vortices in BaTiO3/SrTiO3 superlattices monolithically grown on silicon,” Nature Communications, vol. 17, no. 1, Art. no. 9648, Sep. 9, 2026, doi 10.1038/s41467-026-77270-0.

If we were to draw an arrow to represent polarisation at each point in a film, we might find that the arrows make one complete turn as they pass through a very small region. The curious thing about the work of Valentin Hevelke and colleagues is how these swirls are organized into tubes and form stripes that change direction 90 degrees on the silicon.

Valentin Hevelke and his collaborators have studied superlattices formed by alternating layers of barium titanate and strontium titanate, epitaxially grown on silicon using an intermediate layer of SrTiO₃. Researchers from the Pennsylvania State University in the United States, among other institutions, participate in the work. Using X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy they identify a periodic organization compatible with polarization vortex tubes within the layers.

The stacking repeats layers of BaTiO₃ of about 4.8 nm and SrTiO₃ of about 2.0 nm ten times. When heated, the order of the patterns is lost around 450 K. To interpret why the tubes were stable before heating, the authors resort to a phase field model in which the deformation and coupling between layers can vary. They are two difficult controls to separate when looking only at the finished film.

Related reading. Switchable topological polar states in epitaxial BaTiO3 nanoislands on silicon. Nature Communications (2024).
