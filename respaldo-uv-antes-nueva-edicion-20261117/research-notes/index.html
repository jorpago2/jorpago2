Three readings on photonics, materials and neuromorphic computing

Optical memory in lithium niobate

Gao and colleagues · Nature Photonics · 6 November 2026

Thin-film lithium niobate has become an established platform for photonic circuits. Gao and colleagues now propose using it as an optical memory too: they modify the material by implanting oxygen ions and obtain a device whose state can be written and read with light.

According to the authors, the device retains several states after the stimulus is removed and responds in less than a microsecond. It is a step towards circuits that can store information on the same platform that processes light.

A question is how these devices will behave when many need to be fabricated and controlled together. The paper reports a device-level demonstration; a complete photonic network remains further away.

Antimony selenide for programmable photonic circuits

Shen and colleagues · NPG Asia Materials · 14 April 2026

Some materials change their optical properties when they switch between amorphous and crystalline states. If that change can be controlled, it can be used to adjust a photonic circuit. The difficulty is that some phase-change materials absorb too much light.

Shen and colleagues study an alternative, Sb₂Se₃, and design a component that uses the difference in refractive index between its two phases. In their simulations, the device supports 32 programmable states with low optical loss.

This is a design and simulation study. Fabrication and reproducible programming will be needed to establish how well the predicted advantages hold up in practice.

A sensor with two timescales

Li and colleagues · npj Nanophotonics · 9 June 2026

A photodetector is usually designed to respond quickly and recover as soon as possible. But a response that persists can also be useful: it leaves a trace of the light received and, with it, some information about what just happened.

Li and colleagues combine both behaviours in one device. Made from GeSe and CsPbBr₃, it acts as a fast detector or maintains a longer response, depending on the applied voltage. The authors use the latter behaviour in neuromorphic vision experiments.

In one experiment, the sensor signal records information about an object’s path. A neural network then analyses that information to predict its trajectory. The sensor does not make the prediction on its own, although its response provides useful temporal information.
